# Some Personal Thoughts on Recreational Mathematics

1. Long before it seemed likely that AI would surpass humans at theorem proving,
   one could already argue that recreational mathematics received too little attention compared with research in pure mathematics.

   The benefits of research in pure mathematics are often overestimated, while those of recreational mathematics seem to be underestimated.
   The following two examples, though admittedly extreme, illustrate this contrast. (Of course, research and recreational mathematics can overlap.)

   > **Example 1: Mathematical research with questionable benefit**
   >
   > Shinichi Mochizuki may have [proven](https://www.kurims.kyoto-u.ac.jp/preprint/file/RIMS1759.pdf) the [ABC conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abc_conjecture).
   > If correct, his proof would represent a major breakthrough in number theory.
   > Yet years after it was made public, its correctness remained disputed.
   >
   > **Questions:**
   >
   > **(a)** How many people will understand the argument well enough to assess its correctness by the time computers surpass humans at theorem proving?  
   > **(b)** How many practical applications will it have found by then?

   <br>

   > **Example 2: Recreational mathematics at its best**
   >
   > Fermat’s [theorem on sums of two squares](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermat%27s_theorem_on_sums_of_two_squares) states that every prime number $p$ of the form $p = 4k + 1$, with $k \in \mathbb{N}$, can be written as
   >
   > $p = m^2 + n^2$ for some $m, n \in \mathbb{N}$.
   >
   > Euler’s original proof spans several pages.
   > In 1990, Don Zagier found a remarkable [“one-sentence proof”](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermat%27s_theorem_on_sums_of_two_squares#Proofs).
   > Tens of millions of people should be able to understand Zagier’s proof with suitable explanation, and millions would likely find it beautiful.

   There are many proofs with a similar appeal, though few are as striking as Zagier’s. Such proofs often receive less attention than they deserve.

   One notable exception is Martin Aigner and Günter M. Ziegler’s book [*Proofs from THE BOOK*](https://www.amazon.de/-/en/Proofs-BOOK-Martin-Aigner/dp/3540404600).

2. AI now seems close to surpassing humans at mathematical research (November 2026).
   One might expect this development to have considerably strengthened the case for recreational mathematics.

   In Example 1, the answer to (a) seems likely to remain very small, while the answer to (b) could be zero.
   For thousands of other recent results in pure mathematics, the answer to (a) will usually be positive, but the answer to (b) will probably still be zero.
   (This does not rule out practical benefits in the longer term, though.)

   Yet this does not seem to have led to a real change in attitude, judging by the following two reactions:

   - Many research mathematicians fear that AI will undermine the way young people learn to do research and want AI companies to help preserve this way of learning (see [their declaration](https://mathandai.org/)). The focus is still on preparing young people for research, rather than on mathematics as a source of enjoyment in its own right.

   - Meanwhile, the major AI labs devote considerable resources to proving open conjectures, including ones of limited historical importance. This is understandable, given research mathematicians’ priorities. But the apparent lack of comparable attention to simplifying longer classical proofs in the spirit of Example 2 again suggests that recreational mathematics is being neglected.

   I expect that a change in attitude will eventually be unavoidable:

   > Research in applied mathematics will likely be almost completely automated.
   > Pure mathematics has historically been justified by both the enjoyment it offers and its potential contributions to applied mathematics, but once applied research is automated, enjoyment becomes the main reason for humans to pursue it.
   > Here, enjoyment includes curiosity and the satisfaction of understanding.

3. In a world where young people study mathematics for enjoyment rather than to become research mathematicians, there will be losses and gains:

   - Opportunities to experience the joy of making genuinely new discoveries will become rarer.

   - On the other hand, a much more flexible approach to studying mathematics will be possible. Students can focus on the most appealing and accessible results without having to work through dry and technical material unless they choose to.
      (Resources such as our proposed Internet Proof Database would be particularly useful for this approach.)

      Ironically, studying less systematically could even offset the disadvantage mentioned above. This leaves more things unknown, preserving the joy of discovering something new to oneself, even if it is already known to others.

      Today’s Fields medalists might find it hard to imagine enjoying a mathematical life in which they studied less systematically and mainly rediscovered relatively elementary results. Yet many questions that engaged Euler, Gauss, and Poincaré require only modest prerequisites by today’s standards. There seems little reason to think that equally gifted adults in a future generation could not find comparable pleasure in exploring such questions for themselves. Knowing that their discoveries were already known would remove one source of satisfaction, but the results’ elementary character need not diminish the joy of discovering them.
