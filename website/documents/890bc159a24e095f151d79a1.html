Statement

Let p\in\mathbb Q[x] be a nonconstant polynomial with no repeated complex roots, and let a_1,a_2,\ldots be any real numbers with a_j\ge1. Starting from f_0=p, successively substitute x=a_j+1/t and clear denominators:

f_j(t)=t^{\deg f_{j-1}}f_{j-1}(a_j+1/t).

For a real polynomial f, let V(f) count sign changes in the sequence of its coefficients after zero coefficients are omitted. There is an integer N such that V(f_j) is either 0 or 1 for every j\ge N. If V(f_j)=0, f_j has no positive real root; if V(f_j)=1, it has exactly one positive real root.

If the transformations continue with one sign variation for all sufficiently large j, the infinite continued fraction

a_1+\cfrac1{a_2+\cfrac1{a_3+\ddots}}

represents a positive root of p; its finite convergents approximate that root.

Proof 1: Shrinking continued-fraction intervals (external)

A link to the proof can be found here (Sections 2–5, especially geometric proof pp.4–6 and Theorem 3 p.7 (PDF indices 3–6).).

Use Alesina–Galuzzi Theorem 3 and its geometric proof, with the following completion for arbitrary real partial quotients and degree drops. Each degree drop loses at least one degree, so after finitely many drops either the polynomial is constant (V=0 thereafter) or restart with a fixed squarefree real polynomial g of positive degree and no further degree drops. For the tail substitutions write the composed fractional map as (A t+B)/(C t+D); after two steps C,D>0. Its endpoint convergents have distance 1/(CD). Denominators satisfy q_j=a_j q_{j-1}+q_{j-2}, hence grow at least as Fibonacci numbers for arbitrary real a_j>=1. The endpoints are positive and their distance tends to zero. With z=Ct/D the transformed polynomial is a nonzero scalar multiple of (1+z)^deg(g) g((B/D+(A/C)z)/(1+z)); positive rescaling and reversing coefficients preserve variation. The geometric theorem gives V<=1 eventually. Degree-one polynomials need no root-separation bound and satisfy the conclusion immediately. Descartes gives the root assertions. If V=1 persists, each original continued-fraction interval contains a root of p, obtained by reversing all substitutions (removed roots cannot be created). These nested intervals have lengths tending to zero and positive endpoints >=a_1; the common limit is a positive root by continuity. Their endpoints are the finite convergents. Endpoint roots are handled after the last degree drop, not by pretending degrees stay fixed.
