Likely alternative prerequisite level: High school only; lowest recorded proof level: Undergraduate level. At n=0 both sides vanish. The difference between ((n+1)(n+2)/2)^2 and (n(n+1)/2)^2 is (n+1)^3 by direct expansion, so induction proves the formula for every n>=0. Only school polynomial arithmetic is needed; Euler-Maclaurin and derivatives are absent.

Statement

For every integer n\ge0, \sum_{i=0}^n i^3=(n(n+1)/2)^2.

Level
High school only
Level justification

The statement is an elementary finite arithmetic identity.

Mathematical Field
High School Mathematics
Required topics
Finite integer sumsCubes and squares
Review notes
none

Proof 1: Insert the cubic into the second-order formula — outline

Take f(x)=x^3 and p=2 in Euler–Maclaurin on [0,n], with b_2=1/6 and P_2(x)=\{x\}^2-\{x\}+1/6, where \{x\}=x-\lfloor x\rfloor. Simplification gives \sum_{i=0}^n i^3=(n(n+1)/2)^2.
