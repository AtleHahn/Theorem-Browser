Statement

For every integer n\ge5, the generic polynomial x^n+a_1x^{n-1}+\cdots+a_n over F=\mathbb Q(a_1,\ldots,a_n), where the coefficients are algebraically independent indeterminates, is not solvable by radicals. Here a radical expression uses the coefficients, arithmetic operations and extraction of integer roots.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Introductory algebra supplies fields and polynomials. Algebraically independent coefficients mean formal variables with no polynomial relation; adjoining rational functions and the explicitly described radical operations are a small extension. The general nonexistence claim can be understood without Galois theory.

Proof 1: Generic Galois group and nonsolvability

We use the Galois solvability criterion: an equation is solvable in terms of radicals if and only if the Galois group of its splitting field (the smallest field that contains all the roots) is solvable, that is, it admits a finite sequence of subgroups from the whole group to the trivial subgroup such that each is normal in the preceding one, with a quotient group that is cyclic.

For n ≥ 5, the symmetric group {\displaystyle {\mathcal {S}}_{n}} of degree n has only the alternating group {\displaystyle {\mathcal {A}}_{n}} as a nontrivial normal subgroup (see Symmetric group § Normal subgroups). For n ≥ 5, the alternating group {\displaystyle {\mathcal {A}}_{n}} is simple (that is, it does not have any nontrivial normal subgroup) and not abelian. This implies that both {\displaystyle {\mathcal {A}}_{n}} and {\displaystyle {\mathcal {S}}_{n}} are not solvable for n ≥ 5.

For proving that the Galois group is {\displaystyle {\mathcal {S}}_{n},} it is simpler to start from the roots. Let {\displaystyle x_{1},\ldots ,x_{n}} be new indeterminates, aimed to be the roots, and consider the polynomial

{\displaystyle P(x)=x^{n}+b_{1}x^{n-1}+\cdots +b_{n-1}x+b_{n}=(x-x_{1})\cdots (x-x_{n}).}

Let {\displaystyle H=\mathbb {Q} (x_{1},\ldots ,x_{n})} be the field of the rational fractions in {\displaystyle x_{1},\ldots ,x_{n},} and {\displaystyle K=\mathbb {Q} (b_{1},\ldots ,b_{n})} be its subfield generated by the coefficients of {\displaystyle P(x).} The permutations of the {\displaystyle x_{i}} induce automorphisms of H. Vieta's formulas imply that every element of K is a symmetric function of the {\displaystyle x_{i},} and is thus fixed by all these automorphisms. It follows that the Galois group {\displaystyle \operatorname {Gal} (H/K)} is the symmetric group {\displaystyle {\mathcal {S}}_{n}.}

The fundamental theorem of symmetric polynomials implies that the {\displaystyle b_{i}} are algebraic independent, and thus that the map that sends each {\displaystyle a_{i}} to the corresponding {\displaystyle b_{i}} is a field isomorphism from F to K. This means that one may consider {\displaystyle P(x)=0} as a generic equation. This finishes the proof that the Galois group of a general equation is the symmetric group, and thus proves the original Abel–Ruffini theorem, which asserts that the general polynomial equation of degree n cannot be solved in radicals for n ≥ 5.

Beyond undergraduate level
Level justification

The argument constructs a splitting field from algebraically independent indeterminates, identifies its automorphism group, transfers a generic polynomial by a field isomorphism, and invokes the Galois solvability criterion. Understanding these steps requires field extensions, algebraic independence, Galois groups and solvable groups together. Elementary permutation calculations do not eliminate that cumulative prerequisite burden. This exceeds a small extension of introductory abstract algebra even when every named theorem is accepted.
