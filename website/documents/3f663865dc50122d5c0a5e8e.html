Statement

Let n\ge1 be an integer, let \Omega\subset\mathbb R^n be bounded and open with C^1 boundary, and let O be an open neighborhood of \overline\Omega. Write \nu for the outward unit normal and dS for surface measure. If F=(F_1,\ldots,F_n)\in C^1(O,\mathbb R^n) and \operatorname{div}F=\sum_i\partial_iF_i, then \int_\Omega\operatorname{div}F\,dV=\int_{\partial\Omega}F\cdot\nu\,dS.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Understanding the statement involves Divergence of a vector field; Volume integrals; Outward flux through a smooth boundary. These prerequisites fit undergraduate coursework or a brief extension of it. A bright US college student should be able to understand this statement after approximately 30 minutes of focused background research; this assesses understanding the claim, not its proof.

Proof 1: Divergence theorem on a bounded smooth domain

Component identity

For any u\in C^1(O) the component identity \int_\Omega\partial_i u=\int_{\partial\Omega}u\nu_i is obtained as follows.

  1. The first step is to reduce to the case where {\displaystyle u\in C_{c}^{1}(\mathbb {R} ^{n})}. Pick {\displaystyle \phi \in C_{c}^{\infty }(O)} such that {\displaystyle \phi =1} on {\displaystyle {\overline {\Omega }}}. Note that {\displaystyle \phi u\in C_{c}^{1}(O)\subset C_{c}^{1}(\mathbb {R} ^{n})} and {\displaystyle \phi u=u} on {\displaystyle {\overline {\Omega }}}. Hence it suffices to prove the theorem for {\displaystyle \phi u}. Hence we may assume that {\displaystyle u\in C_{c}^{1}(\mathbb {R} ^{n})}.

  2. Let {\displaystyle x_{0}\in \partial \Omega } be arbitrary. The assumption that {\displaystyle {\overline {\Omega }}} has {\displaystyle C^{1}} boundary means that there is an open neighborhood {\displaystyle U} of {\displaystyle x_{0}} in {\displaystyle \mathbb {R} ^{n}} such that {\displaystyle \partial \Omega \cap U} is the graph of a {\displaystyle C^{1}} function with {\displaystyle \Omega \cap U} lying on one side of this graph. More precisely, this means that after a translation and rotation of {\displaystyle \Omega }, there are {\displaystyle r>0} and {\displaystyle h>0} and a {\displaystyle C^{1}} function {\displaystyle g:\mathbb {R} ^{n-1}\to \mathbb {R} }, such that with the notation

    {\displaystyle x'=(x_{1},\dots ,x_{n-1}),}

    it holds that

    {\displaystyle U=\{x\in \mathbb {R} ^{n}:|x'|<r{\text{ and }}|x_{n}-g(x')|<h\}}

    and for {\displaystyle x\in U},

    {\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}x_{n}=g(x')&\implies x\in \partial \Omega ,\\-h<x_{n}-g(x')<0&\implies x\in \Omega ,\\0<x_{n}-g(x')<h&\implies x\notin \Omega .\\\end{aligned}}}

    Since {\displaystyle \partial \Omega } is compact, we can cover {\displaystyle \partial \Omega } with finitely many neighborhoods {\displaystyle U_{1},\dots ,U_{N}} of the above form. Note that {\displaystyle \{\Omega ,U_{1},\dots ,U_{N}\}} is an open cover of {\displaystyle {\overline {\Omega }}=\Omega \cup \partial \Omega }. By using a {\displaystyle C^{\infty }} partition of unity subordinate to this cover, it suffices to prove the theorem in the case where either {\displaystyle u} has compact support in {\displaystyle \Omega } or {\displaystyle u} has compact support in some {\displaystyle U_{j}}. If {\displaystyle u} has compact support in {\displaystyle \Omega }, then for all {\displaystyle i\in \{1,\dots ,n\}}, {\displaystyle \int _{\Omega }u_{x_{i}}\,dV=\int _{\mathbb {R} ^{n}}u_{x_{i}}\,dV=\int _{\mathbb {R} ^{n-1}}\int _{-\infty }^{\infty }u_{x_{i}}(x)\,dx_{i}\,dx'=0} by the fundamental theorem of calculus, and {\displaystyle \int _{\partial \Omega }u\nu _{i}\,dS=0} since {\displaystyle u} vanishes on a neighborhood of {\displaystyle \partial \Omega }. Thus the theorem holds for {\displaystyle u} with compact support in {\displaystyle \Omega }. Thus we have reduced to the case where {\displaystyle u} has compact support in some {\displaystyle U_{j}}.

  3. So assume {\displaystyle u} has compact support in some {\displaystyle U_{j}}. The last step now is to show that the theorem is true by direct computation. Change notation to {\displaystyle U=U_{j}}, and bring in the notation from (2) used to describe {\displaystyle U}. Note that this means that we have rotated and translated {\displaystyle \Omega }. This is a valid reduction since the theorem is invariant under rotations and translations of coordinates. Since {\displaystyle u(x)=0} for {\displaystyle |x'|\geq r} and for {\displaystyle |x_{n}-g(x')|\geq h}, we have for each {\displaystyle i\in \{1,\dots ,n\}} that

    {\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}\int _{\Omega }u_{x_{i}}\,dV&=\int _{|x'|<r}\int _{g(x')-h}^{g(x')}u_{x_{i}}(x',x_{n})\,dx_{n}\,dx'\\&=\int _{\mathbb {R} ^{n-1}}\int _{-\infty }^{g(x')}u_{x_{i}}(x',x_{n})\,dx_{n}\,dx'.\end{aligned}}}

    For {\displaystyle i=n} we have by the fundamental theorem of calculus that

    {\displaystyle \int _{\mathbb {R} ^{n-1}}\int _{-\infty }^{g(x')}u_{x_{n}}(x',x_{n})\,dx_{n}\,dx'=\int _{\mathbb {R} ^{n-1}}u(x',g(x'))\,dx'.}

    Now fix {\displaystyle i\in \{1,\dots ,n-1\}}. Note that

    {\displaystyle \int _{\mathbb {R} ^{n-1}}\int _{-\infty }^{g(x')}u_{x_{i}}(x',x_{n})\,dx_{n}\,dx'=\int _{\mathbb {R} ^{n-1}}\int _{-\infty }^{0}u_{x_{i}}(x',g(x')+s)\,ds\,dx'}

    Define {\displaystyle v:\mathbb {R} ^{n}\to \mathbb {R} } by {\displaystyle v(x',s)=u(x',g(x')+s)}. By the chain rule,

    {\displaystyle v_{x_{i}}(x',s)=u_{x_{i}}(x',g(x')+s)+u_{x_{n}}(x',g(x')+s)g_{x_{i}}(x').}

    But since {\displaystyle v} has compact support, we can integrate out {\displaystyle dx_{i}} first to deduce that

    {\displaystyle \int _{\mathbb {R} ^{n-1}}\int _{-\infty }^{0}v_{x_{i}}(x',s)\,ds\,dx'=0.}

    Thus

    {\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}\int _{\mathbb {R} ^{n-1}}\int _{-\infty }^{0}u_{x_{i}}(x',g(x')+s)\,ds\,dx'&=\int _{\mathbb {R} ^{n-1}}\int _{-\infty }^{0}-u_{x_{n}}(x',g(x')+s)g_{x_{i}}(x')\,ds\,dx'\\&=\int _{\mathbb {R} ^{n-1}}-u(x',g(x'))g_{x_{i}}(x')\,dx'.\end{aligned}}}

    In summary, with {\displaystyle \nabla u=(u_{x_{1}},\dots ,u_{x_{n}})} we have

    {\displaystyle \int _{\Omega }\nabla u\,dV=\int _{\mathbb {R} ^{n-1}}\int _{-\infty }^{g(x')}\nabla u\,dV=\int _{\mathbb {R} ^{n-1}}u(x',g(x'))(-\nabla g(x'),1)\,dx'.}

    Recall that the outward unit normal to the graph {\displaystyle \Gamma } of {\displaystyle g} at a point {\displaystyle (x',g(x'))\in \Gamma } is {\displaystyle \nu (x',g(x'))={\frac {1}{\sqrt {1+|\nabla g(x')|^{2}}}}(-\nabla g(x'),1)} and that the surface element {\displaystyle dS} is given by {\textstyle dS={\sqrt {1+|\nabla g(x')|^{2}}}\,dx'}. Thus

    {\displaystyle \int _{\Omega }\nabla u\,dV=\int _{\partial \Omega }u\nu \,dS.}
