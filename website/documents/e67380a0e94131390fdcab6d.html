Statement

Consider n\ge3 distinct lines in the Euclidean plane, with no parallel pair and no three concurrent. A triangular face means a bounded triangular region containing no other arrangement line in its interior. There are at least n-2 triangular faces.

A simple arrangement has no parallel pair and no three concurrent lines; its finite polygon faces are elementary plane geometry.

Proof 1: Preserve triangle areas until the first line collision — outline

Every simple arrangement of three or more lines has at least one triangular face. This follows easily by induction from the fact that adding a line to an arrangement cannot decrease the number of triangular faces: if the line cuts an existing triangle, one of the resulting two pieces is again a triangle.

If the {\displaystyle n} given lines are all moved without changing their slopes, their new positions can be described by a system of {\displaystyle n} real numbers, the offsets of each line from its original position. For each triangular face, there is a linear equation on the offsets of its three lines that, if satisfied, causes the face to retain its original area. Here the slopes fix its shape, and the linear equation preserves its signed scale. If there could be fewer than {\displaystyle n-2} triangles, then (because there would be more variables than equations constraining them) it would be possible to fix two of the lines in place and find a simultaneous linear motion of all remaining lines, keeping their slopes fixed, that preserves all of the triangle areas. Reversing the direction of motion if necessary, such a motion must pass through arrangements that are not simple, for instance when one of the moving lines passes over the crossing point of the two fixed lines. At the time when the moving lines first form a non-simple arrangement, three or more lines meet at a point. Just before these lines meet, by the weaker version of the theorem, the subset of lines that meet would have a triangular face, {\displaystyle \Delta }. Near the meeting point, lines not participating stay away, so this triangle is also a face of the full arrangement. Because this meeting is the first time the arrangement becomes non-simple, it cannot have changed its combinatorial structure from the original arrangement, which must therefore also contain the same triangle {\displaystyle \Delta }. At the time when the lines defining triangle {\displaystyle \Delta } meet, its area becomes zero, but this contradicts the invariance of the areas of the triangles in the initial arrangement. The contradiction shows the impossibility of the assumption that there are fewer than {\displaystyle n-2} triangles.

Proof 2: Count positive sides of bounded faces — outline

Consider only the bounded edges and bounded faces. Let {\displaystyle E} be the number of bounded edges, let {\displaystyle F} be the number of bounded faces, and let {\displaystyle t_{3}} be the number of triangular faces. An edge {\displaystyle e} of a face {\displaystyle f} is called a positive side of {\displaystyle f} if the sum of the two angles made by {\displaystyle e} with its adjacent sides in {\displaystyle f} is less than {\displaystyle 180^{\circ }}. Each bounded edge is a positive side of exactly one of its two incident faces. On the other hand, every bounded face that is not a triangle has at most two positive sides, and if there are two then they are adjacent. Indeed, two nonadjacent positive sides would force the corresponding exterior angles to have total greater than {\displaystyle 360^{\circ }}. Thus, counting positive sides gives {\displaystyle E\leq 2F+t_{3}}. Since {\displaystyle E=n(n-2)} and {\displaystyle 2F=(n-1)(n-2)}, it follows that {\displaystyle t_{3}\geq n-2}.
