Statement

Let A,B,C,D,E,F be six distinct points on a nondegenerate conic in the real projective plane. Put X=AB\cap DE, Y=BC\cap EF, Z=CD\cap FA, where AB denotes the projective line through A and B. Then X,Y,Z are collinear.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

The real projective plane and its nondegenerate conics are homogeneous-coordinate and quadratic-form constructions, a limited extension of undergraduate linear algebra. The statement itself does not require cross-ratio theory.

Proof 1: Cayley–Bacharach cubic union outline

Use the Cayley–Bacharach theorem: if two cubics without a common component intersect in nine distinct points, any cubic through eight of those points also passes through the ninth.

Take the eight points as the six points on the hexagon and two of the points, X and Y, on the would-be Pascal line, and the ninth point as the third point Z. The first two cubics are two sets of three lines through the six points on the hexagon: the set AB,CD,EF, and the set BC,DE,FA. The third cubic is the union of the conic and the line XY. Here the “ninth intersection” Z cannot lie on the conic, and hence it lies on XY.

The source gives this argument in generic position; the stated incidence extends to well-defined limiting configurations by continuity of projective incidence.

Proof 2: Bézout and a cubic pencil

Suppose f is the cubic polynomial vanishing on the three lines through AB, CD, EF and g is the cubic vanishing on the other three lines BC, DE, FA. Pick a generic point P on the conic and choose λ so that the cubic h = f + λg vanishes on P. Then h = 0 is a cubic that has 7 points A, B, C, D, E, F, P in common with the conic. But by Bézout's theorem a cubic and a conic have at most 3 × 2 = 6 points in common, unless they have a common component. So the cubic h = 0 has a component in common with the conic which must be the conic itself, so h = 0 is the union of the conic and a line. This line is the Pascal line, because any point in the intersection of the solution sets of f and g will also be in the solution set of h. In particular, X,Y,Z lie on h=0 and, since they are not on the nondegenerate conic, must lie on the remaining line.

Proof 3: Cross-ratio projections outline

Put P=DC\cap AB and Q=FA\cap BC. Project the conic tetrad A,B,C,E from D onto AB to get A,B,P,X, and from F onto BC to get Q,B,C,Y. Preservation of the conic cross-ratio gives R(A,B;P,X)=R(Q,B;C,Y). The two line ranges have the common corresponding point B; the projective perspectivity criterion then makes the joins AQ,PC,XY concurrent. The first two meet at Z=FA\cap CD, so XY also passes through Z. The source’s projective continuity reduction handles limiting configurations where these auxiliary projections become degenerate.

Proof 4: Polar duality applied to Brianchon — outline

Put (A_1,A_2,A_3,A_4,A_5,A_6)=(A,B,C,D,E,F), with indices taken cyclically. Use the polarity of the nonsingular conic: it sends a point on the conic to its tangent, interchanges joins and intersections, and takes concurrency of lines to collinearity of their poles. Let \ell_i be the tangent at A_i, and put V_i=\ell_i\cap\ell_{i+1} with cyclic indices. Then the polar of V_i is A_iA_{i+1}.

Apply the external Brianchon theorem to the hexagon formed by the six tangents: the lines V_1V_4, V_2V_5, V_3V_6 are concurrent. Their poles are respectively X,Y,Z. Dualizing this concurrency gives the asserted collinearity.
