Statement

In a finite simple graph, let d(U,V) be the number of ordered adjacent pairs in U\times V divided by |U||V|, for nonempty vertex subsets U,V. The pair (U,V) is \varepsilon-regular if |d(U',V')-d(U,V)|\leq\varepsilon whenever U'\subseteq U, V'\subseteq V, |U'|\geq\varepsilon|U| and |V'|\geq\varepsilon|V|. Let X,Y,Z be nonempty vertex subsets whose three pairs are \varepsilon-regular for 0<\varepsilon\leq1/2, with densities d_{XY},d_{XZ},d_{YZ}\geq2\varepsilon. Then the number of ordered triples (x,y,z)\in X\times Y\times Z forming a triangle is at least (1-2\varepsilon)(d_{XY}-\varepsilon)(d_{XZ}-\varepsilon)(d_{YZ}-\varepsilon)|X||Y||Z|.

Level
High school only
Level justification

Regularity and densities are explicitly finite counting ratios and subset inequalities.

Proof 1: Discard low-degree vertices and count neighborhood edges

Low-degree lemma

Since {\displaystyle (X,Y)} is a regular pair, less than {\displaystyle \varepsilon |X|} of the vertices in {\displaystyle X} have fewer than {\displaystyle (d_{XY}-\varepsilon )|Y|} neighbors in {\displaystyle Y}; otherwise, this set of vertices from {\displaystyle X} together with all of {\displaystyle Y} would witness irregularity of {\displaystyle (X,Y)}, a contradiction. Intuitively, we are saying that not too many vertices in {\displaystyle X} can have a small degree in {\displaystyle Y}.

Triangle count

By an analogous argument in the pair {\displaystyle (X,Z)}, less than {\displaystyle \varepsilon |X|} of the vertices in {\displaystyle X} have fewer than {\displaystyle (d_{XZ}-\varepsilon )|Z|} neighbors in {\displaystyle Z}. Combining these two subsets of {\displaystyle X} and taking their complement, we obtain a subset {\displaystyle X'\subseteq X} of size at least {\displaystyle (1-2\varepsilon )|X|} such that every vertex {\displaystyle x\in X'} has at least {\displaystyle (d_{XY}-\varepsilon )|Y|} neighbors in {\displaystyle Y} and at least {\displaystyle (d_{XZ}-\varepsilon )|Z|} neighbors in {\displaystyle Z}.

We also know that {\displaystyle d_{XY},d_{XZ}\geq 2\varepsilon }, and that {\displaystyle (Y,Z)} is an {\displaystyle \varepsilon }-regular pair; therefore, the density between the neighborhood of {\displaystyle x} in {\displaystyle Y} and the neighborhood of {\displaystyle x} in {\displaystyle Z} is at least {\displaystyle (d_{YZ}-\varepsilon )}, because by regularity it is {\displaystyle \varepsilon }-close to the actual density between {\displaystyle Y} and {\displaystyle Z}.

Summing up, for each of these at least {\displaystyle (1-2\varepsilon )|X|} vertices {\displaystyle x\in X'}, there are at least {\displaystyle (d_{XY}-\epsilon )(d_{XZ}-\epsilon )(d_{YZ}-\epsilon )|Y||Z|} ordered choices of adjacent pairs between the neighborhood of {\displaystyle x} in {\displaystyle Y} and the neighborhood of {\displaystyle x} in {\displaystyle Z}. From there we can conclude this proof.
