Major

Shows that a classically interpreted language with the stated diagonal property cannot define its own truth predicate for all its sentences. Applied to arithmetic, it separates arithmetical truth from arithmetically definable predicates and gives a precise reason to distinguish a language from a metalanguage used to describe its truth.

Statement

Let (L,\mathcal N) be a classically interpreted formal language with negation and Gödel numbering g. Suppose it has the semantic diagonal property: for each one-variable formula B(v) there is a sentence D with \mathcal N\models D\leftrightarrow B(g(D)). Then no formula \mathrm{True}(v) satisfies \mathcal N\models\mathrm{True}(g(D))\leftrightarrow D for every sentence D.

Proof 1: Apply diagonalization to the negated truth formula

The proof is by reductio ad absurdum. Suppose that an L-formula \mathrm{True}(v) as in the statement existed, i.e., if A is an L-sentence, then \mathrm{True}(g(A)) holds in \mathcal N if and only if A holds in \mathcal N. Hence for all A, the formula

\mathrm{True}(g(A))\iff A

holds in \mathcal N. But the assumed diagonal property, with B(v) instantiated to \neg\mathrm{True}(v), yields a counterexample to this equivalence, by giving a “liar” sentence S such that

S\iff\neg\mathrm{True}(g(S))

holds in \mathcal N. This is a contradiction.
