Statement

For 1<p<\infty, H denotes the Hilbert transform on L^p(\mathbb R), with sign convention Hf(t)=\pi^{-1}\operatorname{p.v.}\int_{\mathbb R}f(\tau)/(t-\tau)\,d\tau. The Fourier transform convention is \widehat f(\xi)=\int f(t)e^{-2\pi it\xi}\,dt, extended to L^2 or tempered distributions as appropriate. On L^p(\mathbb R), H^2=-I.

Beyond undergraduate level
Level justification

Proof 1: The square of the Hilbert transform is minus the identity — outline

When the Hilbert transform is applied twice, the phase of the negative and positive frequency components of u(t) are respectively shifted by +180° and −180°, which are equivalent amounts. The signal is negated; i.e., H(H(u)) = −u, because

{\displaystyle \left(\sigma _{\operatorname {H} }(\omega )\right)^{2}=e^{\pm i\pi }=-1\quad {\text{for }}\omega \neq 0.}

Here the multiplier is -i\operatorname{sgn}\omega. The argument is direct on L^2, where the single frequency zero is negligible; boundedness of H on L^p and density of L^p\cap L^2 extend the identity to L^p.

Level
Beyond undergraduate level
Level justification

Square the multiplier almost everywhere in L2 and extend by Lp boundedness and density. Lebesgue Fourier theory, density and operator extensions require graduate analysis.
