Statement

Fix 0<p<1, put q=1-p, and define b_n(k)=\binom nkp^kq^{n-k} for integers 0\le k\le n, where \binom nk=n!/(k!(n-k)!) and 0!=1. Fix a nonzero real c, and let k_n be nearest to np-c\sqrt{npq}. Then \frac{b_n(k_n+1)-b_n(k_n)}{b_n(k_n)}\frac{npq}{np-k_n}\to1. All denominators are nonzero for sufficiently large n.

Proof 1: Compare leading terms after rounding

Use the adjacent binomial-mass identity

\frac{b_n(k+1)-b_n(k)}{b_n(k)}=\frac{np-k-q}{kq+q}.

Put c_n=(np-k_n)/\sqrt{npq}. As k_n takes just integral values, c is subject to a rounding error. However, the maximum of this error, 0.5/\sqrt{npq}, is a vanishing value. Thus c_n\to c\ne0.

The required result can be shown directly:

\begin{aligned} \frac{b_n(k_n+1)-b_n(k_n)}{b_n(k_n)} \frac{npq}{np-k_n} &=\frac{np-k_n-q}{k_nq+q}\frac{\sqrt{npq}}{c_n}\\ &=\frac{c_n\sqrt{npq}-q}{npq-c_nq\sqrt{npq}+q} \frac{\sqrt{npq}}{c_n}\\ &\longrightarrow1. \end{aligned}

The last holds because the term c_nnpq dominates both the denominator and the numerator as n\to\infty.

Level justification

Rounding contributes a bounded error divided by sqrt(npq), so c_n tends to the nonzero c. Substitution into the exact mass ratio and division by the leading powers of n gives the limit. Basic sequence limits and algebra suffice; no asymptotic probability theorem is needed.
