Statement

If f:\mathbb C\to\mathbb C is holomorphic everywhere and bounded, then f is constant.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Real analysis supplies bounds, uniform convergence and derivatives. Holomorphic means complex differentiable, and entire means so on all of C. These direct extensions suffice for the full bound, image or convergence claim; no Riemann surfaces or complex-geometric structures occur.

Proof 1: Send the coefficient-estimate radius to infinity — outline

If {\displaystyle f} is an entire function, it can be represented by its Taylor series about 0:

{\displaystyle f(z)=\sum _{k=0}^{\infty }a_{k}z^{k}}

where (by Cauchy's integral formula)

{\displaystyle a_{k}={\frac {f^{(k)}(0)}{k!}}={1 \over 2\pi i}\oint _{C_{r}}{\frac {f(\zeta )}{\zeta ^{k+1}}}\,d\zeta }

and {\displaystyle C_{r}} is the positively oriented circle about 0 of radius {\displaystyle r>0}. Suppose {\displaystyle f} is bounded: i.e. there exists a constant {\displaystyle M} such that {\displaystyle |f(z)|\leq M} for all {\displaystyle z}. We can estimate directly

{\displaystyle |a_{k}|\leq {\frac {1}{2\pi }}\oint _{C_{r}}{\frac {|f(\zeta )|}{|\zeta |^{k+1}}}\,|d\zeta |\leq {\frac {1}{2\pi }}\oint _{C_{r}}{\frac {M}{r^{k+1}}}\,|d\zeta |={\frac {M}{2\pi r^{k+1}}}\oint _{C_{r}}|d\zeta |={\frac {M}{2\pi r^{k+1}}}2\pi r={\frac {M}{r^{k}}},}

where in the second inequality we have used the fact that {\displaystyle |z|=r} on the circle {\displaystyle C_{r}}. (This estimate is known as Cauchy's estimate.) But the choice of {\displaystyle r} in the above is an arbitrary positive number. Therefore, letting {\displaystyle r} tend to infinity (we let {\displaystyle r} tend to infinity since {\displaystyle f} is analytic on the entire plane) gives {\displaystyle a_{k}=0} for all {\displaystyle k\geq 1}. Thus {\displaystyle f(z)=a_{0}} and this proves the theorem.

Proof 2: Compare averages on overlapping large disks — outline

The real and imaginary parts of f are harmonic. By their disk mean-value property, the average of f over a disk equals its value at the center.

Given two points, choose two balls with the given points as centers and of equal radius. If the radius is large enough, the two balls will coincide except for an arbitrarily small proportion of their volume. Since {\displaystyle f} is bounded, the averages of it over the two balls are arbitrarily close, and so {\displaystyle f} assumes the same value at any two points.
