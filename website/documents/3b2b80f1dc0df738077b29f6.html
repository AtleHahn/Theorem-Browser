Proof 1: Real monotone subsequence and successive coordinates

Monotone subsequence lemma

Lemma: Every infinite sequence {\displaystyle (x_{n})} in {\displaystyle \mathbb {R} } has an infinite monotone subsequence (a subsequence that is either non-decreasing or non-increasing).

Let us call a positive integer {\displaystyle n} of a sequence a "peak index" of the sequence when {\displaystyle x_{m}\leq x_{n}} for every {\displaystyle m>n}. Suppose first that the sequence has infinitely many peaks, which means there is a subsequence with the following indices {\displaystyle n_{1}<n_{2}<n_{3}<\dots <n_{j}<\dots } and the following terms {\displaystyle x_{n_{1}}\geq x_{n_{2}}\geq x_{n_{3}}\geq \dots \geq x_{n_{j}}\geq \dots }. So, the infinite sequence {\displaystyle (x_{n})} in {\displaystyle \mathbb {R} } has a monotone (non-increasing) subsequence, which is {\displaystyle (x_{n_{j}})}. But suppose now that there are only finitely many peaks, let {\displaystyle N} be the final peak if one exists (let {\displaystyle N=0} otherwise) and let the first index of a new subsequence {\displaystyle (x_{n_{j}})} be set to {\displaystyle n_{1}=N+1}. Then {\displaystyle n_{1}} is not a peak, since {\displaystyle n_{1}} comes after the final peak, which implies the existence of {\displaystyle n_{2}} with {\displaystyle n_{1}<n_{2}} and {\displaystyle x_{n_{1}}<x_{n_{2}}}. Again, {\displaystyle n_{2}} comes after the final peak, hence there is an {\displaystyle n_{3}} where {\displaystyle n_{2}<n_{3}} with {\displaystyle x_{n_{2}}<x_{n_{3}}}. Repeating this process leads to an infinite non-decreasing subsequence  {\displaystyle x_{n_{1}}<x_{n_{2}}<x_{n_{3}}<\ldots }, thereby proving that every infinite sequence {\displaystyle (x_{n})} in {\displaystyle \mathbb {R} } has a monotone subsequence.

Convergence

Now suppose one has an infinite bounded sequence in {\displaystyle \mathbb {R} }. By the local monotone subsequence lemma, there exists a monotone subsequence, likewise also bounded. It follows from the monotone convergence theorem that this subsequence converges.

Successive coordinate subsequences

Let I=\mathbb N. Every infinite subset of I below is enumerated in increasing order, so it defines a subsequence.

Let {\displaystyle (x_{m})_{m\in I}} be any bounded sequence in {\displaystyle \mathbb {R} ^{n}}, then it can be expressed as an n-tuple of sequences in {\displaystyle \mathbb {R} } by writing {\displaystyle x_{m}=(x_{m1},x_{m2},\dots ,x_{mn})}, where {\displaystyle (x_{mj})_{m\in I}} is a sequence for {\displaystyle j=1,2,\dots ,n}. Since {\displaystyle (x_{m})} is bounded, {\displaystyle (x_{mj})} is also bounded for {\displaystyle j=1,2,\dots ,n}. It follows then by the real Bolzano–Weierstrass theorem proved locally that {\displaystyle (x_{m1})} has a convergent subsequence and hence there exists an infinite set {\displaystyle K_{1}\subseteq I} such that {\displaystyle (x_{m1})_{m\in K_{1}}} converges. For the sequence {\displaystyle (x_{m2})}, by applying the locally proved real Bolzano–Weierstrass theorem once again there exists an infinite set {\displaystyle K_{2}\subseteq K_{1}\subseteq I} such that {\displaystyle (x_{m2})_{m\in K_{2}}} converges and hence {\displaystyle (x_{m2})} has a convergent subsequence. This reasoning may be applied until we obtain an infinite set {\displaystyle K_{n}} for which {\displaystyle (x_{mj})_{m\in K_{n}}} converges for {\displaystyle j=1,2,\dots ,n}. Hence, {\displaystyle (x_{m})_{m\in K_{n}}} converges and therefore, since {\displaystyle (x_{m})} was arbitrary, any bounded sequence in {\displaystyle \mathbb {R} ^{n}} has a convergent subsequence.
