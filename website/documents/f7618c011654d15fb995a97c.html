Statement

Graphs are finite and simple. A clique is a set of pairwise adjacent vertices; its maximum size is the clique number. A proper coloring assigns different colors to adjacent vertices; the least number of colors is the chromatic number. An induced subgraph keeps a vertex subset and all edges between its vertices. A graph is perfect if these two numbers agree for every induced subgraph. The complement joins exactly the distinct nonadjacent vertex pairs. A graph is bipartite if its vertices split into two sets with no edge inside either set. The line graph has the original edges as vertices, with two adjacent exactly when they share an endpoint. The complement of the line graph of every bipartite graph is perfect.

High school only
Level justification

All operations are defined locally: edges become line-graph vertices, complementation reverses adjacency, and perfection compares coloring and clique counts on every subset. These finite constructions can be followed by drawing an edge set and checking pairs. They do not introduce substantial background beyond elementary graph definitions.

Proof 1: Translate cliques and star-partition colorings — outline

A clique in the complement of the line graph of G is just a matching in G. And a coloring in the complement of the line graph of G, when G is bipartite, is a partition of the edges of G into subsets of edges sharing a common endpoint; the endpoints shared by each of these subsets form a vertex cover for G. Conversely, a vertex cover gives such a partition by assigning each edge to one of its covering endpoints and discarding empty classes.

Therefore, Kőnig's theorem, which equates the maximum matching size with the minimum vertex-cover size in a bipartite graph, can also be interpreted as stating that the complements of line graphs of bipartite graphs are perfect. Restricting to an arbitrary subset of the edges gives the same correspondence for each induced subgraph.
