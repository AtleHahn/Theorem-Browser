Statement

For an integer k\geq1, set L_1(x)=\ln x and L_{j+1}(x)=\ln L_j(x). Let N_k be the least positive integer where L_k(N_k) is defined and at least 1. Empty products below equal 1. Then \sum_{n=N_k}^{\infty}[n\prod_{j=1}^k L_j(n)]^{-1} diverges.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Understanding the statement involves Real series and divergence; Iterated logarithms; Finite products. These prerequisites fit undergraduate coursework or a brief extension of it. Proof 1: Divergence of the iterated-logarithm series — proof

To see the divergence of the series using the integral test, note that by repeated application of the chain rule

\frac{d}{dx}L_{k+1}(x)=\frac{d}{dx}\ln(L_k(x)) =\frac{1}{L_k(x)}\frac{d}{dx}L_k(x) =\cdots=\frac{1}{x\prod_{j=1}^{k}L_j(x)},

hence

\int_{N_k}^{\infty}\frac{dx}{x\prod_{j=1}^{k}L_j(x)} =\left.L_{k+1}(x)\right|_{N_k}^{\infty}=\infty.

The integrand is positive and decreasing on this interval, as required by the integral test.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Use the next iterated logarithm as antiderivative and apply the integral test to its unbounded limit. Undergraduate calculus and series suffice.
