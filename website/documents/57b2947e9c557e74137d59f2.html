Statement

Let f:S^1\to S^1 be a smooth orientation-preserving diffeomorphism, with f(e^{i\theta})=e^{ig(\theta)} and g(\theta+2\pi)=g(\theta)+2\pi. For any smooth \psi:[0,1]\to[0,1] equal to zero near zero and one near one, the map F(0)=0, F(re^{i\theta})=r\exp(i\psi(r)g(\theta)+i(1-\psi(r))\theta) is a smooth orientation-preserving diffeomorphism of the closed unit disk extending f. Here a smooth diffeomorphism is a bijection with smooth inverse; smoothness at the disk boundary means smooth extension to a neighborhood.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Understanding the statement involves smooth maps in real coordinates; circle parametrization; polar coordinates; smooth inverses. These prerequisites fit undergraduate coursework or a brief extension of it. A bright US college student should be able to understand this statement after approximately 30 minutes of focused background research; this assesses understanding the claim, not its proof.

Proof 1: Radial interpolation of an angular lift — outline

If f is an orientation-preserving smooth diffeomorphism of the circle, the Alexander extension gives a way of extending f to a diffeomorphism of the unit disk D:

{\displaystyle \displaystyle {F(r,\theta )=r\exp[i\psi (r)g(\theta )+i(1-\psi (r))\theta ],}}

where ψ is a smooth function with values in [0,1], equal to 0 near 0 and 1 near 1, and

{\displaystyle \displaystyle {f(e^{i\theta })=e^{ig(\theta )},}}

with g(θ + 2π) = g(θ) + 2π.

The extension F can be continued to any larger disk |z| < R with R > 1. Set F(0)=0 at the origin.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

The lift is given explicitly. Its interpolated angular derivative is psi(r)gprime(theta)+1-psi(r)>0; each radius therefore has a smooth invertible angular map. Near zero the formula is the identity. Polar coordinates and the local inverse theorem suffice, without general manifold theory.
