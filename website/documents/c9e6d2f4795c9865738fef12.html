Statement

For f(z)=1/(1+z^2), its Taylor series at zero has radius of convergence 1, and at 1 has radius \sqrt2. Each series converges within its disk and diverges at every point strictly outside it.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Power-series coefficients, convergence radii and complex exponentials extend familiar calculus. A circle integral is parametrized by a real angle, and formal reciprocals use coefficient multiplication. These direct definitions suffice without abstract continuation theory.

Proof 1: Locate the nearest poles

The function f extends into a meromorphic function

{\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}&f:\mathbb {C} \cup \{\infty \}\to \mathbb {C} \cup \{\infty \}\\&f(z)={\frac {1}{1+z^{2}}}\end{aligned}}}

on the compactified complex plane. It has simple poles at {\textstyle z=i} and {\textstyle z=-i}, and it is analytic elsewhere. Use the Taylor-series radius theorem: the convergence radius is the distance from the centre to the nearest complex singularity. Write B(z_0,r)=\{z:|z-z_0|<r\}. Therefore, Taylor series of f centered at 0 converges on B(0, 1) and it does not converge for any z ∈ C with |z| > 1 due to the poles at i and −i. For the same reason the Taylor series of f centered at 1 converges on {\textstyle B(1,{\sqrt {2}})} and does not converge for any z ∈ C with {\textstyle \left\vert z-1\right\vert >{\sqrt {2}}}.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Locate the two poles and apply the supplied nearest-singularity Taylor-radius theorem. Undergraduate complex analysis suffices; compactification is incidental to the finite pole-distance calculation and requires no Riemann-surface theory.
