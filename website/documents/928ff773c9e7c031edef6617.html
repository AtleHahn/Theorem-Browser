Statement

The real matrix A=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\1&3\end{pmatrix} has no orthogonal basis of eigenvectors for the usual Euclidean inner product on \mathbb R^2.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Understanding the statement involves Real matrices; Eigenvectors; Euclidean inner product. These prerequisites fit undergraduate coursework or a brief extension of it. A bright US college student should be able to understand this statement after approximately 30 minutes of focused background research; this assesses understanding the claim, not its proof.

Proof 1: Compute the inner product of the two eigenvectors

The {\displaystyle 2\times 2} real matrix

{\displaystyle \mathbf {A} ={\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\1&3\end{bmatrix}}}

may be diagonalized as {\displaystyle \mathbf {Q} ^{-1}\mathbf {A} \mathbf {Q} }, where {\displaystyle \mathbf {Q} } is a non-singular matrix to be determined:

{\displaystyle \mathbf {Q} ={\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{bmatrix}}\in \mathbb {R} ^{2\times 2}.}

Then

{\displaystyle {\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{bmatrix}}^{-1}{\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\1&3\end{bmatrix}}{\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{bmatrix}}={\begin{bmatrix}x&0\\0&y\end{bmatrix}},}

for some real numbers {\displaystyle x} and {\displaystyle y}.

Multiplying both sides of the equation on the left by {\displaystyle \mathbf {Q} },

{\displaystyle {\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\1&3\end{bmatrix}}{\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{bmatrix}}={\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{bmatrix}}{\begin{bmatrix}x&0\\0&y\end{bmatrix}}.}

The above equation can be decomposed into two simultaneous equations:

{\displaystyle {\begin{cases}{\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\1&3\end{bmatrix}}{\begin{bmatrix}a\\c\end{bmatrix}}={\begin{bmatrix}ax\\cx\end{bmatrix}}\\[1.2ex]{\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\1&3\end{bmatrix}}{\begin{bmatrix}b\\d\end{bmatrix}}={\begin{bmatrix}by\\dy\end{bmatrix}}.\end{cases}}}

Factoring out the eigenvalues {\displaystyle x} and {\displaystyle y},

{\displaystyle {\begin{cases}{\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\1&3\end{bmatrix}}{\begin{bmatrix}a\\c\end{bmatrix}}=x{\begin{bmatrix}a\\c\end{bmatrix}}\\[1.2ex]{\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\1&3\end{bmatrix}}{\begin{bmatrix}b\\d\end{bmatrix}}=y{\begin{bmatrix}b\\d\end{bmatrix}}.\end{cases}}}

Letting

{\displaystyle \mathbf {a} ={\begin{bmatrix}a\\c\end{bmatrix}}\quad {\text{and}}\quad \mathbf {b} ={\begin{bmatrix}b\\d\end{bmatrix}}}

gives us a system of two vector equations:

{\displaystyle {\begin{cases}\mathbf {A} \mathbf {a} =x\mathbf {a} \\\mathbf {A} \mathbf {b} =y\mathbf {b} .\end{cases}}}

This can be represented by a single vector equation involving two solutions as eigenvalues:

{\displaystyle \mathbf {A} \mathbf {u} =\lambda \mathbf {u} ,}

where {\displaystyle \lambda } represents any of the eigenvalues, {\displaystyle x} or {\displaystyle y}, and {\displaystyle \mathbf {u} } represents the corresponding eigenvector, {\displaystyle \mathbf {a} } or {\displaystyle \mathbf {b} }.

Shifting {\displaystyle \lambda \mathbf {u} } to the left-hand side and factoring {\displaystyle \mathbf {u} } out,

{\displaystyle \left(\mathbf {A} -\lambda \mathbf {I} \right)\mathbf {u} =\mathbf {0} .}

Since {\displaystyle \mathbf {Q} } is non-singular, it is essential that {\displaystyle \mathbf {u} } is nonzero. Therefore,

{\displaystyle \det(\mathbf {A} -\lambda \mathbf {I} )=0.}

Thus

{\displaystyle (1-\lambda )(3-\lambda )=0,}

giving us the solutions of the eigenvalues for the matrix {\displaystyle \mathbf {A} } as {\displaystyle \lambda =1} or {\displaystyle \lambda =3}. Ordering the eigenvalues as 1,3, the resulting diagonal matrix from the eigendecomposition of {\displaystyle \mathbf {A} } is thus {\displaystyle \left[{\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\0&3\end{smallmatrix}}\right]}.

Putting the solutions back into the above simultaneous equations,

{\displaystyle {\begin{cases}{\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\1&3\end{bmatrix}}{\begin{bmatrix}a\\c\end{bmatrix}}=1{\begin{bmatrix}a\\c\end{bmatrix}}\\[1.2ex]{\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\1&3\end{bmatrix}}{\begin{bmatrix}b\\d\end{bmatrix}}=3{\begin{bmatrix}b\\d\end{bmatrix}}.\end{cases}}}

Solving the equations, we have

{\displaystyle a=-2c\quad {\text{and}}\quad b=0,\qquad c,d\in \mathbb {R} \setminus \{0\}.}

Thus the matrix {\displaystyle \mathbf {Q} } required for the eigendecomposition of {\displaystyle \mathbf {A} } is

{\displaystyle \mathbf {Q} ={\begin{bmatrix}-2c&0\\c&d\end{bmatrix}},\quad {\text{where}}\quad c,d\in \mathbb {R} \setminus \{0\}.}

Finally, the diagonalization of {\displaystyle \mathbf {A} } is:

{\displaystyle {\begin{bmatrix}-2c&0\\c&d\end{bmatrix}}^{-1}{\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\1&3\end{bmatrix}}{\begin{bmatrix}-2c&0\\c&d\end{bmatrix}}={\begin{bmatrix}1&0\\0&3\end{bmatrix}},\quad {\text{where}}\quad c,d\in \mathbb {R} \setminus \{0\}.}

Writing \mathbf u_1=(-2c,c)^T and \mathbf u_2=(0,d)^T, in this example, {\displaystyle \mathbf {u} _{1}} and {\displaystyle \mathbf {u} _{2}}, eigenvectors associated with distinct eigenvalues, cannot be orthogonal to each other. Indeed:

{\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}\langle \mathbf {u} _{1},\mathbf {u} _{2}\rangle =0&\iff -2c\!\times \!0+cd=0\\&\iff c=0\quad {\text{or}}\quad d=0\\&\iff \mathbf {u} _{1}=0\quad {\text{or}}\quad \mathbf {u} _{2}=0,\end{aligned}}}

which would make {\displaystyle \mathbf {Q} } singular.

Level justification

Solve the two eigenvalue equations and show every pair of nonzero eigenvectors has nonzero dot product. Ordinary two-dimensional linear algebra suffices.
