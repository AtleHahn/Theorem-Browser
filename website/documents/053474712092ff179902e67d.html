Statement

Let a,b,c,d\in\mathbb R^n, n\ge2, and equip \Lambda^2\mathbb R^n with its induced Euclidean inner product. Then (a\wedge b)\cdot(c\wedge d)=(a\cdot c)(b\cdot d)-(a\cdot d)(b\cdot c). For n=3, under the standard identification of bivectors with cross products, this is (a\times b)\cdot(c\times d)=(a\cdot c)(b\cdot d)-(a\cdot d)(b\cdot c).

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Linear algebra supplies bases, inner products and determinants. The second exterior power can be learned concretely as the space with orthonormal basis e_i wedge e_j for i<j, with a wedge b having coordinates a_i b_j-a_j b_i. This single finite-dimensional construction, its induced dot product and the three-dimensional cross-product identification fit about 30 minutes of preparation; general exterior algebra is unnecessary.

Proof 1: Use the induced exterior-product inner product

The inner product on the exterior algebra of a vector space is defined on wedge-decomposable elements as the Gram determinant of their components. For a,b,c,d\in\mathbb R^n, this is

(a\wedge b)\cdot(c\wedge d)= \det\begin{pmatrix}a\cdot c&a\cdot d\\b\cdot c&b\cdot d\end{pmatrix}.

It may also be written as a formula giving the dot product of two wedge products, as

(a\wedge b)\cdot(c\wedge d)=(a\cdot c)(b\cdot d)-(a\cdot d)(b\cdot c),

which can be written as

(a\times b)\cdot(c\times d)=(a\cdot c)(b\cdot d)-(a\cdot d)(b\cdot c)

in the n=3 case.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Only the second exterior power of a finite Euclidean space is used. It can be understood concretely with orthonormal basis e_i wedge e_j (i<j) and coefficients a_i b_j-a_j b_i. This is a small extension of undergraduate linear algebra; the supplied determinant definition then gives the identity by a two-by-two expansion. General exterior algebra or differential forms are not needed.
