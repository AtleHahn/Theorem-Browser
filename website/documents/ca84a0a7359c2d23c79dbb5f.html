Philosophical significance
Auxiliary

Its philosophical relevance is auxiliary to the Banach–Tarski paradox. Decomposing the free group by initial letters supplies the algebraic pattern that the geometric proof transfers to free rotation orbits; further choice and geometric steps yield duplication of a ball. The group lemma itself does not establish a paradox about Euclidean volume.

Statement

Let F_2 be the free group on two generators. It contains two disjoint subsets, each equidecomposable with F_2 under left translations: each subset can be partitioned into finitely many pieces which, translated on the left by group elements, form a partition of F_2.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

A free group is described by words with adjacent inverse cancellations; a free product similarly alternates nonidentity factors. These concrete word models extend familiar group operations and make the stated subsets, rank bound or matrix-group freeness intelligible without geometric group theory.

Proof 1: Cancellation of initial letters

Let F_2 be the free group on generators a,b, represented by reduced words in a,a^{-1},b,b^{-1}, with identity the empty word e and multiplication by concatenation and cancellation.

The group {\displaystyle F_{2}} can be "paradoxically decomposed" as follows: Let S(a) be the subset of {\displaystyle F_{2}} consisting of all strings that start with a, and define S(a−1), S(b) and S(b−1) similarly. Clearly,

{\displaystyle F_{2}=\{e\}\cup S(a)\cup S(a^{-1})\cup S(b)\cup S(b^{-1})}

but also

{\displaystyle F_{2}=aS(a^{-1})\cup S(a),}

and

{\displaystyle F_{2}=bS(b^{-1})\cup S(b),}

where the notation aS(a−1) means take all the strings in S(a−1) and concatenate them on the left with a.

This is at the core of the proof. For example, there may be a string {\displaystyle aa^{-1}b} in the set {\displaystyle aS(a^{-1})} which, because of the rule that {\displaystyle a} must not appear next to {\displaystyle a^{-1}}, reduces to the string {\displaystyle b}. Similarly, {\displaystyle aS(a^{-1})} contains all the strings that start with {\displaystyle a^{-1}} (for example, the string {\displaystyle aa^{-1}a^{-1}} which reduces to {\displaystyle a^{-1}}). In this way, {\displaystyle aS(a^{-1})} contains all the strings that start with {\displaystyle b}, {\displaystyle b^{-1}} and {\displaystyle a^{-1}}, as well as the empty string {\displaystyle e}.

Thus S(a)\cup S(a^{-1}) and S(b)\cup S(b^{-1}) are disjoint subsets, each equidecomposable with F_2 by left translations, using two pieces. The identity is not part of either subset.
