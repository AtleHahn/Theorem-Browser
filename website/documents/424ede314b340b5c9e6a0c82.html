Statement

Let A\subset\mathbb R^n be open and let f:A\to\mathbb R^n be C^1. Suppose a\in A and Df(a) is invertible. There are open neighborhoods U of a and V of f(a) such that f:U\to V is bijective with a C^1 inverse g, and Dg(y)=(Df(g(y)))^{-1} for y\in V.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Understanding the statement involves Real multivariable calculus; Jacobians; Finite-dimensional linear algebra. These prerequisites fit undergraduate coursework or a brief extension of it. A bright US college student should be able to understand this statement after approximately 30 minutes of focused background research; this assesses understanding the claim, not its proof.

Proof 1: Successive approximation and the inverse derivative estimate — outline

To prove existence, it can be assumed after an affine transformation that {\displaystyle f(0)=0} and {\displaystyle f^{\prime }(0)=I}, so that the base point and its image are both 0.

By the mean value theorem for vector-valued functions, for a differentiable function {\displaystyle u:[0,1]\to \mathbb {R} ^{n}}, {\textstyle \|u(1)-u(0)\|\leq \sup _{0\leq t\leq 1}\|u^{\prime }(t)\|}. Setting {\displaystyle u(t)=f(x+t(x^{\prime }-x))-x-t(x^{\prime }-x)}, it follows that

{\displaystyle \|f(x)-f(x^{\prime })-x+x^{\prime }\|\leq \|x-x^{\prime }\|\,\sup _{0\leq t\leq 1}\|f^{\prime }(x+t(x^{\prime }-x))-I\|.}

Now choose \delta>0 with the ball B(0,\delta) in the domain, so that {\textstyle \|f'(x)-I\|<{1 \over 2}} for {\displaystyle \|x\|<\delta }. Suppose that {\displaystyle \|y\|<\delta /2} and define {\displaystyle x_{n}} inductively by {\displaystyle x_{0}=0} and {\displaystyle x_{n+1}=x_{n}+y-f(x_{n})}. The assumptions show that if {\displaystyle \|x\|,\,\,\|x^{\prime }\|<\delta } then

{\displaystyle \|f(x)-f(x^{\prime })-x+x^{\prime }\|\leq \|x-x^{\prime }\|/2}.

In particular {\displaystyle f(x)=f(x^{\prime })} implies {\displaystyle x=x^{\prime }}. In the inductive scheme,

\|x_{n+1}-x_n\|\leq2^{-n}\|y\|, \qquad \|x_n\|\leq2\|y\|<\delta.

Thus (x_n) is a Cauchy sequence tending to x, with \|x\|\leq2\|y\|<\delta. By construction f(x)=y as required. Set

U=B(0,\delta)\cap f^{-1}(B(0,\delta/2)), \qquad V=B(0,\delta/2).

Then f:U\to V is bijective.

To check that {\displaystyle g=f^{-1}} is C1, put x=g(y) and write {\displaystyle g(y+k)=x+h} so that {\displaystyle f(x+h)=f(x)+k}. By the inequalities above, {\displaystyle \|h-k\|\leq\|h\|/2} so that {\displaystyle \|h\|/2\leq\|k\|\leq2\|h\|}. On the other hand, if {\displaystyle A=f^{\prime }(x)}, then {\displaystyle \|A-I\|<1/2}. Using the geometric series for {\displaystyle B=I-A}, it follows that {\displaystyle \|A^{-1}\|<2}. But then

{\displaystyle {\|g(y+k)-g(y)-f^{\prime }(g(y))^{-1}k\| \over \|k\|}={\|h-f^{\prime }(x)^{-1}[f(x+h)-f(x)]\| \over \|k\|}\leq 4{\|f(x+h)-f(x)-f^{\prime }(x)h\| \over \|h\|}}

tends to 0 as {\displaystyle k} and {\displaystyle h} tend to 0, proving that {\displaystyle g} is C1 with {\displaystyle g^{\prime }(y)=f^{\prime }(g(y))^{-1}}.

The lower Lipschitz bound makes g continuous. Also, g' is the composition of g, f' and matrix inversion, so g' is continuous. Undoing the initial affine transformation completes the proof.

Proof 2: Minimize squared distance to the target — outline

After an affine transformation, assume that the base point and its image are 0 and that f'(0)=I. Choose r>0 such that the closed ball \overline B(0,r) lies in the domain and \|f'(x)-I\|<1/2 on a neighborhood of it. The mean value inequality gives

\|f(x)-f(x')-x+x'\|\leq\frac12\|x-x'\|.

In particular f is injective on B(0,r) and \frac12\|x\|\leq\|f(x)\|.

We shall directly show B(0,r/4)\subset f(B(0,r)). Given a point y in B(0,r/4), consider the function

P(x)=\|f(x)-y\|^2

defined on a neighborhood of \overline B(0,r). If P'(x)=0, then

0=P'(x)=2\bigl[f_1(x)-y_1,\ldots,f_n(x)-y_n\bigr]f'(x),

and so f(x)=y, since f'(x) is invertible.

Now, by the extreme value theorem, P attains a minimum at some point x_0 on the closed ball \overline B(0,r). This point lies in B(0,r): on the boundary,

\|f(x)-y\|\geq r/2-\|y\|>\|y\|,

whereas P(0)=\|y\|^2. Since P'(x_0)=0, f(x_0)=y, which proves the claimed inclusion. Therefore

f:U=B(0,r)\cap f^{-1}(B(0,r/4))\longrightarrow V=B(0,r/4)

is bijective.

We show the inverse g=f^{-1} is continuously differentiable. Let A=f'(x). For x=g(y), write g(y+k)=x+h, or y+k=f(x+h). By the earlier estimate, we have

\|h-k\|=\|f(x+h)-f(x)-h\|\leq\|h\|/2,

and so \|h\|/2\leq\|k\|. Since \|A-I\|<1/2, we have \|Ah\|\geq\|h\|/2. Thus the finite-dimensional square matrix A is invertible and \|A^{-1}\|\leq2. Now

\|g(y+k)-g(y)-A^{-1}k\| =\|h-A^{-1}(f(x+h)-f(x))\| \leq\|A^{-1}\|\,\|Ah-f(x+h)+f(x)\|.

As k\to0, we have h\to0 and \|h\|/\|k\| is bounded. Hence g is differentiable at y with derivative g'(y)=f'(g(y))^{-1}. Also, g' is the composition \iota\circ f'\circ g, where \iota:T\mapsto T^{-1}, so g' is continuous. Undo the initial affine transformation.

Proof 3: Apply the implicit function theorem to h(x)-x prime

In this proof write h=(h_1,\ldots,h_m) for the given map, with m=n, and b for its base point. Put a=h(b). Work on the open domain of h near b.

We can introduce new coordinates (x'_1,\ldots,x'_m) by supplying the continuously differentiable functions h_1,\ldots,h_m, using

x'_1=h_1(x_1,\ldots,x_m),\ldots,x'_m=h_m(x_1,\ldots,x_m).

Given coordinates (x'_1,\ldots,x'_m), can we go back and calculate the same point's original coordinates (x_1,\ldots,x_m)? The implicit function theorem provides an answer. The new and old coordinates are related by f=0, with

f(x'_1,\ldots,x'_m,x_1,\ldots,x_m) =(h_1(x_1,\ldots,x_m)-x'_1,\ldots,h_m(x_1,\ldots,x_m)-x'_m).

Now the Jacobian matrix of f at (a,b) is

Df(a,b)= \left[ \begin{array}{ccc|ccc} -1&\cdots&0&\frac{\partial h_1}{\partial x_1}(b)&\cdots&\frac{\partial h_1}{\partial x_m}(b)\\ \vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\ 0&\cdots&-1&\frac{\partial h_m}{\partial x_1}(b)&\cdots&\frac{\partial h_m}{\partial x_m}(b) \end{array} \right]=[-I_m\mid J],

where I_m denotes the m\times m identity matrix and J=Dh(b).

The implicit function theorem states that an invertible dependent-variable block gives a unique local C^1 graph of all zeros. Thus we can locally express (x_1,\ldots,x_m) as a function of (x'_1,\ldots,x'_m) if J is invertible. Demanding J is invertible is equivalent to \det J\neq0, so we can go back from the primed to the unprimed coordinates if the determinant of the Jacobian is nonzero. This gives the local inverse g.

The derivative formula from the implicit function theorem is minus the inverse dependent-variable block times the independent-variable block. Here it gives

Dg(x')=-[Dh(g(x'))]^{-1}(-I_m)=[Dh(g(x'))]^{-1}.
