Statement

Let I be a real interval and A:I\to M_n(\mathbb F), where \mathbb F=\mathbb R or \mathbb C, with continuous trace \operatorname{tr}A(t)=\sum_{i=1}^n a_{ii}(t). Let \Phi:I\to M_n(\mathbb F) be differentiable and satisfy \Phi\prime(t)=A(t)\Phi(t). Then for t,t_0\in I, \det\Phi(t)=\det\Phi(t_0)\exp\!\left(\int_{t_0}^{t}\operatorname{tr}A(s)\,ds\right).

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Understanding the statement involves real or complex matrices; matrix derivatives; determinants; trace; definite integrals; exponential function. These prerequisites fit undergraduate coursework or a brief extension of it. A bright US college student should be able to understand this statement after approximately 30 minutes of focused background research; this assesses understanding the claim, not its proof.

Proof 1: Differentiate determinant rows and use an integrating factor

Use x for the independent variable (denoted t in the statement), and omit it where convenient. By the Leibniz formula for determinants, the derivative of the determinant of Φ = (Φi, j)i, j ∈ {1,...,n} can be calculated by differentiating one row at a time and taking the sum, i.e.

{\displaystyle (\det \Phi )'=\sum _{i=1}^{n}\det {\begin{pmatrix}\Phi _{1,1}&\Phi _{1,2}&\cdots &\Phi _{1,n}\\\vdots &\vdots &&\vdots \\\Phi '_{i,1}&\Phi '_{i,2}&\cdots &\Phi '_{i,n}\\\vdots &\vdots &&\vdots \\\Phi _{n,1}&\Phi _{n,2}&\cdots &\Phi _{n,n}\end{pmatrix}}.}

Since the matrix-valued solution Φ satisfies the equation Φ' = AΦ, we have for every entry of the matrix Φ'

{\displaystyle \Phi '_{i,k}=\sum _{j=1}^{n}a_{i,j}\Phi _{j,k}\,,\qquad i,k\in \{1,\ldots ,n\},}

or for the entire row

{\displaystyle (\Phi '_{i,1},\dots ,\Phi '_{i,n})=\sum _{j=1}^{n}a_{i,j}(\Phi _{j,1},\ldots ,\Phi _{j,n}),\qquad i\in \{1,\ldots ,n\}.}

When we subtract from the i-th row the linear combination

{\displaystyle \sum _{\scriptstyle j=1 \atop \scriptstyle j\neq i}^{n}a_{i,j}(\Phi _{j,1},\ldots ,\Phi _{j,n}),}

of all the other rows, then the value of the determinant remains unchanged, hence

{\displaystyle \det {\begin{pmatrix}\Phi _{1,1}&\Phi _{1,2}&\cdots &\Phi _{1,n}\\\vdots &\vdots &&\vdots \\\Phi '_{i,1}&\Phi '_{i,2}&\cdots &\Phi '_{i,n}\\\vdots &\vdots &&\vdots \\\Phi _{n,1}&\Phi _{n,2}&\cdots &\Phi _{n,n}\end{pmatrix}}=\det {\begin{pmatrix}\Phi _{1,1}&\Phi _{1,2}&\cdots &\Phi _{1,n}\\\vdots &\vdots &&\vdots \\a_{i,i}\Phi _{i,1}&a_{i,i}\Phi _{i,2}&\cdots &a_{i,i}\Phi _{i,n}\\\vdots &\vdots &&\vdots \\\Phi _{n,1}&\Phi _{n,2}&\cdots &\Phi _{n,n}\end{pmatrix}}=a_{i,i}\det \Phi }

for every i ∈ {1, . . . , n} by the linearity of the determinant with respect to every row. Hence

{\displaystyle (\det \Phi )'=\sum _{i=1}^{n}a_{i,i}\det \Phi =\mathrm {tr} \,A\,\det \Phi }

by the row-derivative sum above and the definition of the trace. It remains to show that this representation of the derivative implies Liouville's formula.

Fix x0 ∈ I. Since the trace of A is assumed to be continuous function on I, it is bounded on every closed and bounded subinterval of I and therefore integrable, hence

{\displaystyle g(x):=\det \Phi (x)\exp \left(-\int _{x_{0}}^{x}\mathrm {tr} \,A(\xi )\,{\textrm {d}}\xi \right),\qquad x\in I,}

is a well defined function. Differentiating both sides, using the product rule, the chain rule, the derivative of the exponential function and the fundamental theorem of calculus, we obtain

{\displaystyle g'(x)={\bigl (}(\det \Phi (x))'-\det \Phi (x)\,\mathrm {tr} \,A(x){\bigr )}\exp \left(-\int _{x_{0}}^{x}\operatorname {tr} \,A(\xi )\,{\textrm {d}}\xi \right)=0,\qquad x\in I,}

due to the determinant differential identity just obtained. Therefore, g has to be constant on I, because otherwise we would obtain a contradiction to the mean value theorem (applied separately to the real and imaginary part in the complex-valued case). Since g(x0) = det Φ(x0), Liouville's formula follows by solving the definition of g for det Φ(x).

Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Differentiate the determinant row by row and use Phi′=A Phi. Multilinearity removes the off-diagonal contributions, giving (det Phi)′=tr(A)det(Phi). A scalar integrating factor then proves the formula; the mean value theorem applied to real and imaginary parts makes the zero-derivative conclusion rigorous. The proof needs only calculus and linear algebra, and never divides by det(Phi).

Statement used:

Application in proof:

Differentiate the integrating-factor product and conclude it is constant; in the complex case apply the zero-derivative fact to both components.
