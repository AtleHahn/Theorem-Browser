Statement

Let a_n\ge0 for n\ge0 and suppose \sum_{n=0}^\infty a_nx^n\sim(1-x)^{-1} as x\uparrow1. Put g(t)=1/t for 1/e<t<1 and g(t)=0 otherwise on [0,1]. Then \lim_{x\uparrow1}(1-x)\sum_{n=0}^\infty a_nx^ng(x^n)=1.

A bright US college student should be able to understand this statement after approximately 30 minutes of focused background research; this assesses understanding the claim, not its proof.

Proof 1: Polynomial approximation sandwich — outline

Consider the functions {\displaystyle g} such that

{\displaystyle \lim _{x\to 1}(1-x)\sum a_{n}x^{n}g(x^{n})=\int _{0}^{1}g(t)dt}

An easy calculation shows that all monomials {\displaystyle g(x)=x^{k}} have this property, and therefore so do all polynomials {\displaystyle g}. This can be extended to a function {\displaystyle g} with simple (step) discontinuities by approximating it by polynomials from above and below (using the Weierstrass approximation theorem and a little extra fudging) and using the fact that the coefficients {\displaystyle a_{n}} are nonnegative. In particular the function given by {\displaystyle g(t)=1/t} if {\displaystyle 1/e<t<1} and {\displaystyle 0} otherwise has this property. The integral of this g is \int_{1/e}^1dt/t=1. The source leaves the upper and lower polynomial approximation construction implicit.

Level justification

Verify monomials from the power-series asymptotic, extend linearly to polynomials and sandwich the piecewise continuous cutoff using continuous majorants and minorants with close integrals. Undergraduate real analysis and uniform polynomial approximation suffice; the omitted construction is a Riemann-integral approximation step.
