Statement

Fix 0<p<1 and put q=1-p. Let B_1,B_2,\ldots be independent variables with P(B_j=1)=p and P(B_j=0)=q, and put X_n=\sum_{j=1}^nB_j. Then (X_n-np)/\sqrt{npq} converges in distribution to the standard normal law, whose density is (2\pi)^{-1/2}e^{-t^2/2}.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Finite Bernoulli sums have explicit probabilities, and the normal density is supplied. Convergence in distribution means convergence of cumulative probabilities at continuity points, a single limit-based extension of familiar calculus. Understanding this special limit claim does not require a general stochastic-process theory.

Proof 1: Identify the success fraction with a Bernoulli sample mean

Use the iid central limit theorem: for independent identically distributed variables with mean \mu and variance \sigma^2>0, the standardized sample mean \sqrt n(\overline X_n-\mu)/\sigma converges in distribution to the standard normal law.

It is a special case of the central limit theorem because a Bernoulli process can be thought of as the drawing of independent random variables from a two-point discrete distribution with non-zero probability only for values 0 and 1. In this case, the binomial distribution models the number of successes (i.e., the number of 1s), whereas the central limit theorem states that, given sufficiently large n, the distribution of the sample means will be approximately normal. However, because in this case the fraction of successes (i.e., the number of 1s divided by the number of trials, n) is equal to the sample mean, the distribution of the fractions of successes (described by the binomial distribution divided by the constant n) and the distribution of the sample means (approximately normal with large n due to the central limit theorem) are equivalent.

Here each Bernoulli variable has mean p and variance pq, so the standardized sample mean is (X_n-np)/\sqrt{npq}.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

The argument applies the stated iid central limit theorem to Bernoulli variables, computes their mean and variance, and simplifies the standardized average. Undergraduate probability covers these quantities and the normal law; convergence in distribution here means convergence of distribution functions at the continuous normal limit, a modest additional definition. No measure-theoretic construction or proof of the CLT is used.
