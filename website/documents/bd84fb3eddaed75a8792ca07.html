Simpler proof assessment

Likely alternative prerequisite level: High school only; lowest recorded proof level: Undergraduate level. Translate a vertex to zero. The first edge is a primitive integer vector, otherwise its segment contains another lattice point. Bezout's integer identity gives an invertible integer coordinate change sending it to (1,0), preserving lattice points and area. Write the second edge (a,b). If |b|>1 a horizontal integer-height slice of the fundamental parallelogram contains a lattice point other than a vertex; that point or its reflection in the parallelogram center lies in the triangle. Hence |b|=1 and the area is 1/2. Only two-coordinate arithmetic, the Euclidean algorithm and finite geometry are needed.

Statement

A nondegenerate triangle with integer vertices and no other integer lattice points on its boundary or in its interior has area 1/2.

Level
High school only
Level justification

The statement uses integer coordinates, polygon area and finite counts.

Mathematical Field

Review notes

Proof 1: Count triangular tiles per lattice point — outline

The proof uses the fact that all triangles tile the plane, with adjacent triangles rotated by 180° from each other around their shared edge. For tilings by a triangle with three integer vertices and no other integer points, each point of the integer grid is a vertex of six tiles. Because the number of triangles per grid point (six) is twice the number of grid points per triangle (three), the triangles are twice as dense in the plane as the grid points. Any scaled region of the plane contains twice as many triangles (in the limit as the scale factor goes to infinity) as the number of grid points it contains. Therefore, each triangle has area {\displaystyle {\tfrac {1}{2}}}, as needed for the proof.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

The half-turn tiling gives six tile incidences per lattice point and three vertices per tile; the proof then passes to large-region density limits. Elementary geometry plus a real-analysis boundary-to-area limiting argument suffices, with no algebraic or measure-theoretic lattice theorem needed.
