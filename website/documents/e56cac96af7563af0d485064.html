Statement

On the unit sphere fix distinct nonantipodal points A,B, joined by the minor great-circle arc. Let C,X lie in the same open hemisphere bounded by the great circle AB. Triangles use minor arcs and their ordinary enclosed spherical areas. Write A^*=-A, B^*=-B. Then ABC and ABX have equal area if and only if X lies on the arc through C of the small circle determined by A^*,B^*,C.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Three-dimensional geometry and calculus supply spheres, planes, angles and surface area. Great circles are central plane sections and small circles are other plane sections; a lune is the region between two semicircles. The explicit antipodes and arcs specify the configurations without abstract differential geometry.

Proof 1: Isosceles subdivision and Girard’s theorem — outline

Let l be the small circle through A^*,B^*,C, and let P be its spherical center. Split \triangle A^*B^*C into three isosceles triangles with common apex P.

Let the base angles of the isosceles triangles {\displaystyle \triangle B^{*~\!\!}CP}, {\displaystyle \triangle CA^{*~\!\!}P}, and {\displaystyle \triangle A^{*~\!\!}B^{*~\!\!}P} be respectively {\displaystyle \alpha ,} {\displaystyle \beta ,} and {\displaystyle \delta .} (In some cases {\displaystyle P} is outside {\displaystyle \triangle A^{*~\!\!}B^{*~\!\!}C}; then one of the quantities {\displaystyle \alpha ,\beta ,\delta } will be negative.) We can compute the internal angles of {\displaystyle \triangle ABC} in terms of these angles: {\displaystyle \angle A=\pi -\beta -\delta } (the supplement of {\displaystyle \angle A^{*~\!\!}}) and likewise {\displaystyle \angle B=\pi -\alpha -\delta ,} and finally {\displaystyle \angle C=\alpha +\beta .}

By Girard's theorem the spherical excess of {\displaystyle \triangle ABC} is

{\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}\varepsilon &=\angle A+\angle B+\angle C-\pi \\[3mu]&=(\pi -\beta -\delta )+(\pi -\alpha -\delta )+(\alpha +\beta )-\pi \\[3mu]&=\pi -2\delta .\end{aligned}}}

If base {\displaystyle AB} is fixed, for any third vertex {\displaystyle C} falling on the same arc of Lexell's circle, the point {\displaystyle P} and therefore the quantity {\displaystyle \delta } will not change, so the excess {\displaystyle \varepsilon } of {\displaystyle \triangle ABC,} which depends only on {\displaystyle \delta ,} will likewise be constant. And vice versa: if {\displaystyle \varepsilon } remains constant when the point {\displaystyle C} is changed, then so must {\displaystyle \delta } be, and therefore {\displaystyle P} must be fixed, so {\displaystyle C} must remain on Lexell's circle.

Proof 2: Polar perimeter and equal tangent arcs — outline

Every spherical triangle has a dual, its polar triangle; if triangle {\displaystyle \triangle A'B'C'} is the polar triangle of {\displaystyle \triangle ABC} then the vertices {\displaystyle A'\!,B'\!,C'} are the poles of the respective sides {\displaystyle BC,CA,AB,} and vice versa, the vertices {\displaystyle A,B,C} are the poles of the sides {\displaystyle B'C'\!,C'A'\!,A'B'\!.} The polar duality exchanges the sides (central angles) and external angles (dihedral angles) between the two triangles.

Because each side of the dual triangle is the supplement of an internal angle of the original triangle, the spherical excess {\displaystyle \varepsilon } of {\displaystyle \triangle ABC} is a function of the perimeter {\displaystyle p'} of the dual triangle {\displaystyle \triangle A'B'C'}:

{\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}\varepsilon &=\angle A+\angle B+\angle C-\pi \\[3mu]&={\bigl (}\pi -|B'C'|{\bigr )}+{\bigl (}\pi -|C'A'|{\bigr )}+{\bigl (}\pi -|A'B'|{\bigr )}-\pi \\[3mu]&=2\pi -p',\end{aligned}}}

where the notation {\displaystyle |PQ|} means the angular length of the great-circle arc {\displaystyle PQ.}

Under polar duality the Lexell circle {\displaystyle l} circumscribing {\displaystyle \triangle A^{*~\!\!}B^{*~\!\!}C} becomes an excircle {\displaystyle l'} of {\displaystyle \triangle A'B'C'} (incircle of a colunar triangle) externally tangent to side {\displaystyle A'B'.}

Let T_B,T_A be the contacts of l^\prime on the extensions of C^\prime A^\prime,C^\prime B^\prime, and T_C its contact on A^\prime B^\prime.

If vertex {\displaystyle C} is moved along the same arc of {\displaystyle l,} the side {\displaystyle A'B'} changes but always remains tangent to the same circle {\displaystyle l'.} Because the arcs from each vertex to either adjacent touch point of an incircle or excircle are congruent, {\displaystyle A'T_{B}\cong A'T_{C}} and {\displaystyle B'T_{A}\cong B'T_{C}}, the perimeter {\displaystyle p'} is

{\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}p'&=|A'B'|+|B'C'|+|C'A'|\\[3mu]&={\bigl (}|A'T_{C}|+|B'T_{C}|{\bigr )}+|C'B'|+|C'A'|\\[3mu]&={\bigl (}|C'A'|+|A'T_{B}|{\bigr )}+{\bigl (}|C'B'|+|B'T_{A}|{\bigr )}\\[3mu]&=|C'T_{B}|+|C'T_{A}|,\end{aligned}}}

which remains constant, depending only on the circle {\displaystyle l'} but not on the changing side {\displaystyle A'B'.} Conversely, if the point {\displaystyle C} moves off of {\displaystyle l,} the associated excircle {\displaystyle l'} will change in size, moving the points {\displaystyle T_{A}} and {\displaystyle T_{B}} both toward or both away from {\displaystyle C'^{*~\!\!}} and changing the perimeter {\displaystyle p'} of {\displaystyle \triangle A'B'C'\!} and thus changing {\displaystyle \varepsilon .}

The locus of points {\displaystyle C} for which {\displaystyle \varepsilon } is constant is therefore {\displaystyle l.}
