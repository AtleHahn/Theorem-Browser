Statement

If real functions f,g are differentiable at a and defined near a, with g(a)\ne0, then (f/g)^{\prime}(a)=[f^{\prime}(a)g(a)-f(a)g^{\prime}(a)]/g(a)^2.

Level

Level justification

Understanding the statement involves real derivatives; quotients with nonzero denominators. These prerequisites fit undergraduate coursework or a brief extension of it. A bright US college student should be able to understand this statement after approximately 30 minutes of focused background research; this assesses understanding the claim, not its proof.

Proof 1: Apply the product rule and reciprocal derivative

Apply the following calculation at x=a; continuity of g and g(a)\ne0 ensure that the reciprocal is defined nearby.

Write the function f(x)/g(x) as the product f(x) · 1/g(x). First apply the product rule:

{\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}{\frac {d}{dx}}\left({\frac {f(x)}{g(x)}}\right)&={\frac {d}{dx}}\left(f(x)\cdot {\frac {1}{g(x)}}\right)\\&=f'(x)\cdot {\frac {1}{g(x)}}+f(x)\cdot {\frac {d}{dx}}\left({\frac {1}{g(x)}}\right).\end{aligned}}}

To compute the derivative of 1/g(x), notice that it is the composite of g with the reciprocal function, that is, the function that sends x to 1/x. The derivative of the reciprocal function is {\displaystyle -1/x^{2}\!}. By applying the chain rule, the last expression becomes:

{\displaystyle f'(x)\cdot {\frac {1}{g(x)}}+f(x)\cdot \left(-{\frac {1}{g(x)^{2}}}\cdot g'(x)\right)={\frac {f'(x)g(x)-f(x)g'(x)}{g(x)^{2}}},}

which is the usual formula for the quotient rule.

Proof 2: Combine the difference quotients over a common denominator

Let ⁠{\displaystyle \textstyle h(a)={\frac {f(a)}{g(a)}}}⁠. Continuity of g and g(a)\ne0 make h defined near a. Applying the definition of the derivative and properties of limits gives the following proof, with the term {\displaystyle f(a)g(a)} added and subtracted to allow splitting and factoring in subsequent steps without affecting the value:

{\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}h'(a)&=\lim _{k\to 0}{\frac {h(a+k)-h(a)}{k}}\\&=\lim _{k\to 0}{\frac {{\frac {f(a+k)}{g(a+k)}}-{\frac {f(a)}{g(a)}}}{k}}\\&=\lim _{k\to 0}{\frac {f(a+k)g(a)-f(a)g(a+k)}{k\cdot g(a)g(a+k)}}\\&=\lim _{k\to 0}{\frac {f(a+k)g(a)-f(a)g(a+k)}{k}}\cdot \lim _{k\to 0}{\frac {1}{g(a)g(a+k)}}\\&=\lim _{k\to 0}\left[{\frac {f(a+k)g(a)-f(a)g(a)+f(a)g(a)-f(a)g(a+k)}{k}}\right]\cdot {\frac {1}{[g(a)]^{2}}}\\&=\left[\lim _{k\to 0}{\frac {f(a+k)g(a)-f(a)g(a)}{k}}-\lim _{k\to 0}{\frac {f(a)g(a+k)-f(a)g(a)}{k}}\right]\cdot {\frac {1}{[g(a)]^{2}}}\\&=\left[\lim _{k\to 0}{\frac {f(a+k)-f(a)}{k}}\cdot g(a)-f(a)\cdot \lim _{k\to 0}{\frac {g(a+k)-g(a)}{k}}\right]\cdot {\frac {1}{[g(a)]^{2}}}\\&={\frac {f'(a)g(a)-f(a)g'(a)}{[g(a)]^{2}}}.\end{aligned}}}

The limit evaluation {\textstyle \lim _{k\to 0}{\frac {1}{g(a+k)g(a)}}={\frac {1}{[g(a)]^{2}}}} is justified by the differentiability of g at a, implying continuity, which can be expressed as ⁠{\displaystyle \textstyle \lim _{k\to 0}g(a+k)=g(a)}⁠.
