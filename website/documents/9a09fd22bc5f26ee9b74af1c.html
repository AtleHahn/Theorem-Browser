Statement

Define f(0)=0 and f(z)=z^5/|z|^4 for nonzero z\in\mathbb C. The function is continuous on \mathbb C and satisfies f_x(0)+if_y(0)=0, but its complex derivative does not exist at any point of \mathbb C. Here the complex derivative at z_0 means the limit \lim_{z\to z_0}(f(z)-f(z_0))/(z-z_0).

Proof 1: Check directional quotients at zero and the antiholomorphic derivative elsewhere — outline

For z\ne0, |f(z)|=|z|, so the formula, together with f(0)=0, is continuous at zero; away from zero continuity follows from the quotient formula. On the axes, f(t)=t and f(it)=it, so f_x(0)=1 and f_y(0)=i, giving f_x(0)+if_y(0)=0. Yet f(z)/z=z^4/|z|^4 equals 1 along the real axis and -1 along z=t e^{i\pi/4}. The complex derivative at zero therefore does not exist. For z\ne0, rewrite f(z)=z^3/\bar z^2. It is real-smooth there, and its Wirtinger derivative is \partial f/\partial\bar z=-2z^3/\bar z^3\ne0. The Cauchy–Riemann criterion for a real-differentiable complex function therefore rules out a complex derivative at every nonzero point as well.
