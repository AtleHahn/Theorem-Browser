Statement

For a real sequence (a_n)_{n\ge1}, if the series \sum_{n=1}^\infty a_n converges, then a_n\to0. Equivalently, failure of the terms to tend to zero implies divergence.

Proof 1: Difference of consecutive partial sums

If s_n=\sum_{k=1}^n a_k are the partial sums of the series, then the assumption that the series converges means that

\lim_{n\to\infty}s_n=L

for some real number L. Then

\lim_{n\to\infty}a_n =\lim_{n\to\infty}(s_n-s_{n-1}) =\lim_{n\to\infty}s_n-\lim_{n\to\infty}s_{n-1} =L-L=0.
Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Express a term as the difference of consecutive partial sums and subtract their common limit. Standard undergraduate series theory suffices.

Proof 2: One-term tail in the Cauchy condition

Assuming that the series converges implies that its partial sums are Cauchy: for every \varepsilon>0 there is a number N such that

|a_{n+1}+a_{n+2}+\cdots+a_{n+p}|<\varepsilon

holds for all n>N and p\geq1. Setting p=1 recovers the claim

\lim_{n\to\infty}a_n=0.
