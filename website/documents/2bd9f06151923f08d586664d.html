Statement

Let \Omega be a set, let (\Omega',\mathcal A') be a measurable space, and let T:\Omega\to\Omega' be any function. Define

\sigma(T)=\{T^{-1}(B):B\in\mathcal A'\}.

A function f:\Omega\to[0,1] is measurable with respect to \sigma(T) and the Borel sets of [0,1] if and only if there is an \mathcal A'-measurable function g:\Omega'\to[0,1] such that

f=g\circ T.

Equivalently, such a factorization exists if and only if \sigma(f)\subseteq\sigma(T), where \sigma(f)=\{f^{-1}(B):B\text{ is Borel in }[0,1]\}.

A sigma-algebra is a family of subsets containing the whole space and closed under complements and countable unions. Borel sets form the sigma-algebra generated by open sets. Measurability means that inverse images of measurable sets are measurable.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Undergraduate analysis and point-set topology supply inverse images, countable operations and open sets. Learning sigma-algebras, Borel generation and measurability is a closely related extension of that background within about 30 minutes; no measures, integration or conditional expectation are needed.

Proof 1: Indicators, simple functions and a measurable supremum

If g is measurable, then g\circ T is measurable for \sigma(T), since T is measurable by the definition of \sigma(T).

Conversely, suppose f is measurable. For every A\in\sigma(T) choose B\in\mathcal A' with A=T^{-1}(B). Then \mathbf1_A=\mathbf1_B\circ T, where \mathbf1_A is 1 on A and 0 elsewhere. Taking finite nonnegative linear combinations gives a factorization s=h\circ T for each nonnegative simple measurable function s; a simple function has finitely many values.

Use the standard simple-function approximation theorem to choose simple measurable functions 0\leq f_n\leq1 increasing pointwise to f. Factor each as f_n=h_n\circ T, with h_n\geq0 measurable. Set g_n=\min\{1,h_n\}. These functions are measurable, take values in [0,1], and still satisfy f_n=g_n\circ T.

The function g(y)=\sup_{n\geq1}g_n(y) is measurable and takes values in [0,1]. Here we use the standard closure of measurable functions under countable suprema. On the image of T, the values g_n increase, and for every x\in\Omega,

g(T(x))=\sup_n f_n(x)=f(x).

This proves the factorization. The equivalent sigma-algebra formulation follows directly from the definition of measurability.

The range restriction g_n=\min\{1,h_n\} makes explicit a routine detail left implicit in the source: arbitrary simple-function factors need not already be bounded by 1 outside T(\Omega).
