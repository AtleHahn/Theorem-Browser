Statement

Let r>1 be irrational and put s=r/(r-1), so 1/r+1/s=1. For u>1 write \mathcal B_u=\{\lfloor nu\rfloor:n=1,2,\ldots\}, where \lfloor x\rfloor is the greatest integer at most x. Every positive integer belongs to exactly one of \mathcal B_r and \mathcal B_s.

Proof 1: Rank the merged reciprocal multiples

Given {\displaystyle r>1\,,} let {\displaystyle s=r/(r-1)}. We must show that every positive integer lies in one and only one of the two sequences {\displaystyle {\mathcal {B}}_{r}} and {\displaystyle {\mathcal {B}}_{s}}. We shall do so by considering the ordinal positions occupied by all the fractions {\displaystyle j/r} and {\displaystyle k/s} when they are jointly listed in nondecreasing order for positive integers j and k.

To see that no two of the numbers can occupy the same position (as a single number), suppose to the contrary that {\displaystyle j/r=k/s} for some j and k. Then {\displaystyle r/s} = {\displaystyle j/k}, a rational number, but also, {\displaystyle r/s=r(1-1/r)=r-1,} not a rational number. Therefore, no two of the numbers occupy the same position.

For any {\displaystyle j/r}, there are {\displaystyle j} positive integers {\displaystyle i} such that {\displaystyle i/r\leq j/r} and {\displaystyle \lfloor js/r\rfloor } positive integers {\displaystyle k} such that {\displaystyle k/s\leq j/r}, so that the position of {\displaystyle j/r} in the list is {\displaystyle j+\lfloor js/r\rfloor }. The equation {\displaystyle 1/r+1/s=1} implies

{\displaystyle j+\lfloor js/r\rfloor =j+\lfloor j(s-1)\rfloor =\lfloor js\rfloor .}

Likewise, the position of {\displaystyle k/s} in the list is {\displaystyle \lfloor kr\rfloor }.

Conclusion: every positive integer (that is, every position in the list) is of the form {\displaystyle \lfloor nr\rfloor } or of the form {\displaystyle \lfloor ns\rfloor }, but not both.

Level
High school only
Level justification

The proof merges two increasing lists of positive multiples, excludes equal entries by irrationality and counts each entry’s finite position using floors. Only finite counting below a given value, algebra and irrational numbers are needed; ordinal positions here mean ordinary positive-integer ranks, not transfinite ordinals. Every bounded interval contains only finitely many entries, so the increasing merged list is well-defined.

Statement used:

For positive a and y, the number of positive integers j with ja<=y is floor(y/a); floor(m+x)=m+floor(x) for integer m. Two increasing arithmetic lists with finitely many entries below each bound can be merged and ranked by these counts.

Application in proof:

Compute the ranks j+floor(js/r)=floor(js) and their counterparts.

Proof 2: Exclude collisions and gaps

Both r and s are irrational and greater than one.

Collisions: Suppose that, contrary to the theorem, there are integers j > 0 and k and m such that

{\displaystyle j=\left\lfloor {k\cdot r}\right\rfloor =\left\lfloor {m\cdot s}\right\rfloor \,.}

This is equivalent to the inequalities

{\displaystyle j\leq k\cdot r<j+1{\text{ and }}j\leq m\cdot s<j+1.}

For non-zero j, the irrationality of r and s is incompatible with equality, so

{\displaystyle j<k\cdot r<j+1{\text{ and }}j<m\cdot s<j+1,}

which leads to

{\displaystyle {j \over r}<k<{j+1 \over r}{\text{ and }}{j \over s}<m<{j+1 \over s}.}

Adding these together and using the hypothesis, we get

{\displaystyle j<k+m<j+1}

which is impossible (one cannot have an integer between two adjacent integers). Thus the supposition must be false.

Gaps: If a positive integer j is absent from both sequences, take nonnegative integers k=\lfloor j/r\rfloor and m=\lfloor j/s\rfloor. Then

{\displaystyle k\cdot r<j{\text{ and }}j+1\leq (k+1)\cdot r{\text{ and }}m\cdot s<j{\text{ and }}j+1\leq (m+1)\cdot s\,.}

Since j + 1 is non-zero and r and s are irrational, we can exclude equality, so

{\displaystyle k\cdot r<j{\text{ and }}j+1<(k+1)\cdot r{\text{ and }}m\cdot s<j{\text{ and }}j+1<(m+1)\cdot s.}

Then we get

{\displaystyle k<{j \over r}{\text{ and }}{j+1 \over r}<k+1{\text{ and }}m<{j \over s}{\text{ and }}{j+1 \over s}<m+1}

Adding corresponding inequalities, we get

{\displaystyle k+m<j{\text{ and }}j+1<k+m+2}{\displaystyle k+m<j<k+m+1}

which is also impossible. Thus the supposition is false.

Level justification

For a collision, divide the two strict floor inequalities and add to place an integer strictly between j and j+1. For a gap, use the preceding indices floor(j/r) and floor(j/s), then add the next-index inequalities to obtain another impossible integer interval. The zero values of preceding indices are allowed. All reasoning is elementary floor arithmetic and irrationality.
