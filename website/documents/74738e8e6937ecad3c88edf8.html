Statement

Assuming the axiom of choice, the unit sphere S^2\subset\mathbb R^3 admits a finite partition into two subsets, each of which is equidecomposable with S^2 by rotations. Here equidecomposability means the existence of finite partitions into corresponding rotated pieces.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Sphere rotations and finite partitions are elementary geometry and set theory. Equidecomposability is explicitly defined by rotated pieces, and choice is an explicit assumption. No measure or amenability theory is required to understand the existence claim.

Proof 1: Free orbits and countable-set absorption (outline)

Free rotation subgroup (external prerequisite)

There is a subgroup H=\langle a,b\rangle of SO(3) freely generated by two rotations. This existence result is used as a prerequisite here; its arithmetic proof is not supplied locally.

Orbit decomposition

Since any rotation of S^2 other than the identity has exactly two fixed points, and since H, which is isomorphic to the free group on two generators, is countable, there are countably many points of S^2 fixed by some nonidentity rotation in H. Denote this set of fixed points by D.

Identify H with reduced words on a,b, let S(c) denote words beginning with c, and let \langle a\rangle=\{a^n:n\in\mathbb Z\}.

The complement Y=S^2\setminus D is partitioned into orbits by the action of our group H: two points belong to the same orbit if and only if there is a rotation in H which moves the first point into the second. The axiom of choice can be used to pick exactly one point from every orbit; collect these points into a set M. The action of H on a given orbit is free and transitive and so each orbit can be identified with H. In other words, every point in Y can be reached in exactly one way by applying the proper rotation from H to the proper element from M. Because of this, the paradoxical decomposition of H yields a paradoxical decomposition of Y into four pieces A1, A2, A3, A4 as follows:

{\displaystyle A_{1}=(S(a)\cup \langle a\rangle )M}{\displaystyle A_{2}=(S(a^{-1})\setminus \langle a\rangle )M}{\displaystyle \displaystyle A_{3}=S(b)M}{\displaystyle \displaystyle A_{4}=S(b^{-1})M}

where we define

{\displaystyle S(a)M=\{s(x)|s\in S(a),x\in M\}}

and likewise for the other sets.

The complement Y has now been divided into four sets and when two of these are rotated, the result is double what was had before:

{\displaystyle aA_{2}=A_{2}\cup A_{3}\cup A_{4}}{\displaystyle bA_{4}=A_{1}\cup A_{2}\cup A_{4}}

The unions in these two relations are disjoint. Consequently A_1\sqcup aA_2=Y and A_3\sqcup bA_4=Y, giving two copies of Y.

Restore the countable exceptional set

Let λ be some line through the origin that does not intersect any point in D. This is possible since D is countable. Let J be the set of angles, α, such that for some positive integer n, and some P in D, r(nα)P is also in D, where r(nα) is a rotation about λ of nα. Then J is countable. So there exists an angle θ not in J. Let ρ be the rotation about λ by θ. By this choice, ρn(D) is disjoint from D for every positive integer n, and for natural m<n, ρn(D) is disjoint from ρm(D). Let E be the disjoint union of ρn(D) over n = 0, 1, 2, ... . Then ρ(E) is the disjoint union of ρn(D) over n ≥ 1 and therefore ρ(E) = E − D. Now we can conclude

S2 = E ∪ (S2 − E) ~ ρ(E) ∪ (S2 − E) = (E − D) ∪ (S2 − E) = S2 − D, where ~ denotes "is equidecomposable to".

The two pieces are E and S^2\setminus E, moved by \rho and the identity respectively; empty pieces may be omitted.

Thus S^2\sim Y, and each of the two reconstructed copies of Y can also be equidecomposed with S^2. Equidecomposability is transitive: compose two finite piecewise isometries by subdividing their pieces, using at most the product of the two piece counts. This produces the two copies of the sphere.
