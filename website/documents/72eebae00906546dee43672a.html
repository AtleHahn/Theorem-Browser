Statement

Let \varphi:[-1,1]\to\mathbb C have an expansion with complex coefficients \varphi(y)=\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}a_j\cos((2j+1)\pi y/2) on [-1,1]. Fix an integer k\ge0, and assume the expansion may be integrated term by term after multiplication by \cos((2k+1)\pi y/2). Then a_k=\int_{-1}^{1}\varphi(y)\cos((2k+1)\pi y/2)\,dy.

Proof 1: Integrate against the selected cosine — outline

Write the expansion as

\varphi(y)=a_0\cos\frac{\pi y}{2} +a_1\cos\frac{3\pi y}{2} +a_2\cos\frac{5\pi y}{2}+\cdots.

Multiplying both sides by \cos((2k+1)\pi y/2) and then integrating from y=-1 to y=1 yields the coefficient. Indeed, the integral

\begin{aligned} &\int_{-1}^{1}\varphi(y)\cos\frac{(2k+1)\pi y}{2}\,dy\\ &=\int_{-1}^{1}\left( a_0\cos\frac{\pi y}{2}\cos\frac{(2k+1)\pi y}{2} +a_1\cos\frac{3\pi y}{2}\cos\frac{(2k+1)\pi y}{2} +\cdots\right)\,dy \end{aligned}

can be carried out term by term by hypothesis. All terms involving \cos((2j+1)\pi y/2)\cos((2k+1)\pi y/2) for j\ne k vanish when integrated from -1 to 1, leaving only the kth term. Its cosine-product integral is 1, so the surviving term is a_k. Thus

a_k=\int_{-1}^{1}\varphi(y)\cos\frac{(2k+1)\pi y}{2}\,dy.
