Statement

Let T=\{0,1\}^{\mathbb N_{>0}} and f_2(t)=\sum_{k\ge1}t(k)2^{-k}. Enumerate the dyadic numbers in (0,1) as (r_n)_{n\ge1}, in increasing denominator order and then increasing odd numerator: 1/2,1/4,3/4,1/8,3/8,5/8,7/8,\ldots. Enumerate the eventually constant binary sequences as (s_n)_{n\ge1} by putting first 000\ldots,111\ldots, then, for each r_n in that order, its terminating expansion followed by its eventually-1 expansion. Define g:T\to(0,1) by g(s_n)=r_n for every n\ge1, and g(t)=f_2(t) on all remaining strings. Then g is a bijection.

Proof 1: Reassign the eventually constant strings (outline)

The function f 2(t) = 0.t2 will be modified to produce a bijection from T to (0, 1). Because some numbers have two binary expansions, f 2(t) is not even injective. For example, f 2(1000...) = 0.1000...2 = 1/2 and f 2(0111...) = 0.0111...2 = 1/4 + 1/8 + 1/16 + ... = 1/2, so both 1000... and 0111... map to the same number, 1/2.

To modify f2 (t), observe that it is a bijection except for a countably infinite subset of (0, 1) and a countably infinite subset of T. It is not a bijection for the numbers in (0, 1) that have two binary expansions. These are called dyadic numbers and have the form m / 2n where m is an odd integer and n is a natural number. Put these numbers in the sequence: r = (1/2, 1/4, 3/4, 1/8, 3/8, 5/8, 7/8, ...). Also, f2 (t) is not a bijection to (0, 1) for the strings in T appearing after the binary point in the binary expansions of 0, 1, and the numbers in sequence r. Put these eventually-constant strings in the sequence: s = (000..., 111..., 1000..., 0111..., 01000..., 00111..., 11000..., 10111..., ...). Define the bijection g(t) from T to (0, 1): If t is the nth string in sequence s, let g(t) be the nth number in sequence r ; otherwise, g(t) = 0.t2.
