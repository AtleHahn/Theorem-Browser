Statement

A lattice has meet a\wedge b and join a\vee b, the greatest lower bound and least upper bound. In a lattice with bottom 0, a nonzero element x is join-irreducible if x=y\vee z implies x=y or x=z, and join-prime if x\le y\vee z implies x\le y or x\le z.

Let L be a finite distributive lattice and let P be its join-irreducible elements with their inherited order. Then L is isomorphic to the lattice J(P) of lower subsets of P, via

x\longmapsto\{p\in P:p\le x\},\qquad S\longmapsto\bigvee S.

Here a lower subset contains all predecessors of its elements, and \bigvee\varnothing=0.

Proof 1: Join-primality and inverse lower-set maps — outline

Local join-primality lemma

In any lattice, a join-prime element must be join-irreducible. Equivalently, an element that is not join-irreducible is not join-prime. For, if a nonzero element x is not join-irreducible, there exist smaller y and z such that x = y ∨ z. But then x ≤ y ∨ z, and x is not less than or equal to either y or z, showing that it is not join-prime. The bottom element is excluded from both definitions.

Conversely, suppose that x is join-irreducible, and that x ≤ y ∨ z. This inequality is equivalent to the statement that x = x ∧ (y ∨ z), and by the distributive law x = (x ∧ y) ∨ (x ∧ z). But since x is join-irreducible, at least one of the two terms in this join must be x itself, showing that either x = x ∧ y (equivalently x ≤ y) or x = x ∧ z (equivalently x ≤ z).

Inverse lower-set correspondences

In any partial order, the lower sets form a lattice in which the lattice's partial ordering is given by set inclusion, the join operation corresponds to set union, and the meet operation corresponds to set intersection, because unions and intersections preserve the property of being a lower set. Because set unions and intersections obey the distributive law, this is a distributive lattice.

For any lower set S of join-irreducible elements, let x be the join of S, and let T be the lower set of the join-irreducible elements less than or equal to x. Then S = T. For, every element of S clearly belongs to T, and any join-irreducible element less than or equal to x must (by join-primality) be less than or equal to one of the members of S, and therefore must (by the assumption that S is a lower set) belong to S itself. Conversely, for any element x of L, let S be the join-irreducible elements less than or equal to x, and let y be the join of S. Then x = y. For, as a join of elements less than or equal to x, y can be no greater than x itself, but if x is join-irreducible then x belongs to S while if x is the join of two or more join-irreducible items then they must again belong to S, so y ≥ x. Therefore, the correspondence is one-to-one and the theorem is proved.
