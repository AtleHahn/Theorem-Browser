Statement

For n\ge1, let \varphi(n)=\#\{1\le r\le n:\gcd(r,n)=1\}. If a\in\mathbb Z is coprime to n, the least positive integer k with a^k\equiv1\pmod n exists and divides \varphi(n).

Proof 1: Use the cyclic subgroup order

The residue classes modulo n that are coprime to n form a group under multiplication. The order of that group is \varphi(n). Lagrange's theorem states that the order of any subgroup of a finite group divides the order of the entire group, in this case \varphi(n). If a is any number coprime to n, then a is in one of these residue classes, and its powers a,a^2,\ldots,a^k modulo n form a cyclic subgroup of order k, with k the least positive integer such that a^k\equiv1\pmod n. Lagrange's theorem says k must divide \varphi(n).

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Use the group of coprime residue classes and apply Lagrange to its generated cyclic subgroup. Undergraduate abstract algebra suffices; unlike the numerical exponent substitutions, this proof explicitly uses group orders.
