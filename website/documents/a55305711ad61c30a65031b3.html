Statement

Let D^n=\{x\in\mathbb R^n:|x|\le1\} and S^{n-1}=\partial D^n, for an integer n\ge1. There is no smooth map r:D^n\to S^{n-1} with r|_{S^{n-1}}=\mathrm{id}.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Understanding the statement involves Euclidean balls and spheres; smooth maps; boundary retractions. These prerequisites fit undergraduate coursework or a brief extension of it. A bright US college student should be able to understand this statement after approximately 30 minutes of focused background research; this assesses understanding the claim, not its proof.

Proof 1: A regular fibre would have exactly one endpoint

Let D^n=\{x\in\mathbb R^n:|x|\le1\} and S^{n-1}=\partial D^n, for an integer n\ge1. Assume that r:D^n\to S^{n-1} is a smooth retraction.

Such a retraction must have a non-singular value p ∈ ∂Dn, by Sard's theorem, which is also non-singular for the restriction to the boundary (which is just the identity). Thus the inverse image r^{-1}(p) would be a compact 1-manifold with boundary. Such a boundary would have to contain at least two endpoints, and these would have to lie on the boundary of the original ball. This would mean that the inverse image of one point on ∂Dn contains a different point on ∂Dn, contradicting the definition of a retraction Dn → ∂Dn.

Level
Beyond undergraduate level
Level justification

The argument needs smooth manifolds with boundary, regular values, Sard’s theorem, the boundary version of the preimage theorem and the classification of compact one-manifolds. These form a substantial differential-topology prerequisite chain; applying them is not reduced to ordinary multivariable calculus by the final endpoint count.

Proof 2: Oriented volume stays constant — outline

Let B=\{x\in\mathbb R^n:|x|\le1\} and S^{n-1}=\partial B, for an integer n\ge1.

If {\displaystyle r\colon B\to \partial B} is a smooth retraction, one considers the smooth deformation {\displaystyle g^{t}(x):=tr(x)+(1-t)x,} and the smooth function

{\displaystyle \varphi (t):=\int _{B}\det Dg^{t}(x)\,dx.}

Differentiating under the sign of integral it is not difficult to check that φ′(t) = 0 for all t, so φ is a constant function, which is a contradiction because φ(0) is the n-dimensional volume of the ball, while φ(1) is zero.

The geometric idea is that φ(t) is the oriented area of gt(B) (that is, the Lebesgue measure of the image of the ball via gt, taking into account multiplicity and orientation), and should remain constant (as it is very clear in the one-dimensional case). On the other hand, as the parameter t passes from 0 to 1 the map gt transforms continuously from the identity map of the ball, to the retraction r, which is a contradiction since the oriented area of the identity coincides with the volume of the ball, while the oriented area of r is necessarily 0, as its image is the boundary of the ball, a set of null measure.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

The outline can be followed with multivariable calculus and determinant algebra. Write v=r-id and g_t=id+t v. Differentiating det(Dg_t) gives the cofactor contraction with Dv; the cofactor-divergence identity converts it to div(cof(Dg_t)^T v), whose boundary flux is zero because v=0 there. This finite-coordinate identity follows by cancellation of mixed partial derivatives and needs no general degree or differential-form theory. At t=1, differentiating |r|^2=1 makes every derivative column perpendicular to r, so det Dr=0. These are related extensions of undergraduate calculus rather than a new topological theory.

Cofactor divergence identity and determinant variation
Kind:

Theorem

Treatment:

Implicitly assumed

Statement used:

For a smooth Euclidean map g, let C_ij be the cofactor of entry (i,j) of Dg. Then sum_j partial_j C_ij=0. Consequently, for any smooth v, sum_ij C_ij partial_j v_i=div(C^T v). Also d/dt det D(g+t v)|_(t=0)=sum_ij C_ij partial_j v_i.

Application in proof:

Express the derivative of the Jacobian integral as a boundary flux without assuming Dg_t invertible.

Application level:

Undergraduate level

Level justification:

The identities are finite determinant expansion, the product rule and equality of mixed partials; their application requires only multivariable calculus and linear algebra.

Review notes:

Cofactors are polynomial in derivative entries, so the calculation remains valid at singular matrices.
