Cut elimination removes intermediate lemmas from proofs without changing what is provable. It supports a structural analysis of deduction and consistency, though the resulting proofs may be much longer.

Statement

In each of Gentzen’s first-order sequent calculi LK (classical logic) and LJ (intuitionistic logic), every sequent that has a finite derivation has a finite derivation with the same endsequent and no applications of the cut rule.

A sequent \Gamma\vdash\Delta consists of finite lists of first-order formulas; \Gamma gives the assumptions and \Delta the conclusions. In LJ, \Delta has at most one formula. The cut rule has the form

\frac{\Gamma\vdash\Delta,A\qquad A,\Pi\vdash\Lambda}{\Gamma,\Pi\vdash\Delta,\Lambda},

with the single-conclusion restriction in LJ. A derivation without this rule is called cut-free.

Proof 1: Extended-cut reduction and induction on cut rank (external)

A link to the proof can be found here (Sections11–12, pp.52–68; Lemma12.1 and Theorems12.2–12.3).

The source uses multisets; insert exchange rules to realize each ordering and restore the exact endsequent lists. Its implication exercise reduces a principal cut on B\supset C by first cutting on B against the implication-right premise, then cutting on C against the second implication-left premise. With repeated cut formulas, first use the depth induction to remove their residual occurrences from those premises, then make the two smaller cuts and contract duplicate contexts. For LJ, keep the left cut premise single-conclusion throughout: only the antecedent of the right premise can contain repeated cut formulas. Rename eigenvariables before substitutions and permutations so they stay fresh for the added contexts.
