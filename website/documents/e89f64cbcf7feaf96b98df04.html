Explanation

A stronger well-foundedness principle is essential. This includes ordinary Goodstein sequences as a special case. Their uniform termination is not provable in Peano arithmetic, so a proof formalizable in ordinary finite arithmetic cannot establish this extension. This does not rule out an accessible explanation of the ordinal proof.

Statement

For an integer base b\ge2, hereditary base-b notation expands a nonnegative integer in base b, then recursively expands all exponents the same way. Coefficients lie in \{0,\ldots,b-1\}. Let B_{b,c}(a) replace every base b in that notation by c\ge b. Let b_1,b_2,\ldots be any nondecreasing sequence of integers with b_1\ge2. Starting at any nonnegative integer m, set a_1=m and, while positive, a_{n+1}=B_{b_n,b_{n+1}}(a_n)-1. The sequence reaches zero.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Hereditary base notation, recursively replacing bases inside exponents and termination for arbitrary base sequences require undergraduate recursive reasoning; graduate ordinal theory is needed for some proofs, not to understand the statement. A bright US college student should be able to understand the statement after about 30 minutes of focused background research; this rating concerns understanding the claim, not proving it.

Proof 1: Apply the same ordinal descent to nondecreasing bases — outline

Given the extended Goodstein sequence (a_n), we construct a parallel sequence (P_n) of ordinal numbers in Cantor normal form that is strictly decreasing until termination.

We define F_b(u) by computing the hereditary base-b representation of u and then replacing each occurrence of the base b with the first infinite ordinal number \omega. Terms are interpreted in decreasing-exponent Cantor normal form, with finite coefficients on the right. For example,

F_3(100)=F_3(3^{3^1+1}+2\cdot3^2+1) =\omega^{\omega^1+1}+\omega^2\cdot2+1 =\omega^{\omega+1}+\omega^2\cdot2+1.

Each term P_n is then defined as F_{b_n}(a_n). Addition, multiplication and exponentiation of ordinal numbers are well defined. By Cantor normal-form comparison, F_b(u) is strictly increasing in u.

We claim that F_{b_n}(a_n)>F_{b_{n+1}}(a_{n+1}) whenever a_n>0.

Let a'_n be a_n after applying the first, base-changing operation in generating the next element of the sequence, but before the second minus 1 operation. Observe that a_{n+1}=a'_n-1.

Then F_{b_n}(a_n)=F_{b_{n+1}}(a'_n). To calculate the left-hand side, one replaces all occurrences of b_n by \omega in the hereditary representation of a_n. To calculate the right-hand side, one first replaces them by b_{n+1} to get a'_n, then changes all occurrences of b_{n+1} to \omega.

Now we apply the minus 1 operation, and

F_{b_{n+1}}(a'_n)>F_{b_{n+1}}(a_{n+1}),

as a'_n=a_{n+1}+1. For example, if b_n=4 and b_{n+1}=9, then

F_4(3\cdot4^{4^4}+4) =\omega^{\omega^\omega}\cdot3+\omega =F_9(3\cdot9^{9^9}+9),

hence the ordinal F_4(3\cdot4^{4^4}+4) is strictly greater than F_9((3\cdot9^{9^9}+9)-1).

Thus the sequence (P_n) is strictly decreasing as long as a_n>0. As the standard order on ordinals is well-founded, an infinite strictly decreasing sequence cannot exist. But P_n is calculated directly from a_n. Hence the sequence (a_n) must terminate as well, meaning that it must reach 0.
