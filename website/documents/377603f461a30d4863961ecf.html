Statement

Let X,Y be F-spaces, that is, topological vector spaces with topologies induced by complete translation-invariant metrics. A linear map T:X\to Y is continuous if and only if its graph \Gamma_T is closed in X\times Y.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

The F-space metric condition is defined. From linear algebra and basic metric topology, a topological vector space adds continuity of addition and scalar multiplication; a translation-invariant complete metric combines already familiar metric properties with that vector structure. This small extension fits about 30 minutes and explains the full product-closed-graph assertion, without dual spaces or specialized local-convexity theory.

Proof 1: Apply open mapping to the graph projection

If T is continuous, its graph is the pre-image (T\times\operatorname{id}_Y)^{-1}(D), where D=\{(y,y):y\in Y\} is closed since Y is Hausdorff.

Conversely, the closed graph \Gamma_T is an F-space, as a closed linear subspace of the product F-space X\times Y. Use the open mapping theorem: a continuous surjective linear map between F-spaces is open.

Let {\displaystyle f:X\to Y} be the linear map T with closed graph. Then it factors as

{\displaystyle f:X{\overset {i}{\to }}\Gamma _{f}{\overset {q}{\to }}Y}.

Now, {\displaystyle i} is the inverse of the projection {\displaystyle p:\Gamma _{f}\to X}. So, if the open mapping theorem holds for {\displaystyle p}; i.e., {\displaystyle p} is an open mapping, then {\displaystyle i} is continuous and then {\displaystyle f} is continuous (as the composition of continuous maps). Here f=T, i(x)=(x,Tx) and q is the second projection; p is a continuous linear bijection between F-spaces.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

F-spaces are explicitly defined by complete translation-invariant metrics. With metric completeness, vector-space operations, and basic topology, one checks that the graph is an F-space and applies the stated open-mapping theorem to its bijective projection. No proof of that theorem or dual-space theory is needed; the new terminology is a small extension.
