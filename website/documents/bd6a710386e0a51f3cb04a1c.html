Simpler proof assessment

Likely alternative prerequisite level: High school only; lowest recorded proof level: Undergraduate level. For each length N, apply finite van der Waerden to intervals of its sufficient length starting arbitrarily far to the right. Some one of the finitely many colors then has length-N progressions at unbounded initial terms. The sets of colors with this property are nonempty and decrease as N increases, so one color belongs to all of them. This proves exactly the quantifier order in the statement, using finite colors, integer bounds and the elementary finite color-focusing proof, without shift-space topology.

Statement

For every finite partition \mathbb Z=S_1\cup\cdots\cup S_r, one fixed class contains arithmetic progressions of arbitrarily large length with arbitrarily large absolute initial term. Precisely, some j satisfies: for all positive integers N,R, there are a\in\mathbb Z and d\ge1 with |a|\ge R and a,a+d,\ldots,a+(N-1)d\in S_j.

Level
High school only
Level justification

The claim concerns finite colorings of the integers and arbitrarily long arithmetic progressions with specified starting values. These are elementary arithmetic and combinatorial notions. This rating concerns understanding the statement, independently of the difficulty of its proof.

Proof 1: Shift-space reduction to multiple recurrence — outline

Use the multiple Birkhoff recurrence theorem: if X is a nonempty compact metric space and T_1,\ldots,T_N:X\to X are homeomorphisms that commute, then there exist x\in X and an increasing sequence of positive integers n_1<n_2<\cdots such that

\lim_j d(T_i^{n_j}x,x)=0,\qquad i=1,\ldots,N.

It suffices first to show that for each length N, there is at least one class of any finite partition that contains an arithmetic progression of length N. Once this is proved, we can cut out every integer in any prescribed finite interval into its own singleton class, and repeat the process with length at least two. The resulting progression avoids that interval. Thus, for each length, one of the finitely many original classes contains progressions with arbitrarily large absolute initial term. One original class has this property for unboundedly many lengths, and taking initial subprogressions gives the class we want.

Consider the state space \Omega=\{1,\ldots,r\}^{\mathbb Z}, which is compact under the metric (in fact, ultrametric)

d((x_i),(y_i))=\max\{2^{-|i|}:x_i\neq y_i\},

with the empty maximum equal to zero. Since the sets S_1,\ldots,S_r partition \mathbb Z, we have a well-defined sequence z=(\ldots,z_{-1},z_0,z_1,\ldots)=(z_i)_i with i\in S_{z_i} for all i.

Let T:\Omega\to\Omega be the shift map

T((x_i)_i)=(x_{i+1})_i,

and let X=\operatorname{cl}(\{T^jz:j\in\mathbb Z\}) be the closure of all shifts of the sequence z. This is a nonempty compact shift-invariant space. By the multiple Birkhoff recurrence theorem (for the maps T,T^2,\ldots,T^N), there exist a sequence x\in X and an integer s\geq1 such that

d(T^sx,x),d(T^{2s}x,x),\ldots,d(T^{Ns}x,x)<\frac14.

Since X is the closure of shifts of z, and T is continuous, there exists a shift T^mz such that simultaneously, x is very close to T^mz, and T^sx is very close to T^{s+m}z, and so on:

d(x,T^mz),d(T^sx,T^{m+s}z),\ldots,d(T^{Ns}x,T^{m+Ns}z)<\frac14.

By the triangle inequality, we then immediately have d(T^{m+is}z,x)<\tfrac12 for i=0,\ldots,N. But by construction, any sequences y,y'\in\Omega with d(y,y')<1 must have y_0=y'_0. Thus z_m=z_{m+s}=\cdots=z_{m+Ns}, and so all m,m+s,\ldots,m+Ns lie in the class S_{z_m}.
