Statement

Let a,c_n\in\mathbb C and put t=\limsup_{n\to\infty}|c_n|^{1/n}\in[0,+\infty], where limsup is the limiting supremum of the tails. For \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_n(z-a)^n, the radius of convergence is R=1/t: the series converges absolutely for |z-a|<R and diverges for |z-a|>R. Here 1/0=+\infty and 1/(+\infty)=0; the series always converges at z=a.

Proof 1: Coefficient-root bounds and the term test — outline

Without loss of generality assume that {\displaystyle a=0}. We will show first that the power series {\textstyle \sum _{n}c_{n}z^{n}} converges for {\displaystyle |z|<R}, and then that it diverges for {\displaystyle |z|>R}.

First suppose {\displaystyle |z|<R}. Let t=\limsup_{n\to\infty}|c_n|^{1/n} and put R=1/t; suppose t is not {\displaystyle 0} or {\displaystyle \pm \infty .} For any {\displaystyle \varepsilon >0}, there exists only a finite number of {\displaystyle n} such that {\textstyle {\sqrt[{n}]{|c_{n}|}}\geq t+\varepsilon }. Now {\displaystyle |c_{n}|<(t+\varepsilon )^{n}} for all but a finite number of {\displaystyle c_{n}}, so the series {\textstyle \sum _{n}c_{n}z^{n}} converges if {\displaystyle |z|<1/(t+\varepsilon )}. Choosing \varepsilon sufficiently small proves the first part.

Conversely, for {\displaystyle 0<\varepsilon<t}, {\displaystyle |c_{n}|\geq (t-\varepsilon )^{n}} for infinitely many {\displaystyle c_{n}}, so if {\displaystyle |z|=1/(t-\varepsilon )>R}, we see that the series cannot converge because its nth term does not tend to 0.

Every finite |z|>1/t has the indicated form for an appropriate 0<\varepsilon<t.

Level justification

Coefficient-root bounds from the limsup definition give geometric majorants inside the radius and terms bounded away from zero along a subsequence outside it. These are introductory real-analysis methods with complex absolute values. The supplied outline spells out only 0<t<infinity; the endpoint cases require the same elementary limsup estimates.
