Statement

Let A be a commutative non-Noetherian ring and I\subset A an ideal that is not finitely generated. Define C=A\oplus A with multiplication (a,x)(b,y)=(ab,bx+ay) and the A-algebra structure a\mapsto(a,0). Then B=A\oplus I is a subalgebra of C, the algebra C is of finite type over A and finite as a B-module, but B is not of finite type over A.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Introductory rings and ideals plus linear algebra provide the starting point. Here non-Noetherian means an ideal need not be finitely generated; finite type means polynomial generation and finite module means finite linear span over the indicated ring. The multiplication and algebra inclusion are explicit on pairs. Learning these closely related notions of generation in this concrete construction fits about 30 minutes, without learning general extension or module structure theory.

Proof 1: Square-zero extension outline

Indeed, for any non-Noetherian ring A we can define an A-algebra structure on {\displaystyle C=A\oplus A} by declaring {\displaystyle (a,x)(b,y)=(ab,bx+ay)}. Then for any ideal {\displaystyle I\subset A} which is not finitely generated, {\displaystyle B=A\oplus I\subset C} is not of finite type over A, while C is of finite type over A and finite as a B-module.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

All structure is given by the explicit multiplication of pairs. Put epsilon=(0,1); then epsilon^2=0 and C is generated over A as an algebra by epsilon and over B as a module by 1,epsilon. If finitely many pairs (a_i,x_i) generated B as an A-algebra, every second coordinate of a polynomial in them would be an A-linear combination of the x_i, forcing I to be finitely generated. From introductory rings and ideals, the only extra preparation is finite span versus polynomial generation, made concrete here; no abstract extension or module structure theorem is needed.
