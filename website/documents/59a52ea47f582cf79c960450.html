Simpler proof assessment

Likely alternative prerequisite level: High school only; lowest recorded proof level: Undergraduate level. Expand f(t+h) as a finite polynomial in h. A double root makes its constant and linear coefficients zero. Arithmetic weights b_i=u+vi recombine f(r) and r times that linear coefficient, giving g(r)=0 including r=0. At a local extremum a nonzero linear coefficient would dominate the finitely many higher powers for sufficiently small positive and negative h, contradicting the extremum. Its vanishing gives the second displayed formula. These are finite algebraic estimates with no derivative theorem.

Statement

Let f(x)=\sum_{i=0}^n a_i x^{n-i} be a real polynomial of degree n\ge2, with a double root r. If b_0,\ldots,b_n is an arithmetic progression, then r is a root of g(x)=\sum_{i=0}^n a_i b_i x^{n-i}.

Let f(x)=a_0x^n+a_1x^{n-1}+\cdots+a_n be a real polynomial, n\ge1. If f has a local maximum or minimum at x=t\in\mathbb R, then na_0t^n+(n-1)a_1t^{n-1}+\cdots+a_{n-1}t=0.

A double root means (x-r)^2 divides f(x). A local maximum at t means f(x)\le f(t) throughout some open interval containing t, and a local minimum reverses this inequality.

Level
High school only
Level justification

Both rules state algebraic vanishing conditions. Local extrema are defined by nearby inequalities, so understanding the statement requires only high-school polynomial and inequality reasoning, even though its source proof uses calculus.

Proof 1: Hudde’s arithmetic-weight rule for a double root — outline

This rule is a form of the theorem that if r is a double root of f(x)=0, then r is a root of f'(x)=0. The arithmetic-progression recombination is not expanded in the source.

For the second rule, Fermat’s stationary-point theorem gives f'(t)=0. The polynomial on the left of the asserted equation is t f'(t), so it vanishes.
