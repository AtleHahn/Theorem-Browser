Statement

Let A be a commutative Noetherian ring with identity, and let (x) be a proper principal ideal. Every prime ideal \mathfrak p minimal over (x) has height at most one; height is the supremum of lengths of strictly increasing chains of prime ideals ending at that prime.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Introductory ring theory supplies ideals, prime/maximal ideals, products and inclusion. Noetherian means every ideal is finitely generated. The further conditions are short ideal-based definitions: height counts chains of primes, local means a unique maximal ideal, and primary uses powers in the defining product condition. The relevant definitions are a small extension of familiar ideals, without localization or scheme theory.

Proof 1: Symbolic powers and two applications of Nakayama

Let {\displaystyle A} be a Noetherian ring, x an element of it and {\displaystyle {\mathfrak {p}}} a minimal prime over x. Localization preserves the prime chains below \mathfrak p. Replacing A by the localization {\displaystyle A_{\mathfrak {p}}}, we can assume {\displaystyle A} is local with the maximal ideal {\displaystyle {\mathfrak {p}}}. Let {\displaystyle {\mathfrak {q}}\subsetneq {\mathfrak {p}}} be a strictly smaller prime ideal and let {\displaystyle {\mathfrak {q}}^{(n)}={\mathfrak {q}}^{n}A_{\mathfrak {q}}\cap A}, which is a {\displaystyle {\mathfrak {q}}}-primary ideal called the n-th symbolic power of {\displaystyle {\mathfrak {q}}}. It forms a descending chain of ideals {\displaystyle A\supset {\mathfrak {q}}\supset {\mathfrak {q}}^{(2)}\supset {\mathfrak {q}}^{(3)}\supset \cdots }. Thus, there is the descending chain of ideals {\displaystyle ({\mathfrak {q}}^{(n)}+(x))/(x)} in the ring {\displaystyle {\overline {A}}=A/(x)}. Now, by minimality and the local maximal-ideal condition, \mathfrak p is the only prime containing (x), so \sqrt{(x)}=\mathfrak p. Since {\displaystyle (x)} contains some power of its radical, it follows that {\displaystyle {\overline {A}}} is an Artinian ring and thus the chain {\displaystyle ({\mathfrak {q}}^{(n)}+(x))/(x)} stabilizes and so there is some n such that {\displaystyle {\mathfrak {q}}^{(n)}+(x)={\mathfrak {q}}^{(n+1)}+(x)}. This implies:

{\displaystyle {\mathfrak {q}}^{(n)}={\mathfrak {q}}^{(n+1)}+x\,{\mathfrak {q}}^{(n)}},

from the fact {\displaystyle {\mathfrak {q}}^{(n)}} is {\displaystyle {\mathfrak {q}}}-primary (if {\displaystyle y} is in {\displaystyle {\mathfrak {q}}^{(n)}\subseteq {\mathfrak {q}}^{(n+1)}+(x)}, then {\displaystyle y=z+ax} with {\displaystyle z\in {\mathfrak {q}}^{(n+1)}} and {\displaystyle a\in A}. Since {\displaystyle {\mathfrak {p}}} is minimal over {\displaystyle x}, {\displaystyle x\not \in {\mathfrak {q}}} and so {\displaystyle ax\in {\mathfrak {q}}^{(n)}} implies {\displaystyle a} is in {\displaystyle {\mathfrak {q}}^{(n)}}.) Now, quotienting out both sides by {\displaystyle {\mathfrak {q}}^{(n+1)}} yields {\displaystyle {\mathfrak {q}}^{(n)}/{\mathfrak {q}}^{(n+1)}=(x){\mathfrak {q}}^{(n)}/{\mathfrak {q}}^{(n+1)}}. Then, by Nakayama's lemma (which says a finitely generated module M is zero if {\displaystyle M=IM} for some ideal I contained in the Jacobson radical), we get {\displaystyle M={\mathfrak {q}}^{(n)}/{\mathfrak {q}}^{(n+1)}=0}; i.e., {\displaystyle {\mathfrak {q}}^{(n)}={\mathfrak {q}}^{(n+1)}} and thus {\displaystyle {\mathfrak {q}}^{n}A_{\mathfrak {q}}={\mathfrak {q}}^{n+1}A_{\mathfrak {q}}}. Using Nakayama's lemma again, {\displaystyle {\mathfrak {q}}^{n}A_{\mathfrak {q}}=0} and {\displaystyle A_{\mathfrak {q}}} is an Artinian ring; thus, the height of {\displaystyle {\mathfrak {q}}} is zero. {\displaystyle \square }

Every strictly smaller prime has height zero, so \operatorname{ht}(\mathfrak p)\leq1.
