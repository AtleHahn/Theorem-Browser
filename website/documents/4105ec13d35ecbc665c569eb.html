Statement

A simple closed smooth or polygonal curve C in \mathbb R^2 has exactly two complementary connected components, one bounded and one unbounded.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Basic topology supplies continuous injections, connected components, path connectedness and homeomorphisms. The sphere or Jordan curve is explicitly a familiar Euclidean subset or continuous circle image; the full assertion needs no homology or manifold machinery used by its proof.

Proof 1: Connect through a collar and distinguish winding numbers — outline

The curve has a tubular neighbourhood, defined in the smooth case by the field of unit normal vectors to the curve or in the polygonal case by points at a distance of less than ε from the curve. In a neighbourhood of a differentiable point on the curve, there is a coordinate change in which the curve becomes the diameter of an open disk. Taking a point not on the curve, a straight line aimed at the curve starting at the point will eventually meet the tubular neighborhood; the path can be continued next to the curve until it meets the disk. It will meet it on one side or the other. This proves that the complement of the curve has at most two connected components. On the other hand, using the Cauchy integral formula for the winding number, it can be seen that the winding number is constant on connected components of the complement of the curve, is zero near infinity and increases by 1 when crossing the curve. Hence the curve separates the plane into exactly two components, its "interior" and its "exterior", the latter being unbounded.

Level
Beyond undergraduate level
Level justification

Use a collar to bound the number of components and local winding-number jumps to separate the two sides. Tubular neighborhoods plus the contour-theoretic winding jump require a cumulative geometric-topological background; retain beyond undergraduate conservatively.
