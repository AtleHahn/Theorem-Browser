Likely alternative prerequisite level: Undergraduate level; lowest recorded proof level: Beyond undergraduate level. Triangulate the polygonal faces. Summing vertex defects gives 2pi V-pi F, and each edge belongs to two triangles, so 3F=2E. Therefore the sum is 2pi(V-E+F)=2pi chi. Face triangulation preserves Euler characteristic; sphere topology gives chi=2. This covers closed surfaces, not just convex polyhedra, with finite counting and basic surface/Euler-characteristic terminology. The statement keeps the target at undergraduate even though the arithmetic is elementary.

Statement

Let P be a compact closed polyhedral surface. At each vertex v, let \delta_v=2\pi minus the sum of incident face angles. Then \sum_v\delta_v=2\pi\chi(P). In particular a sphere-like polyhedron has total defect 4\pi.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

For a finite polyhedral surface the Euler characteristic is the combinatorial number vertices minus edges plus faces. The remaining quantities are face angles and their finite sums. These definitions suffice for the full polyhedral formula without curvature tensors or homology.

Proof 1: Total angular defect of a closed polyhedral surface — outline

This is the special case of Gauss–Bonnet in which the curvature is concentrated at discrete points (the vertices). Thinking of curvature as a measure rather than a function, Descartes' theorem is Gauss–Bonnet where the curvature is a discrete measure, and Gauss–Bonnet for measures generalizes both Gauss–Bonnet for smooth manifolds and Descartes' theorem.

The masses at the vertices are the angular defects \delta_v, so the measure form of Gauss–Bonnet gives \sum_v\delta_v=2\pi\chi(P). In particular, for a polyhedron homeomorphic to the sphere, \chi(P)=2 and the sum of the defects is 4\pi. The curvature-measure theorem and the identification of its vertex masses are used here without proof.

Level
Beyond undergraduate level
Level justification

Apply Gauss-Bonnet to a curvature measure and identify its atomic masses with angle defects. The actual proof uses generalized curvature measures rather than finite face counting, requiring graduate geometric analysis.
