Statement

A Pythagorean triple means positive integers (a,b,c) with a^2+b^2=c^2; it is primitive if \gcd(a,b,c)=1. Up to exchanging the legs, every triple is uniquely k(m^2-n^2,2mn,m^2+n^2) with positive k, coprime m>n>0 of opposite parity.

Proof 1: Source argument

Use Euclid's formula for primitive triples: a=m^2-n^2, b=2mn, c=m^2+n^2. Every primitive triple arises (after the exchange of a and b, if a is even) from a unique pair of coprime numbers m>n>0, one of which is even.

Despite generating all primitive triples, Euclid's formula does not produce all triples—for example, (9, 12, 15) cannot be generated using integer m and n. This can be remedied by inserting an additional parameter k to the formula: after exchanging the legs if necessary, every Pythagorean triple is generated uniquely by

{\displaystyle a=k\cdot (m^{2}-n^{2}),\ \,b=k\cdot (2mn),\ \,c=k\cdot (m^{2}+n^{2})}

where m, n, and k are positive integers with m > n, and with m and n coprime and not both odd.

That these formulas generate Pythagorean triples can be verified by expanding a2 + b2 using elementary algebra and verifying that the result equals c2. Since every Pythagorean triple can be divided through by some integer k to obtain a primitive triple, every triple can be generated uniquely by using the formula with m and n to generate its primitive counterpart and then multiplying through by k as in the last equation.

Level justification

The primitive-triple formula is invoked in its explicitly stated arithmetic form. Dividing a triple by its greatest common divisor yields the unique primitive triple; multiplying back gives k, while uniqueness of m,n comes from the invoked formula. Expanding the squares checks the equation. These operations use only integer divisibility and high-school algebra.
