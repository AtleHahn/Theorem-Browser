Statement

Let P=u^2+v^2 be a prime, where u,v\in\mathbb Z. If P\mid a^2+b^2 for integers a,b, then (a^2+b^2)/P is a sum of two integer squares.

Level
High school only
Level justification

The assertion uses integer divisibility by a prime and a sum-of-squares representation of an integer quotient.

Proof 1: Factor a difference and divide the product identity

Write u=p, v=q, so the given prime is P=p^2+q^2.

Indeed, suppose for example that {\displaystyle a^{2}+b^{2}} is divisible by {\displaystyle p^{2}+q^{2}} and that this latter is a prime. Then {\displaystyle p^{2}+q^{2}} divides

{\displaystyle p^{2}(a^{2}+b^{2})-a^{2}(p^{2}+q^{2})=p^{2}b^{2}-a^{2}q^{2}=(pb-aq)(pb+aq).}

Since {\displaystyle p^{2}+q^{2}} is a prime, it divides one of the two factors. Suppose that it divides {\displaystyle pb-aq}. Since

{\displaystyle (a^{2}+b^{2})(p^{2}+q^{2})=(ap+bq)^{2}+(aq-bp)^{2}}

(Diophantus's identity) it follows that {\displaystyle p^{2}+q^{2}} must divide {\displaystyle (ap+bq)^{2}}. Since the divisor is prime, it also divides ap+bq. So the equation can be divided by the square of {\displaystyle p^{2}+q^{2}}. Dividing the expression by {\displaystyle (p^{2}+q^{2})^{2}} yields:

{\displaystyle {\frac {a^{2}+b^{2}}{p^{2}+q^{2}}}=\left({\frac {ap+bq}{p^{2}+q^{2}}}\right)^{2}+\left({\frac {aq-bp}{p^{2}+q^{2}}}\right)^{2}}

and thus expresses the quotient as a sum of two squares, as claimed.

On the other hand if {\displaystyle p^{2}+q^{2}} divides {\displaystyle pb+aq}, a similar argument holds by using the following variant of Diophantus's identity:

{\displaystyle (a^{2}+b^{2})(q^{2}+p^{2})=(aq+bp)^{2}+(ap-bq)^{2}.}
