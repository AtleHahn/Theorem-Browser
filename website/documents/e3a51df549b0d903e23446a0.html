Statement

For an odd prime p and integer a not divisible by p, write (a/p)=1 if a is a nonzero square modulo p, and (a/p)=-1 otherwise. Let n count the least positive residues of a,2a,\ldots,((p-1)/2)a exceeding p/2. Then (a/p)=(-1)^n.

Level
High school only
Level justification

Quadratic residues, least positive residues and finite counts are elementary integer arithmetic.

Proof 1: Gauss's lemma for quadratic residues — proof

A fairly simple proof, reminiscent of one of the simplest proofs of Fermat's little theorem, can be obtained by evaluating the product

{\displaystyle Z=a\cdot 2a\cdot 3a\cdot \cdots \cdot {\frac {p-1}{2}}a}

modulo p in two different ways. All congruences below are modulo p. On one hand it is equal to

{\displaystyle Z=a^{(p-1)/2}\left(1\cdot 2\cdot 3\cdot \cdots \cdot {\frac {p-1}{2}}\right)}

The second evaluation takes more work. If x is a nonzero residue modulo p, using its representative in \{1,\ldots,p-1\}, let us define the "absolute value" of x to be

{\displaystyle |x|={\begin{cases}x&{\mbox{if }}1\leq x\leq {\frac {p-1}{2}},\\p-x&{\mbox{if }}{\frac {p+1}{2}}\leq x\leq p-1.\end{cases}}}

Since n counts those multiples ka which are in the latter range, and since for those multiples, −ka is in the first range, we have

{\displaystyle Z\equiv(-1)^{n}\left(|a|\cdot |2a|\cdot |3a|\cdot \cdots \cdots \left|{\frac {p-1}{2}}a\right|\right).}

Now observe that the values |ra| are distinct for r = 1, 2, …, (p − 1)/2. Indeed, we have

{\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}|ra|&\equiv |sa|&{\pmod {p}}\\ra&\equiv \pm sa&{\pmod {p}}\\r&\equiv \pm s&{\pmod {p}}\end{aligned}}}

because a is coprime to p.

This gives r = s, since 1\le r,s\le(p-1)/2. But there are exactly (p − 1)/2 of them, so their values are a rearrangement of the integers 1, 2, …, (p − 1)/2. Therefore,

{\displaystyle Z\equiv(-1)^{n}\left(1\cdot 2\cdot 3\cdot \cdots \cdot {\frac {p-1}{2}}\right).}

Comparing with our first evaluation, we may cancel out the nonzero factor

{\displaystyle 1\cdot 2\cdot 3\cdot \cdots \cdot {\frac {p-1}{2}}}

and we are left with

{\displaystyle a^{(p-1)/2}\equiv(-1)^{n}.}

This is the desired result, because by Euler's criterion the left hand side is just an alternative expression for the Legendre symbol {\displaystyle \left({\frac {a}{p}}\right)}.

Both the Legendre symbol and (-1)^n are \pm1, so their congruence modulo the odd prime p implies equality.
