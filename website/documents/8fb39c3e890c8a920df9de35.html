Statement

Let I\subseteq\mathbb R be a nondegenerate interval and let f:I\to\mathbb R be continuous and injective. Write g:f(I)\to I for its inverse. If f is differentiable at x_0\in I and f'(x_0)\ne0, then g is differentiable at y_0=f(x_0) and

g'(y_0)=\frac{1}{f'(x_0)}=\frac{1}{f'(g(y_0))}.

At an included endpoint of the intervals, derivatives are understood as the corresponding one-sided derivatives.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

The full assertion uses standard single-variable calculus; one-sided endpoint derivatives are ordinary one-sided limits and add no further prerequisite theory.

Proof 1: Invert a continuous difference quotient (external)

A link to the proof can be found here (Differentiability, Proposition4; Continuity, Proposition1 and Theorem4).

All limits are relative to the respective intervals. For an increasing f, values at nearby points on either side of x_0 bound the inverse near f(x_0); at an endpoint only the available side is needed. A decreasing function is handled by reversing the inequalities. Thus inverse continuity holds at included endpoints too. For y\ne y_0, put x=g(y) and use

\frac{g(y)-g(y_0)}{y-y_0}=\left(\frac{f(x)-f(x_0)}{x-x_0}\right)^{-1}.

As y\to y_0, inverse continuity gives x\to x_0, so the right side tends to 1/f'(x_0). No differentiability away from x_0 is required.
