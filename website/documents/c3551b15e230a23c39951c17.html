Statement

Let f be a map from a neighborhood of (a,b)\in\mathbb R^n\times\mathbb R^m to \mathbb R^m. If f(a,b)=0, f is continuously differentiable in a neighborhood of (a,b) and the derivative of y\mapsto f(a,y) at b is invertible, then there exists a continuously differentiable map g:U\to V for some neighborhoods U,V of a,b such that f(x,g(x))=0. Moreover, if f(x,y)=0, x\in U, y\in V, then y=g(x); i.e., g(x) is a unique solution.

The neighborhoods can be chosen so that D_yf(x,g(x)) is invertible for every x\in U, and the derivative of the implicit function is

Dg(x)=-[D_yf(x,g(x))]^{-1}D_xf(x,g(x)).

Here D_yf is the m\times m matrix of partial derivatives with respect to the y coordinates, D_xf is the m\times n matrix for the x coordinates, and Dg is the m\times n Jacobian of g.

Proof 1: Implicit function theorem from local inversion — outline

To see this, consider the map F(x,y)=(x,f(x,y)). Its derivative at (a,b) has identity in the first diagonal block and D_yf(a,b) in the second, and is invertible. By the inverse function theorem, F has a continuously differentiable inverse G between neighborhoods of (a,b) and (a,0). We then have:

(x,y)=F(G_1(x,y),G_2(x,y)) =(G_1(x,y),f(G_1(x,y),G_2(x,y))),

implying x=G_1(x,y) and y=f(x,G_2(x,y)). Thus g(x)=G_2(x,0) has the required property, after shrinking to product neighborhoods U\times V so that (x,0) is in the inverse domain and g(x)\in V for x\in U. Injectivity of F gives the asserted uniqueness.

The source states the displayed general matrix formula for the derivative of g; the routine multivariable chain-rule computation from f(x,g(x))=0 is left implicit. Its two-variable discussion explicitly uses this differentiation step.
