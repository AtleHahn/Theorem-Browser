Statement

Graphs are finite undirected graphs considered up to isomorphism. A minor is obtained by a sequence of vertex deletions, edge deletions and edge contractions. The following assertions are equivalent: (i) there is no infinite antichain under minors; (ii) every infinite graph family has two distinct comparable members; (iii) every graph family has finitely many minor-minimal members; (iv) every minor-closed graph family is characterized by a finite set of excluded minors.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Linear algebra, basic topology and set relations supply the objects. Convex cones, finite graph minors and generated set families are explicitly defined through familiar combinations, deletions or intersections; these are small concrete extensions.

Proof 1: Relate antichains, minimal elements and complement obstructions

Every graph in a family has a minor-minimal family member as a minor, by descent in the nonnegative integer |V|+|E|.

If {\displaystyle {\mathcal {S}}} is a set of graphs, and {\displaystyle {\mathcal {M}}} is a subset of {\displaystyle {\mathcal {S}}} containing one representative graph for each equivalence class of minimal elements (graphs that belong to {\displaystyle {\mathcal {S}}} but for which no proper minor belongs to {\displaystyle {\mathcal {S}}}), then {\displaystyle {\mathcal {M}}} forms an antichain; therefore, absence of infinite antichains implies that, in any infinite set {\displaystyle {\mathcal {S}}} of graphs, there must be only a finite number of non-isomorphic minimal elements.

Another equivalent form of the theorem is that, in any infinite set {\displaystyle {\mathcal {S}}} of graphs, there must be a pair of graphs one of which is a minor of the other. The statement that every infinite set has finitely many minimal elements implies this form of the theorem, for if there are only finitely many minimal elements, then each of the remaining graphs must belong to a pair of this type with one of the minimal elements. And in the other direction, this form of the theorem implies the statement that there can be no infinite antichains, because an infinite antichain is a set that does not contain any pair related by the minor relation.

The existence of forbidden minor characterizations for all minor-closed graph families is an equivalent way of stating the Robertson–Seymour theorem. For, suppose that every minor-closed family {\displaystyle {\mathcal {F}}} has a finite set {\displaystyle {\mathcal {H}}} of minimal forbidden minors, and let {\displaystyle {\mathcal {S}}} be any infinite set of graphs. Determine {\displaystyle {\mathcal {F}}} from {\displaystyle {\mathcal {S}}} as the family of graphs that do not have a minor in {\displaystyle {\mathcal {S}}}. Then {\displaystyle {\mathcal {F}}} is minor-closed and has a finite set {\displaystyle {\mathcal {H}}} of minimal forbidden minors. Let {\displaystyle {\mathcal {C}}} be the complement of {\displaystyle {\mathcal {F}}}. {\displaystyle {\mathcal {S}}} is a subset of {\displaystyle {\mathcal {C}}} since {\displaystyle {\mathcal {S}}} and {\displaystyle {\mathcal {F}}} are disjoint, and {\displaystyle {\mathcal {H}}} are the minimal graphs in {\displaystyle {\mathcal {C}}}. Consider a graph {\displaystyle G} in {\displaystyle {\mathcal {H}}}. {\displaystyle G} cannot have a proper minor in {\displaystyle {\mathcal {S}}} since {\displaystyle G} is minimal in {\displaystyle {\mathcal {C}}}. At the same time, {\displaystyle G} must have a minor in {\displaystyle S}, since otherwise {\displaystyle G} would be an element in {\displaystyle {\mathcal {F}}}. Therefore, {\displaystyle G} is an element in {\displaystyle {\mathcal {S}}}, i.e., {\displaystyle {\mathcal {H}}} is a subset of {\displaystyle {\mathcal {S}}}, and all other graphs in {\displaystyle {\mathcal {S}}} have a minor among the graphs in {\displaystyle {\mathcal {H}}}, so {\displaystyle {\mathcal {H}}} is the finite set of minimal elements of {\displaystyle {\mathcal {S}}}.

To demonstrate the other direction of the equivalence, assume that every set of graphs has a finite set of all minor-minimal graphs and let a minor-closed set {\displaystyle {\mathcal {F}}} be given. We want to find a set {\displaystyle {\mathcal {H}}} of graphs such that a graph is in {\displaystyle {\mathcal {F}}} if and only if it does not have a minor in {\displaystyle {\mathcal {H}}}. Let {\displaystyle {\mathcal {E}}} be the graphs which are not minors of any graph in {\displaystyle {\mathcal {F}}}, and let {\displaystyle {\mathcal {H}}} be the finite set of minimal graphs in {\displaystyle {\mathcal {E}}}. Now, let an arbitrary graph {\displaystyle G} be given. Assume first that {\displaystyle G} is in {\displaystyle {\mathcal {F}}}. {\displaystyle G} cannot have a minor in {\displaystyle {\mathcal {H}}} since {\displaystyle G} is in {\displaystyle {\mathcal {F}}} and {\displaystyle {\mathcal {H}}} is a subset of {\displaystyle {\mathcal {E}}}. Now assume that {\displaystyle G} is not in {\displaystyle {\mathcal {F}}}. Then {\displaystyle G} is not a minor of any graph in {\displaystyle {\mathcal {F}}}, since {\displaystyle {\mathcal {F}}} is minor-closed. Therefore, {\displaystyle G} is in {\displaystyle {\mathcal {E}}}, so {\displaystyle G} has a minor in {\displaystyle {\mathcal {H}}}.
