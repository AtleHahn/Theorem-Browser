Statement

If R is a UFD and n\ge1, then R[x_1,\ldots,x_n] is a UFD.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Introductory polynomial and ring theory supplies degrees, divisibility, ideals and finite-field arithmetic. The radical consists of elements with a power in the ideal, and Krull dimension counts lengths of prime-ideal chains. These limited ideal-based definitions suffice without schemes, localization or field-extension arithmetic.

Proof 1: Polynomial rings over a UFD have unique factorization — proof

Let F be the field of fractions of R. For a nonzero polynomial h\in F[x], choose d\ne0 in R so that dh has coefficients in R, and factor out the gcd q of these coefficients to write dh=qh' with h'\in R[x] primitive. Here primitive means that the gcd of the coefficients is a unit. The primitive representative \operatorname{pp}(h)=h' is unique up to multiplication by a unit of R, and Gauss's lemma gives

\operatorname{pp}(h_1h_2)=\operatorname{pp}(h_1)\operatorname{pp}(h_2)

up to such a unit.

Indeed, by induction, it is enough to show R[x] is a UFD when R is a UFD. Let f\in R[x] be a nonzero polynomial. Now, F[x] is a unique factorization domain (since it is a principal ideal domain) and so, as a polynomial in F[x], f can be factorized as

f=u g_1g_2\cdots g_r,

where u\in F^\times and the g_i are irreducible polynomials of F[x]; the product may be empty.

Now, we write f=cf' for the gcd c of the coefficients of f (and f' is the primitive part) and then, choosing the primitive representatives up to units,

f=cf'=c\operatorname{pp}(g_1)\operatorname{pp}(g_2)\cdots\operatorname{pp}(g_r).

Now, c is, up to a unit, a product of prime elements of R (since R is a UFD), and a prime element p of R is a prime element of R[x], as R[x]/(p)\cong(R/(p))[x] is an integral domain. Hence, c admits a prime factorization (or a unique factorization into irreducibles).

Next, observe that

f'=\operatorname{pp}(g_1)\cdots\operatorname{pp}(g_r)

is a unique factorization into irreducible elements of R[x], as (1) each \operatorname{pp}(g_i) is irreducible by Gauss's irreducibility statement—a nonconstant polynomial in R[x] is irreducible if and only if it is primitive and irreducible in F[x]—and (2) it is unique since the factorization of f' can also be viewed as a factorization in F[x] and factorization there is unique. Primitive representatives are unique up to units of R. Since c and f' are uniquely determined by f up to unit elements, the above factorization of f is a unique factorization into irreducible elements.
