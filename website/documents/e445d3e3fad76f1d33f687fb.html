Statement

For a Euclidean parallelogram ABCD in cyclic order, AC^2+BD^2=2AB^2+2BC^2.

Proof 1: Add the two cosine-law identities

In the parallelogram ABCD, let AD = BC = a, AB = DC = b, {\displaystyle \angle BAD=\alpha .} By using the law of cosines in triangle {\displaystyle \triangle BAD,} we get:

{\displaystyle a^{2}+b^{2}-2ab\cos(\alpha )=BD^{2}.}

In a parallelogram, adjacent angles are supplementary, therefore {\displaystyle \angle ADC=180^{\circ }-\alpha .} Using the law of cosines in triangle {\displaystyle \triangle ADC,} produces:

{\displaystyle a^{2}+b^{2}-2ab\cos(180^{\circ }-\alpha )=AC^{2}.}

By applying the trigonometric identity {\displaystyle \cos(180^{\circ }-x)=-\cos x} to the last equation gives:

{\displaystyle a^{2}+b^{2}+2ab\cos(\alpha )=AC^{2}.}

Now the sum of squares {\displaystyle BD^{2}+AC^{2}} can be expressed as:

{\displaystyle BD^{2}+AC^{2}=a^{2}+b^{2}-2ab\cos(\alpha )+a^{2}+b^{2}+2ab\cos(\alpha ).}

Simplifying this expression, it becomes:

{\displaystyle BD^{2}+AC^{2}=2a^{2}+2b^{2}.}

Proof 2: Coincident diagonal midpoints in Euler’s quadrilateral identity

Euler’s quadrilateral identity says AB^2+BC^2+CD^2+DA^2=AC^2+BD^2+4x^2, where x is the distance between the diagonal midpoints. In a parallelogram the diagonals bisect each other, so x=0. Opposite sides have equal length, giving the stated identity.
