Statement

Let D=\{z\in\mathbb C:|z|<1\} and let f:D\to\mathbb C be holomorphic with f(0)=0 and |f(z)|\le1. Then |f(z)|\le|z| and |f'(0)|\le1. If equality holds at a nonzero point in the first inequality or in the derivative inequality, then f(z)=az for some |a|=1.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Complex differentiability extends the calculus derivative; limits, compact sets, uniform approximation and zeros of functions are familiar analysis. Zero multiplicity is the order of the first nonzero Taylor coefficient, and circle integrals are parametrized ordinary integrals. These limited definitions suffice for the full claim.

Proof 1: Maximum modulus of the divided function

The proof is a straightforward application of the maximum modulus principle on the function

{\displaystyle g(z)={\begin{cases}{\frac {f(z)}{z}}\,&{\mbox{if }}z\neq 0\\f'(0)&{\mbox{if }}z=0,\end{cases}}}

which is holomorphic on the whole of {\displaystyle D }, including at the origin (because {\displaystyle f} is differentiable at the origin and fixes zero). Now if {\displaystyle D _{r}=\{z:|z|\leq r\}} denotes the closed disk of radius {\displaystyle r} centered at the origin, then the maximum modulus principle implies that, for {\displaystyle r<1}, given any {\displaystyle z\in D _{r}}, there exists {\displaystyle z_{r}} on the boundary of {\displaystyle D _{r}} such that

{\displaystyle |g(z)|\leq |g(z_{r})|={\frac {|f(z_{r})|}{|z_{r}|}}\leq {\frac {1}{r}}.}

As {\displaystyle r\rightarrow 1} we get {\displaystyle |g(z)|\leq 1}.

Moreover, suppose that {\displaystyle |f(z)|=|z|} for some non-zero {\displaystyle z\in D }, or {\displaystyle |f'(0)|=1}. Then, {\displaystyle |g(z)|=1} at some point of {\displaystyle D }. So by the maximum modulus principle, {\displaystyle g(z)} is equal to a constant {\displaystyle a} such that {\displaystyle |a|=1}. Therefore, {\displaystyle f(z)=az}, as desired.

Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Remove the divided function singularity and apply maximum modulus on smaller disks, then take their radii to one. Undergraduate complex analysis suffices.
