Statement

Let F be a finite field and n,r\ge1 integers. Let polynomials f_j\in F[X_1,\ldots,X_n] have nonnegative degree bounds d_j with \deg f_j\le d_j, \sum_jd_j<n, and f_j(0)=0 for every j. Then they have a common zero in F^n other than the origin.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Introductory algebra supplies fields, characteristic and multivariate polynomial evaluation. A finite field is a field with finitely many elements; the statements only count its polynomial solutions and compare degrees, without field extensions or norm maps. This is a direct use of the baseline.

Proof 1: Divisibility rules out exactly one zero — outline

Write q=|F| and let p be the characteristic of F.

Local polynomial-sum lemma

Remark: If {\displaystyle 0\le i<q-1} for an integer i (with x^0=1), then

{\displaystyle \sum _{x\in F }x^{i}=0}

so the sum over {\displaystyle F ^{n}} of any polynomial in {\displaystyle x_{1},\ldots ,x_{n}} of degree less than {\displaystyle n(q-1)} also vanishes.

Count the common zeros

The total number of common solutions modulo {\displaystyle p} of {\displaystyle f_{1},\ldots ,f_{r}=0} is equal to

{\displaystyle \sum _{x\in F ^{n}}(1-f_{1}^{q-1}(x))\cdot \ldots \cdot (1-f_{r}^{q-1}(x))}

because each term is 1 for a solution and 0 otherwise. If the sum of the degree bounds of the polynomials {\displaystyle f_{i}} is less than n then this vanishes by the polynomial-sum lemma above. Here the sum is taken in F; an integer maps to zero in F exactly when it is divisible by p.

Chevalley's theorem is an immediate consequence of this divisibility result since p is at least 2 and the origin is a common zero.

Proof 2: Use Warning’s lower bound on the number of zeros

Warning's second theorem states that if the system of polynomial equations has the trivial solution, then it has at least q^{n-d} solutions where q is the size of the finite field and d:=d_1+\dots+d_r. Chevalley's theorem also follows directly from this, since q^{n-d}\ge2.
