Statement

For a nonzero irreducible nonnegative square matrix A, with the positive-length walk convention, its Perron eigenvalue r=\rho(A) is algebraically simple.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Understanding the statement involves finite-dimensional matrices; eigenvalues and spectral radius; irreducibility and positive directed walks. These prerequisites fit undergraduate coursework or a brief extension of it. A bright US college student should be able to understand this statement after approximately 30 minutes of focused background research; this assesses understanding the claim, not its proof.

Proof 1: Source matrix argument

Use the Perron existence theorem to obtain v>0 with Av=rv, r>0. We first give the positivity argument needed below.

One of the definitions of irreducibility for nonnegative matrices is that for all indices i,j there exists m\geq1 such that (A^m)_{ij} is strictly positive. Given a nonzero nonnegative eigenvector v, at least one of its components, say the ith, is strictly positive. Its eigenvalue r is nonnegative because Av=rv\geq0. Given n\geq1 such that (A^n)_{ii}>0, we have

r^n v_i=(A^n v)_i\geq(A^n)_{ii}v_i>0.

Hence r is strictly positive. Then, given m such that (A^m)_{ji}>0,

r^m v_j=(A^m v)_j\geq(A^m)_{ji}v_i>0,

hence v_j is strictly positive, i.e., the eigenvector is strictly positive.

Given a strictly positive eigenvector v corresponding to r, suppose there is another eigenvector w with the same eigenvalue, linearly independent of v. The vectors v and w can be chosen to be real, because A and r are both real, so the null space of A-rI has a basis consisting of real vectors. Assume at least one of the components of w is positive (otherwise multiply w by -1). Given maximal possible \alpha such that u=v-\alpha w is nonnegative, one of the components of u is zero, otherwise \alpha is not maximum. Vector u is a nonzero eigenvector. It is nonnegative, hence by the positivity argument above it is strictly positive. On the other hand, at least one component of u is zero. The contradiction implies that such a w does not exist.

There are no nontrivial Jordan blocks corresponding to the Perron–Frobenius eigenvalue r or any other eigenvalue with the same absolute value. If there is such a Jordan block, then the infinity norm \|(A/r)^k\|_\infty tends to infinity as k\to\infty, but that contradicts the existence of the positive eigenvector.

Normalize r=1 by replacing A by A/r. Letting v be a Perron–Frobenius strictly positive eigenvector, so Av=v, we have

\|v\|_\infty=\|A^kv\|_\infty \geq\|A^k\|_\infty\min_i v_i, \qquad \|A^k\|_\infty\leq\frac{\|v\|_\infty}{\min_i v_i}.

So \|A^k\|_\infty is bounded for all k. This contradicts the existence of a nontrivial Jordan block for any eigenvalue of absolute value 1 (in particular for the Perron–Frobenius one). For a two by two block,

J^k=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda&1\\0&\lambda\end{pmatrix}^k =\begin{pmatrix}\lambda^k&k\lambda^{k-1}\\0&\lambda^k\end{pmatrix},

hence \|J^k\|_\infty=1+k for |\lambda|=1, so it tends to infinity when k does so. Larger nontrivial Jordan blocks have the same unbounded entries. If J=C^{-1}AC is the Jordan form, then

\|A^k\|_\infty\geq \frac{\|J^k\|_\infty}{\|C^{-1}\|_\infty\|C\|_\infty},

so it also tends to infinity. The resulting contradiction implies that there are no nontrivial Jordan blocks for the corresponding eigenvalues.

Combining the two claims above reveals that the Perron–Frobenius eigenvalue r is a simple root of the characteristic polynomial.
