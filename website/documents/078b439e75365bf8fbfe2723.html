Statement

Let T be an effectively axiomatized arithmetically sound theory satisfying the arithmetic-strength hypotheses of Gödel’s first incompleteness theorem. Then T does not prove every sentence true in the standard natural-number interpretation. Here arithmetical soundness means that every theorem of T is true in that interpretation.

Proof 1: Apply incompleteness to a sound arithmetic theory

Arithmetical soundness makes T consistent. Gödel's first incompleteness theorem shows that for languages sufficient for doing a certain amount of arithmetic, there can be no consistent and effective deductive system that is complete with respect to the intended interpretation of the symbolism of that language. Thus, not all sound deductive systems are complete in this special sense of completeness, in which the class of models (up to isomorphism) is restricted to the intended one. The completeness theorem for first-order logic applies to all classical models, not some special proper subclass of intended ones.

Level justification

The deduction requires effective arithmetic theories, formal provability, standard-model truth and the precise strength assumptions of an incompleteness theorem. Those are substantial logic prerequisites beyond the baseline. Retain Beyond undergraduate provisionally because the companion refers to arithmetic-strength hypotheses without specifying the theory or interpretability condition; the standard extension-of-Robinson-arithmetic case is clear, but the intended full scope should be stated explicitly.

Statement used:

In the standard arithmetic formulation, a consistent computably axiomatized theory extending Robinson arithmetic is syntactically incomplete: some sentence and its negation are both unprovable.

Application in proof:

Soundness implies consistency; of an undecidable sentence and its negation, one is true in the standard natural numbers, so a true sentence remains unproved.

Application level:

Beyond undergraduate level

Level justification:

Applying this result requires effective axiomatization, theory strength and the distinction between syntactic provability and truth in the standard model.

Review notes:

This consistency-only formulation is the Godel–Rosser strengthening. A sound theory also satisfies the stronger soundness requirements used in original Godel formulations. The companion leaves its precise arithmetic-strength condition unnamed beyond reference to the theorem.
