Statement

Let p>q\ge0 be integers. Choose uniformly among the \binom{p+q}{q} words with p letters A and q letters B. The probability that every nonempty prefix contains more A’s than B’s is (p-q)/(p+q); equivalently, there are \binom{p+q}{q}(p-q)/(p+q) such words.

Level
High school only
Level justification

The statement counts finite words and compares prefix letter totals.

Proof 1: Reflect up to the first tie

For A to be strictly ahead of B throughout the counting of the votes, there can be no ties. Separate the counting sequences according to the first vote. Any sequence that begins with a vote for B must reach a tie at some point, because A eventually wins. For any sequence that begins with A and reaches a tie, reflect the votes up to the point of the first tie (so any A becomes a B, and vice versa) to obtain a sequence that begins with B. Hence every sequence that begins with A and reaches a tie is in one-to-one correspondence with a sequence that begins with B, and the probability that a sequence begins with B is {\displaystyle q/(p+q)}, so the probability that A always leads the vote is

{\displaystyle =1-} the probability of sequences that tie at some point

{\displaystyle =1-} the probability of sequences that tie at some point and begin with A or B

{\displaystyle =1-2\times (} the probability of sequences that tie at some point and begin with B {\displaystyle )}

{\displaystyle =1-2\times (} the probability that a sequence begins with B {\displaystyle )}

{\displaystyle =1-2{\frac {q}{p+q}}={\frac {p-q}{p+q}}}
Level
High school only
Level justification

Reflect the initial segment up to the first tie. That segment has equal numbers of A and B, so reflection preserves the prescribed totals and interchanges the two kinds of bad words. Every B-first word must tie before a positive final lead. The bijection and the first-letter probability q/(p+q) give the answer by elementary counting.

Proof 2: Induction on the number of votes

  • We loosen the condition {\displaystyle p>q} to p\ge q\ge0 with p+q>0. Clearly, the theorem is correct when {\displaystyle p=q}, since in this case the first candidate will not be strictly ahead after all the votes have been counted (so the probability is 0).
  • Clearly the theorem is true if p > 0 and q = 0 when the probability is 1, given that the first candidate receives all the votes; it is also true when p = q > 0 as we have just seen.
  • Assume it is true both when p = a − 1 and q = b, and when p = a and q = b − 1, with a > b > 0. (We don't need to consider the case {\displaystyle a=b} here, since we have already disposed of it before.) Then considering the case with p = a and q = b, the last vote counted is either for the first candidate with probability a/(a + b), or for the second with probability b/(a + b). So the probability of the first being ahead throughout the count to the penultimate vote counted (and also after the final vote) is:
{\displaystyle {a \over (a+b)}{(a-1)-b \over (a+b-1)}+{b \over (a+b)}{a-(b-1) \over (a+b-1)}={a-b \over a+b}.}
  • And so it is true for all p and q with p > q > 0.
Level
High school only
Level justification

Induct on the finite total number of votes, handling q=0 and p=q as base cases. Conditional on the final vote, the preceding word is uniform with one fewer vote of that type. The displayed weighted average simplifies to (a-b)/(a+b). Only finite conditional counting, induction and fractions are needed.

Proof 3: Cancel adjacent AB pairs on a circle — outline

Call a ballot sequence dominating if A is strictly ahead of B throughout the counting of the votes. The cycle lemma asserts that any sequence of {\displaystyle p} A's and {\displaystyle q} B's, where {\displaystyle p>q}, has precisely {\displaystyle p-q} dominating cyclic permutations. To see this, just arrange the given sequence of {\displaystyle p+q} A's and B's in a circle and repeatedly remove adjacent pairs AB until only {\displaystyle p-q} A's remain. Each of these A's was the start of a dominating cyclic permutation before anything was removed. So {\displaystyle p-q} out of the {\displaystyle p+q} cyclic permutations of any arrangement of {\displaystyle p} A votes and {\displaystyle q} B votes are dominating.

Cyclic permutations here are counted by their starting positions, even when the resulting words agree.

Level
High school only
Level justification

The supplied circle-cancellation argument counts p-q favorable starting positions among p+q positions. All objects are finite words and adjacent-letter operations; periodic words are correctly counted with position multiplicity. Uniformly choosing a word and a marked starting position makes the rotated word uniform, so the position proportion is the required probability. No probability limit or abstract group-action theorem is needed.

Proof 4: Stop the backwards-counting martingale

Let {\displaystyle n=p+q}. Define the "backwards counting" stochastic process

{\displaystyle X_{k}={\frac {S_{n-k}}{n-k}};\quad k=0,1,...,n-1}

where {\displaystyle S_{n-k}} is the lead of candidate A over B, after {\displaystyle n-k} votes have come in.

With the filtration generated by the backwards observations, the claim is: {\displaystyle X_{k}} is a martingale process.

Given {\displaystyle X_{k}}, we know that {\displaystyle S_{n-k}=(n-k)X_{k}}, so of the first {\displaystyle n-k} votes, {\displaystyle {\frac {X_{k}+1}{2}}(n-k)} were for candidate A, and {\displaystyle {\frac {-X_{k}+1}{2}}(n-k)} were for candidate B.

So, with probability {\displaystyle {\frac {X_{k}+1}{2}}}, we have {\displaystyle S_{n-k-1}=S_{n-k}-1}, and {\displaystyle X_{k+1}={\frac {n-k}{n-k-1}}X_{k}-{\frac {1}{n-k-1}}}. The B case has the opposite sign in the final term. For 0\le k\le n-2, compute to find {\displaystyle E[X_{k+1}|X_{k}]=X_{k}}.

Define the stopping time {\displaystyle T} as either the minimum {\displaystyle k} such that {\displaystyle X_{k}=0}, or {\displaystyle n-1} if there's no such {\displaystyle k}. Then the probability that candidate A leads all the time is just {\displaystyle E[X_{T}]}, which by the optional stopping theorem is {\displaystyle E[X_{T}]=E[X_{0}]}. Using the final lead as {\displaystyle S_{n}}, and the definition of {\displaystyle X_{k}} at 0, {\displaystyle E[X_{0}]={\frac {p-q}{p+q}}}.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

The sample space is the finite set of ballot words. Conditional on the backwards-revealed suffix, remaining positions are uniformly ordered with known counts, so the displayed conditional mean makes X a martingale for the full backwards filtration. A bounded stopping time then gives the expectation identity. Learning finite conditional averages, a martingale and bounded stopping is a small extension of undergraduate probability; general measure-theoretic stochastic processes are unnecessary.

Proof 5: André’s delete-and-interchange bijection

If q=0, the probability is one. Suppose q>0.

The argument shows that the "unfavourable" sequences (those that reach an intermediate tie) consist of an equal number of sequences that begin with A as those that begin with B. Every sequence that begins with B is unfavourable, and there are {\displaystyle {\tbinom {p+q-1}{q-1}}} such sequences with a B followed by an arbitrary sequence of (q-1) B's and p A's. Each unfavourable sequence that begins with A can be transformed to an arbitrary sequence of (q-1) B's and p A's by finding the first B that violates the rule (by causing the vote counts to tie) and deleting it, and interchanging the order of the remaining parts. To reverse the process, take any sequence of (q-1) B's and p A's and search from the end to find where the number of A's first exceeds the number of B's, and then interchange the order of the parts and place a B in between. For example, the unfavourable sequence AABBABAA corresponds uniquely to the arbitrary sequence ABAAAAB. From this, it follows that the number of favourable sequences of p A's and q B's is

{\displaystyle {\binom {p+q}{q}}-2{\binom {p+q-1}{q-1}}={\binom {p+q}{q}}{\frac {p-q}{p+q}}}

and thus the required probability is

{\displaystyle {\frac {p-q}{p+q}}}
Level
High school only
Level justification

Delete the first tie-causing B from an A-first bad word and interchange the two remaining pieces. The inverse finds the shortest suffix with one more A than B, moves it to the front and inserts the deleted B. This gives the stated finite bijection, after which binomial counting and subtraction finish the proof. Only finite word manipulation and elementary algebra are used.

Proof 6: Condition on the first vote

For a\ge b\ge0, consider the weak ballot problem for a A’s and b B’s. If b=0, its probability is one; suppose b>0.

The variant problem can be solved by the reflection method in a similar way to the strict ballot problem. The number of possible vote sequences is {\displaystyle {\tbinom {a+b}{b}}}. Call a sequence "bad" if the second candidate is ever ahead, and if the number of bad sequences can be enumerated then the number of "good" sequences can be found by subtraction and the probability can be computed.

Represent a voting sequence as a North-East lattice path on the Cartesian plane as follows:

  • Start the path at (0, 0)
  • Each time a vote for the first candidate is received move right 1 unit.
  • Each time a vote for the second candidate is received move up 1 unit.

Each such path corresponds to a unique sequence of votes and will end at (a, b). A sequence is 'good' exactly when the corresponding path never goes above the diagonal line y = x; equivalently, a sequence is 'bad' exactly when the corresponding path touches the line y = x + 1.

For each 'bad' path P, define a new path P′ by reflecting the part of P up to the first point it touches the line across it. P′ is a path from (−1, 1) to (a, b). The same operation applied again restores the original P. This produces a one-to-one correspondence between the 'bad' paths and the paths from (−1, 1) to (a, b). The number of these paths is {\displaystyle {\tbinom {a+b}{b-1}}} and so that is the number of 'bad' sequences. This leaves the number of 'good' sequences as

{\displaystyle {\binom {a+b}{b}}-{\binom {a+b}{b-1}}={\binom {a+b}{b}}{\frac {a+1-b}{a+1}}.}

Since there are {\displaystyle {\tbinom {a+b}{b}}} altogether, the probability of a sequence being good is {\displaystyle {\tfrac {a+1-b}{a+1}}}.

In fact, the solutions to the original problem and the variant problem are easily related. For candidate A to be strictly ahead throughout the vote count, they must receive the first vote and for the remaining votes (ignoring the first) they must be either strictly ahead or tied throughout the count. Hence the solution to the original problem is

{\displaystyle {\frac {p}{p+q}}{\frac {p-1+1-q}{p-1+1}}={\frac {p-q}{p+q}}}
Level
High school only
Level justification

Reflect the initial segment of a bad lattice path across y=x+1 to count weak-ballot failures, then condition the strict problem on its necessary first A vote. The reflection is a finite geometric bijection, and the probability is obtained by binomial ratios. Elementary lattice geometry, counting and conditional probabilities suffice.
