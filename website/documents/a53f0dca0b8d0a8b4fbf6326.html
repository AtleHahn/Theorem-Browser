Statement

Let (X,\mathcal F,\mu) be a measure space and f_n:X\to[0,\infty] measurable. Then f=\liminf_n f_n is measurable and \int_X f\,d\mu\le\liminf_n\int_X f_n\,d\mu, allowing infinite values. The conclusion also holds for measurable extended-real f_n nonnegative outside a common null set, with null-set values immaterial.

Level
Beyond undergraduate level
Level justification

The statement concerns measurable functions and measure integrals, beyond the calculus/linear-algebra baseline.

Proof 1: Apply monotone convergence to tail infima

Let {\displaystyle \textstyle g_{n}(x)=\inf _{k\geq n}f_{k}(x)}. Then:

  1. the sequence {\displaystyle \{g_{n}(x)\}_{n}} is pointwise non-decreasing at any x and
  2. {\displaystyle g_{n}\leq f_{n}}, {\displaystyle \forall n\in \mathbb {N} }.

Since

{\displaystyle f(x)=\liminf _{n\to \infty }f_{n}(x)=\sup _{n}\inf _{k\geq n}f_{k}(x)=\sup _{n}g_{n}(x)},

and countable infima and suprema of measurable functions are measurable we see that {\displaystyle f} is measurable.

By the Monotone Convergence Theorem and property (1), the sup and integral may be interchanged:

{\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}\int _{X}f\,d\mu &=\int _{X}\sup _{n}g_{n}\,d\mu \\&=\sup _{n}\int _{X}g_{n}\,d\mu \\&=\liminf _{n\to \infty }\int _{X}g_{n}\,d\mu \\&\leq \liminf _{n\to \infty }\int _{X}f_{n}\,d\mu ,\end{aligned}}}

where the last step used property (2).

For functions nonnegative outside a common null set, replace their values there by zero; none of the integrals changes.

Level justification

Proof 2: Approximate by simple functions and increasing level sets

Set g_n=\inf_{k\ge n}f_k and f=\sup_n g_n. To demonstrate that the monotone convergence theorem is not "hidden", the proof below does not use any properties of Lebesgue integral except those established here and the fact that the functions {\displaystyle f} and {\displaystyle g_{n}} are measurable.

Denote by {\displaystyle \operatorname {SF} (f)} the set of simple {\displaystyle ({\mathcal {F}},\mathcal B_{[0,+\infty]})}-measurable functions {\displaystyle s:X\to [0,\infty )} such that {\displaystyle 0\leq s\leq f} on {\displaystyle X}.

Monotonicity—

  • If {\displaystyle f\leq g} everywhere on {\displaystyle X,} then
{\displaystyle \int _{X}f\,d\mu \leq \int _{X}g\,d\mu .}
  • If {\displaystyle X_{1},X_{2}\in {\mathcal {F}}} and {\displaystyle X_{1}\subseteq X_{2},} then
{\displaystyle \int _{X_{1}}f\,d\mu \leq \int _{X_{2}}f\,d\mu .}
  • For a nonnegative simple function s and measurable sets S_n\uparrow X, \int_{S_n}s\,d\mu\to\int_Xs\,d\mu.

Proof

1. Since {\displaystyle f\leq g,} we have

{\displaystyle \operatorname {SF} (f)\subseteq \operatorname {SF} (g).}

By definition of Lebesgue integral and the properties of supremum,

{\displaystyle \int _{X}f\,d\mu =\sup _{s\in {\rm {SF}}(f)}\int _{X}s\,d\mu \leq \sup _{s\in {\rm {SF}}(g)}\int _{X}s\,d\mu =\int _{X}g\,d\mu .}

2. Let {\displaystyle {\mathbf {1} }_{X_{1}}} be the indicator function of the set {\displaystyle X_{1}.} It can be deduced from the definition of Lebesgue integral that

{\displaystyle \int _{X_{2}}f\cdot {\mathbf {1} }_{X_{1}}\,d\mu =\int _{X_{1}}f\,d\mu }

if we notice that, for every {\displaystyle s\in {\rm {SF}}(f\cdot {\mathbf {1} }_{X_{1}}),} {\displaystyle s=0} outside of {\displaystyle X_{1}.} Combined with the previous property, the inequality {\displaystyle f\cdot {\mathbf {1} }_{X_{1}}\leq f} implies

{\displaystyle \int _{X_{1}}f\,d\mu =\int _{X_{2}}f\cdot {\mathbf {1} }_{X_{1}}\,d\mu \leq \int _{X_{2}}f\,d\mu .}

3. First note that the claim holds if the function is an indicator function of a set, by continuity from below of measures. By linearity, this also immediately implies the claim for simple functions.

Now we turn to the main theorem

Step 1—{\displaystyle g_{n}=g_{n}(x)} is {\displaystyle ({\mathcal {F}},\mathcal B_{[0,+\infty]})}-measurable, for every {\displaystyle n\geq 1}, as is {\displaystyle f}.

Proof

Recall the closed intervals generate the Borel σ-algebra. Thus it suffices to show, for every {\displaystyle t\in [-\infty ,+\infty ]}, that {\displaystyle g_{n}^{-1}([t,+\infty ])\in {\mathcal {F}}}. Now observe that

{\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}g_{n}^{-1}([t,+\infty ])&=\left\{x\in X\mid g_{n}(x)\geq t\right\}\\[3pt]&=\left\{x\in X\;\left|\;\inf _{k\,\geq \,n}f_{k}(x)\geq t\right.\right\}\\[3pt]&=\bigcap _{k\,\geq \,n}\left\{x\in X\mid f_{k}(x)\geq t\right\}\\[3pt]&=\bigcap _{k\,\geq \,n}f_{k}^{-1}([t,+\infty ])\end{aligned}}}

Every set on the right-hand side is from {\displaystyle {\mathcal {F}}}, which is closed under countable intersections. Thus the left-hand side is also a member of {\displaystyle {\mathcal {F}}}.

Similarly, it is enough to verify that {\displaystyle f^{-1}([0,t])\in {\mathcal {F}}}, for every {\displaystyle t\in [-\infty ,+\infty ]}. Since the sequence {\displaystyle \{g_{n}(x)\}} pointwise non-decreases,

{\displaystyle f^{-1}([0,t])=\bigcap _{n}g_{n}^{-1}([0,t])\in {\mathcal {F}}}.

Step 2—Given a simple function {\displaystyle s\in \operatorname {SF} (f)} and a real number {\displaystyle t\in (0,1)}, define

{\displaystyle B_{k}^{s,t}=\{x\in X\mid t\cdot s(x)\leq g_{k}(x)\}\subseteq X.}

Then {\displaystyle B_{k}^{s,t}\in {\mathcal {F}}}, {\displaystyle B_{k}^{s,t}\subseteq B_{k+1}^{s,t}}, and {\displaystyle \textstyle X=\bigcup _{k}B_{k}^{s,t}}.

Proof

Step 2a. To prove the first claim, write s as a weighted sum of indicator functions of disjoint measurable sets forming a partition of X (including the zero level):

{\displaystyle s=\sum _{i=1}^{m}c_{i}\cdot \mathbf {1} _{A_{i}}}.

Then

{\displaystyle B_{k}^{s,t}=\bigcup _{i=1}^{m}{\Bigl (}g_{k}^{-1}{\Bigl (}[t\cdot c_{i},+\infty ]{\Bigr )}\cap A_{i}{\Bigr )}}.

Since the pre-image {\displaystyle g_{k}^{-1}{\Bigl (}[t\cdot c_{i},+\infty ]{\Bigr )}} of the Borel set {\displaystyle [t\cdot c_{i},+\infty ]} under the measurable function {\displaystyle g_{k}} is measurable, and {\displaystyle \sigma }-algebras are closed under finite intersection and unions, the first claim follows.

Step 2b. To prove the second claim, note that, for each {\displaystyle k} and every {\displaystyle x\in X}, {\displaystyle g_{k}(x)\leq g_{k+1}(x).}

Step 2c. To prove the third claim, suppose for contradiction there exists

{\displaystyle x_{0}\in X\setminus \bigcup _{k}B_{k}^{s,t}=\bigcap _{k}(X\setminus B_{k}^{s,t})}

Then {\displaystyle 0\leq g_{k}(x_{0})<t\cdot s(x_{0})<s(x_{0})}, for every {\displaystyle k}. Taking the limit as {\displaystyle k\to \infty },

{\displaystyle f(x_{0})\leq t\cdot s(x_{0})<s(x_{0}).}

This contradicts our initial assumption that {\displaystyle s\leq f}.

Step 3—From Step 2 and the simple-function continuity proved in property 3,

{\displaystyle \lim _{n}\int _{B_{n}^{s,t}}s\,d\mu =\int _{X}s\,d\mu .}

Step 4—For every {\displaystyle s\in \operatorname {SF} (f)},

{\displaystyle \int _{X}s\,d\mu \leq \lim _{k}\int _{X}g_{k}\,d\mu }.

Proof

Indeed, using the definition of {\displaystyle B_{k}^{s,t}}, the non-negativity of {\displaystyle g_{k}}, and the monotonicity of Lebesgue integral, we have

{\displaystyle \forall k\geq 1\qquad \int _{B_{k}^{s,t}}t\cdot s\,d\mu \leq \int _{B_{k}^{s,t}}g_{k}\,d\mu \leq \int _{X}g_{k}\,d\mu }.

In accordance with Step 3, as {\displaystyle k\to \infty } the inequality becomes

{\displaystyle t\int _{X}s\,d\mu \leq \lim _{k}\int _{X}g_{k}\,d\mu }.

Taking the limit as {\displaystyle t\uparrow 1} yields

{\displaystyle \int _{X}s\,d\mu \leq \lim _{k}\int _{X}g_{k}\,d\mu },

as required.

Step 5—To complete the proof, we apply the definition of Lebesgue integral to the inequality established in Step 4 and take into account that {\displaystyle g_{n}\leq f_{n}}:

{\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}\int _{X}f\,d\mu &=\sup _{s\in \operatorname {SF} (f)}\int _{X}s\,d\mu \\&\leq \lim _{k}\int _{X}g_{k}\,d\mu \\&=\liminf _{k}\int _{X}g_{k}\,d\mu \\&\leq \liminf _{k}\int _{X}f_{k}\,d\mu \end{aligned}}}

The proof is complete.

For functions nonnegative outside a common null set, replace their values there by zero; none of the integrals changes.
