Statement

The improper integral satisfies \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\cos x}{1+x^2}\,dx=\frac\pi e.

Level

Level justification

Understanding the statement involves real improper integrals; cosine; rational functions. These prerequisites fit undergraduate coursework or a brief extension of it. A bright US college student should be able to understand this statement after approximately 30 minutes of focused background research; this assesses understanding the claim, not its proof.

Proof 1: One simple residue and a vanishing upper arc

The function

{\displaystyle f(z)={\frac {e^{iz}}{1+z^{2}}},\qquad z\in {\mathbb {C} }\setminus \{i,-i\},}

is of the form e^{iaz}g(z) with a=1 and g(z)=1/(1+z^2), and satisfies the condition of Jordan's lemma for all R > 0 with R ≠ 1. Note that, for R > 1,

{\displaystyle M_{R}=\max _{\theta \in [0,\pi ]}{\frac {1}{|1+R^{2}e^{2i\theta }|}}={\frac {1}{R^{2}-1}}\,,}

hence M_R\to0 as R\to\infty, so Jordan's lemma makes the upper-semicircle integral tend to zero. Since the only singularity of f in the upper half plane is at z = i, the residue theorem on the upper semicircle, followed by this limit, yields

{\displaystyle \int _{-\infty }^{\infty }{\frac {e^{ix}}{1+x^{2}}}\,dx=2\pi i\,\operatorname {Res} (f,i)\,.}

Since z = i is a simple pole of f and 1 + z2 = (z + i)(z − i), we obtain

{\displaystyle \operatorname {Res} (f,i)=\lim _{z\to i}(z-i)f(z)=\lim _{z\to i}{\frac {e^{iz}}{z+i}}={\frac {e^{-1}}{2i}}}

so that

{\displaystyle \int _{-\infty }^{\infty }{\frac {\cos x}{1+x^{2}}}\,dx=\operatorname {Re} \int _{-\infty }^{\infty }{\frac {e^{ix}}{1+x^{2}}}\,dx={\frac {\pi }{e}}\,.}

The real and complex integrands on the real axis are bounded in absolute value by 1/(1+x^2), so their improper integrals converge absolutely.
