Statement

Let \mu be standard Gaussian measure on \mathbb R^n and A measurable with \mu(A)>0. For \epsilon>0, set A_\epsilon=\{x:d(x,A)<\epsilon\}. Then \mu(A_\epsilon)\ge1-\frac{e^{-\epsilon^2/4}}{\mu(A)}.

Level
Beyond undergraduate level
Level justification

The precise measurable-function and probability-density statements require measure theory.

Proof 1: Bound the integral on the neighborhood complement

The proof goes by way of the Gaussian exponential-distance lemma:

\int_{\mathbb R^n}\exp(d(x,A)^2/4)\,d\mu\leq1/\mu(A).

To conclude the concentration inequality from the lemma, note that on \mathbb R^n\setminus A_\epsilon, d(x,A)\geq\epsilon, so we have

\int_{\mathbb R^n}\exp(d(x,A)^2/4)\,d\mu\geq(1-\mu(A_\epsilon))\exp(\epsilon^2/4).

Applying the lemma and rearranging proves the result.
