For m\ge1 and A\in M_m(\mathbb C), there are \lambda\in\mathbb C and v\in\mathbb C^m\setminus\{0\} with Av=\lambda v.

Proof 1: Integrate the resolvent around a large circle

Let A be a complex square matrix of size m > 0 and let Im be the unit matrix of the same size. Assume A has no eigenvalues. Let c(r) denote the counterclockwise circle centered at zero with radius r. Consider the resolvent function

{\displaystyle R(z)=(zI_{m}-A)^{-1},}

which is a meromorphic function on the complex plane with values in the vector space of matrices. The eigenvalues of A are precisely the poles of R(z). Since, by assumption, A has no eigenvalues, the function R(z) is an entire function and Cauchy theorem implies that

{\displaystyle \int _{c(r)}R(z)\,dz=0.}

On the other hand, R(z) expanded as a geometric series gives:

{\displaystyle R(z)=z^{-1}(I_{m}-z^{-1}A)^{-1}=z^{-1}\sum _{k=0}^{\infty }{\frac {1}{z^{k}}}A^{k}.}

This formula is valid outside the closed disc of radius {\displaystyle \|A\|} (the operator norm of A). Let {\displaystyle r>\|A\|.} The series converges uniformly on c(r), so it can be integrated term by term. Then

{\displaystyle \int _{c(r)}R(z)dz=\sum _{k=0}^{\infty }\int _{c(r)}{\frac {dz}{z^{k+1}}}A^{k}=2\pi iI_{m}}

(in which only the summand k = 0 has a nonzero integral). This is a contradiction, and so A has an eigenvalue.
