Statement

Let R be a GCD domain and f,g nonzero polynomials in R[x_1,\ldots,x_n]. If c(h) denotes a gcd of the coefficients, then c(fg) is associated to c(f)c(g).

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Introductory ring theory supplies polynomial division, gcds, units, irreducibility and characteristic. A fraction field uses ordinary fractions over a domain; content is the gcd of coefficients. These closely related definitions suffice without advanced commutative algebra.

Proof 1: Multiplicativity of polynomial content over a GCD domain — outline

Write \operatorname{cont}(h) for the ideal generated by the coefficients of h, and \operatorname{gcd}(I) for the principal ideal generated by a gcd of the generators of I. Thus \operatorname{gcd}(\operatorname{cont}(h))=(c(h)).

Factoring out the gcd's from the coefficients, we can write {\displaystyle f=af'} and {\displaystyle g=bg'} where the gcds of the coefficients of {\displaystyle f',g'} are both 1. Clearly, it is enough to prove the assertion when {\displaystyle f,g} are replaced by {\displaystyle f',g'}; thus, we assume the gcd's of the coefficients of {\displaystyle f,g} are both 1. We need the following simple lemma about gcd:

  • If {\displaystyle \gcd(a,b)=\gcd(a,c)=1}, then {\displaystyle \gcd(a,bc)=1}.(The proof of the lemma is not trivial but is by elementary algebra.)

We argue by induction on the sum of the numbers of the terms in {\displaystyle f,g}; that is, we assume the proposition has been established for any pair of polynomials with one less total number of the terms. Let {\displaystyle (c)=\gcd(\operatorname {cont} (fg))}; i.e., {\displaystyle c} is the gcd of the coefficients of {\displaystyle fg}. Assume {\displaystyle (c)\neq (1)}; otherwise, we are done. Let {\displaystyle f_{0},g_{0}} denote the highest-degree terms of {\displaystyle f,g} in terms of lexicographical monomial ordering. Then {\displaystyle f_{0}g_{0}} is precisely the leading term of {\displaystyle fg} and so {\displaystyle c} divides the (unique) coefficient of {\displaystyle f_{0}g_{0}} (since it divides all the coefficients of {\displaystyle fg}). Now, if {\displaystyle c} does not have a nonunit common factor with the (unique) coefficient of {\displaystyle f_{0}} and does not have a nonunit common factor with that of {\displaystyle g_{0}}, then, by the above lemma, {\displaystyle \gcd(c,\operatorname {cont} (f_{0}g_{0}))=(1)}. But {\displaystyle c} divides the coefficient of {\displaystyle f_{0}g_{0}}; so this is a contradiction. Thus, either {\displaystyle c} has a nonunit common factor with the coefficient of {\displaystyle f_{0}} or does with that of {\displaystyle g_{0}}; say, the former is the case. Here \operatorname{gcd}(c,I) means \operatorname{gcd}((c)+I). Let {\displaystyle (d)=\operatorname {gcd} (c,\operatorname {cont} (f_{0}))}. Since {\displaystyle d} divides the coefficients of {\displaystyle fg-f_{0}g=(f-f_{0})g}, by inductive hypothesis,

{\displaystyle (d)\supset \operatorname {gcd} (\operatorname {cont} ((f-f_{0})g))=\operatorname {gcd} (\operatorname {cont} (f-f_{0}))\operatorname {gcd} (\operatorname {cont} (g))=\operatorname {gcd} (\operatorname {cont} (f-f_{0}))}.

Since {\displaystyle (d)} contains {\displaystyle \operatorname {cont} (f_{0})}, it contains {\displaystyle \operatorname {cont} (f)}; i.e., {\displaystyle (d)=(1)}, a contradiction. {\displaystyle \square }
