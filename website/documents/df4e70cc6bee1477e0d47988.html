Statement

Let ABC be a nondegenerate Euclidean triangle with a=|BC|, b=|CA|, c=|AB| and opposite interior angles \alpha,\beta,\gamma. Then c^2=a^2+b^2-2ab\cos\gamma.

Level

Level justification

The statement and argument concern elementary Euclidean triangles, trigonometry and algebra.

Proof 1: Expand the difference norm and substitute the angle formula

Place \mathbf u=\overrightarrow{CB} and \mathbf v=\overrightarrow{CA}, so that \|\mathbf u\|=a, \|\mathbf v\|=b and \|\mathbf u-\mathbf v\|=c. The basic relation between the norm and the dot product is

\|\mathbf v\|^2=\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf v.

Then

\begin{aligned} \|\mathbf u+\mathbf v\|^2 &=(\mathbf u+\mathbf v)\cdot(\mathbf u+\mathbf v)\\ &=(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf u)+(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v)+(\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf u)+(\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf v)\\ &=\|\mathbf u\|^2+\|\mathbf v\|^2+2(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v), \end{aligned}

and similarly

\|\mathbf u-\mathbf v\|^2=\|\mathbf u\|^2+\|\mathbf v\|^2-2(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v).

This is essentially a vector form of the law of cosines for the triangle formed by the vectors \mathbf u, \mathbf v, and \mathbf u-\mathbf v. In particular,

\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v=\|\mathbf u\|\,\|\mathbf v\|\cos\gamma,

where \gamma is the angle between \mathbf u and \mathbf v. Substitution gives

c^2=a^2+b^2-2ab\cos\gamma.

High school only
Level justification

Expand a planar dot-product square and insert the angle formula. Elementary coordinate vectors, algebra and trigonometry suffice; no abstract inner-product-space theory is needed.

Proof 2: Subtract two Pythagorean identities outline

Case of an obtuse angle. Drop the perpendicular BH to the line AC; H lies beyond C away from A. Apply the Pythagorean theorem to each of the two right triangles AHB and CHB. Using a to denote the line segment CB, b to denote the line segment AC, c to denote the line segment AB, d to denote the line segment CH and h for the height BH, triangle AHB gives us

{\displaystyle c^{2}=(b+d)^{2}+h^{2},}

and triangle CHB gives

{\displaystyle d^{2}+h^{2}=a^{2}.}

Expanding the first equation gives

{\displaystyle c^{2}=b^{2}+2bd+d^{2}+h^{2}.}

Substituting the second equation into this, the following can be obtained:

{\displaystyle c^{2}=a^{2}+b^{2}+2bd.}

To transform this into the law of cosines, note that

{\displaystyle d=a\cos(\pi -\gamma )=-a\cos \gamma .}

Case of an acute angle.

The proof proceeds along the same lines: apply the Pythagorean theorem to both right triangles formed by dropping the perpendicular onto one of the sides enclosing the angle γ and use the square of a difference to simplify.

Here d=a\cos\gamma, so the two equations are c^2=(b-d)^2+h^2 and a^2=d^2+h^2. If H lies beyond A, the length is d-b instead of b-d, whose square is unchanged. If \gamma is right, the identity is the Pythagorean theorem itself.

High school only
Level justification

Proof 3: Coordinate distance and the sine–cosine identity

Consider a triangle with sides of length a, b, c, where γ is the measurement of the angle opposite the side of length c. This triangle can be placed on the Cartesian coordinate system with side a aligned along the x axis and angle γ placed at the origin, by plotting the components of the 3 points of the triangle :

{\displaystyle A=(b\cos \gamma ,b\sin \gamma ),B=(a,0),{\text{ and }}C=(0,0).}

By the distance formula,

{\displaystyle c={\sqrt {(a-b\cos \gamma )^{2}+(0-b\sin \gamma )^{2}}}.}

Squaring both sides and simplifying

{\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}c^{2}&=(a-b\cos \gamma )^{2}+(-b\sin \gamma )^{2}\\&=a^{2}-2ab\cos \gamma +b^{2}\cos ^{2}\gamma +b^{2}\sin ^{2}\gamma \\&=a^{2}+b^{2}(\sin ^{2}\gamma +\cos ^{2}\gamma )-2ab\cos \gamma \\&=a^{2}+b^{2}-2ab\cos \gamma .\end{aligned}}}

An advantage of this proof is that it does not require the consideration of separate cases depending on whether the angle γ is acute, right, or obtuse.

Level
High school only
Level justification

Place the vertices at the given coordinates, apply the distance formula and simplify with sine squared plus cosine squared equal to one. High-school coordinate geometry suffices.

Proof 4: Eliminate the three altitude projections

The altitude through vertex C is a segment perpendicular to the line containing side c. The directed distance from the foot of the altitude to vertex A plus the directed distance from the foot of the altitude to vertex B is equal to the length of side c . Each of these distances can be written as one of the other sides multiplied by the cosine of the adjacent angle,

{\displaystyle c=a\cos \beta +b\cos \alpha .}

(This is still true if α or β is obtuse, in which case the perpendicular falls outside the triangle.) Multiplying both sides by c yields

{\displaystyle c^{2}=ac\cos \beta +bc\cos \alpha .}

The same steps work just as well when treating either of the other sides as the base of the triangle:

{\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}a^{2}&=ac\cos \beta +ab\cos \gamma ,\\[3mu]b^{2}&=bc\cos \alpha +ab\cos \gamma .\end{aligned}}}

Taking the equation for ⁠{\displaystyle c^{2}}⁠ and subtracting the equations for ⁠{\displaystyle b^{2}}⁠ and ⁠{\displaystyle a^{2}}⁠,

{\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}c^{2}-a^{2}-b^{2}&={ac\cos \beta }+{bc\cos \alpha }-{ac\cos \beta }-{bc\cos \alpha }-2ab\cos \gamma \\c^{2}&=a^{2}+b^{2}-2ab\cos \gamma .\end{aligned}}}

Level justification

Proof 5: Signed secant lengths and power of a point outline

First suppose \gamma is obtuse. This proof uses the power of a point theorem directly, without the auxiliary triangles obtained by constructing a tangent or a chord. Construct a circle with center B and radius a which intersects the secant through A and C in C and K. The chord has length CK=2a\cos(\pi-\gamma), so AK=b+2a\cos(\pi-\gamma). The power of the point A with respect to the circle is equal to both AB2 − BC2 and AC·AK. Therefore,

{\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}c^{2}-a^{2}&{}=b(b+2a\cos(\pi -\gamma ))\\&{}=b(b-2a\cos \gamma ),\end{aligned}}}

which is the law of cosines.

Using algebraic measures for line segments (allowing negative numbers as lengths of segments) the case of obtuse angle (CK > 0) and acute angle (CK < 0) can be treated simultaneously.

For directed lengths, orient the line from A towards C. Then CK=-2a\cos\gamma and AK=b-2a\cos\gamma, so the same power product applies. If \gamma is right, the result is the Pythagorean theorem.

Level justification

Proof 6: Sine addition identities and the sine law

The law of cosines can be proven algebraically from the law of sines and a few standard trigonometric identities. To start, three angles of a triangle sum to a straight angle ({\displaystyle \alpha +\beta +\gamma =\pi } radians). Thus by the angle sum identities for sine and cosine,

{\displaystyle {\begin{alignedat}{3}\sin \gamma &={\phantom {-}}\sin(\pi -\gamma )&&={\phantom {-}}\sin(\alpha +\beta )&&=\sin \alpha \,\cos \beta +\cos \alpha \,\sin \beta ,\\[5mu]\cos \gamma &=-\cos(\pi -\gamma )&&=-\cos(\alpha +\beta )&&=\sin \alpha \,\sin \beta -\cos \alpha \,\cos \beta .\end{alignedat}}}

Squaring the first of these identities, then substituting ${\displaystyle \cos \alpha ,\cos \beta ={}} $ {\displaystyle \sin \alpha \,\sin \beta -\cos \gamma } from the second, and finally replacing ${\displaystyle \cos ^{2}\alpha +\sin ^{2}\alpha ={}} $ {\displaystyle \cos ^{2}\beta +\sin ^{2}\beta =1,} the Pythagorean trigonometric identity, we have:

{\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}\sin ^{2}\gamma &=(\sin \alpha \,\cos \beta +\cos \alpha \,\sin \beta )^{2}\\[3mu]&=\sin ^{2}\alpha \,\cos ^{2}\beta +2\sin \alpha \,\sin \beta \,\cos \alpha \,\cos \beta +\cos ^{2}\alpha \,\sin ^{2}\beta \\[3mu]&=\sin ^{2}\alpha \,\cos ^{2}\beta +2\sin \alpha \,\sin \beta (\sin \alpha \,\sin \beta -\cos \gamma )+\cos ^{2}\alpha \,\sin ^{2}\beta \\[3mu]&=\sin ^{2}\alpha (\cos ^{2}\beta +\sin ^{2}\beta )+\sin ^{2}\beta (\cos ^{2}\alpha +\sin ^{2}\alpha )-2\sin \alpha \,\sin \beta \,\cos \gamma \\[3mu]&=\sin ^{2}\alpha +\sin ^{2}\beta -2\sin \alpha \,\sin \beta \,\cos \gamma .\end{aligned}}}

The law of sines holds that

{\displaystyle {\frac {a}{\sin \alpha {\vphantom {\beta }}}}={\frac {b}{\sin \beta }}={\frac {c}{\sin \gamma {\vphantom {\beta }}}}=k,}

so to prove the law of cosines, we multiply both sides of our previous identity by ⁠{\displaystyle k^{2}}⁠:

{\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}\sin ^{2}\gamma {\frac {c^{2}}{\sin ^{2}\gamma }}&=\sin ^{2}\alpha {\frac {a^{2}}{\sin ^{2}\alpha }}+\sin ^{2}\beta {\frac {b^{2}}{\sin ^{2}\beta }}-2\sin \alpha \,\sin \beta \,\cos \gamma {\frac {ab}{\sin \alpha \,\sin \beta {\vphantom {\sin ^{2}}}}}\\[10mu]c^{2}&=a^{2}+b^{2}-2ab\cos \gamma .\end{aligned}}}

This concludes the proof.
