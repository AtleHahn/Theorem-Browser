Statement

Let R be a commutative Noetherian ring and S\subseteq R[X_0,\ldots,X_{n-1}]. The common zero set V(S)\subseteq R^n is the common zero set of finitely many polynomials.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Introductory ring theory supplies ideals and multivariate polynomials. Noetherian means that every ideal is finitely generated, and the zero set is the set of simultaneous solutions. These two directly related definitions suffice to understand the assertion, without algebraic geometry.

Proof 1: Replace the defining ideal by finitely many generators

By induction using Hilbert’s basis theorem we see that {\displaystyle R[X_{0},\dotsc ,X_{n-1}]} will also be Noetherian.

Since any algebraic locus over {\displaystyle R^{n}} (i.e. a locus-set of a collection of polynomials) may be written as the locus of an ideal {\displaystyle {\mathfrak {a}}\subset R[X_{0},\dotsc ,X_{n-1}]} and further as the locus of its generators, it follows that every algebraic locus is the locus of finitely many polynomials — i.e. the intersection of finitely many hypersurfaces.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Starting with rings and ideals, the only additions are the Noetherian condition (every ideal finitely generated) and common polynomial zero sets. Hilbert’s basis theorem gives the condition for the finite-variable polynomial ring. A point annihilates every ideal element exactly when it annihilates a generating family. These closely related notions fit a brief extension of undergraduate abstract algebra; scheme theory or algebraic geometry machinery is unnecessary.

Proof 1 — Replace the defining ideal by finitely many generators

Statement used:

If R is a commutative Noetherian ring, then R[X_0,...,X_(n-1)] is Noetherian for every finite n.

Application in proof:

Obtain finite generators of the ideal generated by S, whose common zero set is V(S).
