Statement

Let k be an algebraically closed field, let X be an algebraic variety over k, and let f:X\to X be a regular map defined over k. If the induced map f:X(k)\to X(k) on the k-rational points is injective, then it is surjective, and hence bijective.

Level
Beyond undergraduate level
Level justification

Algebraic varieties over arbitrary algebraically closed fields, their regular maps and rational points form an interdependent algebraic-geometric framework beyond introductory algebra and topology. Understanding the full formulation cannot confidently fit the 30-minute allowance; the elementary affine-line case does not determine this level.

  • Fundamental rigidity result relating injectivity and surjectivity of algebraic self-maps over arbitrary algebraically closed fields; the full named theorem, not the elementary one-variable case.

Proof 1: Finite-field counting and first-order transfer (external)

The following editorial argument completes the stated scope. A supporting source is Model-theoretic tools for the busy algebraist (Ax–Grothendieck section, slides 17–18 (PDF pp.36–43)).

Reviewer bridge: cover X by finitely many affine charts and partition its points into disjoint constructible pieces by assigning a point to its first chart. Chart overlaps, coordinate changes, and the graph of the everywhere-defined regular map are constructible, so finitely many ring formulas with parameters encode this tagged finite union and its self-map. If injectivity but not surjectivity held, one first-order sentence could assert these properties and the existence of an omitted point. Completeness of ACF_p, and compactness/Lefschetz transfer for characteristic zero as in the source, would realize the same sentence over an algebraic closure of a finite field. Its finitely many parameters and omitted point lie in some finite field. The definable function maps that field’s finite encoded set into itself: its unique value is fixed by Frobenius, because its graph is defined over that field. An injection of a finite set is surjective, a contradiction. Here regular means everywhere defined; no assertion about partially defined rational maps is imported.
