Statement

Let X\subseteq\mathbb R^L and \pi(p)=\max_{x\in X}p\cdot x. At a price p where the finite partial derivative \partial_i\pi(p) exists, every maximizing x^*(p) satisfies \partial_i\pi(p)=x_i^*(p).

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Understanding the statement involves Real vectors and dot products; Maximization; Partial derivatives. These prerequisites fit undergraduate coursework or a brief extension of it. A bright US college student should be able to understand this statement after approximately 30 minutes of focused background research; this assesses understanding the claim, not its proof.

Proof 1: Differentiate the linear price objective

Let t=p_i and fix the other prices at p_{-i}\in\mathbb R^{L-1}. Apply the differentiability-point envelope argument to

f(x,t)=tx_i+p_{-i}\cdot x_{-i}.

For a maximizing x^*(p), the definition of \pi implies

\max_s\bigl[f(x^*(p),s)-\pi(s,p_{-i})\bigr] =f(x^*(p),p_i)-\pi(p)=0,

where s ranges over a sufficiently small interval about p_i. Under the assumptions, the objective function of this maximization problem is differentiable at s=p_i, and the first-order condition is exactly

\frac{\partial\pi(p)}{\partial p_i}=x_i^*(p).

Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Fix a maximizer and vary one price; the difference between its linear objective and the value function has a differentiable local maximum of zero. Undergraduate calculus gives the derivative equality.
