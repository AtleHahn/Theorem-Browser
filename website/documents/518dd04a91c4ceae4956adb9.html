Statement

Write R(n_1,\ldots,n_c) for the least positive integer N such that every assignment of c colors to all unordered pairs of an N-element vertex set contains, for some i, a subset of size n_i whose pairs all have color i. A one-vertex subset is monochromatic in every color. If r,s\ge3 and R(r-1,s),R(r,s-1) are both even, then R(r,s)\le R(r-1,s)+R(r,s-1)-1.

Level
High school only
Level justification

Finite graph terminology is defined by pairs of vertices; the statements require only finite counting and elementary logic.

Proof 1: Choose an even-degree vertex and use parity

Write K_j for the complete graph on j vertices.

Suppose p = R(r − 1, s) and q = R(r, s − 1) are both even. Let t = p + q − 1 and consider a two-coloured complete graph of t vertices. If di is the degree of the i-th vertex in the blue subgraph, then by the Handshaking lemma, {\displaystyle \textstyle \sum _{i=1}^{t}d_{i}} is even. Given that t is odd, there must be an even di. Assume without loss of generality that d1 is even, and that M and N are the vertices incident to vertex 1 in the blue and red subgraphs, respectively. Then both {\displaystyle |M|=d_{1}} and {\displaystyle |N|=t-1-d_{1}} are even. By the Pigeonhole principle, either {\displaystyle |M|\geq p-1,} or {\displaystyle |N|\geq q.} Since {\displaystyle |M|} is even and p – 1 is odd, the first inequality can be strengthened, so either {\displaystyle |M|\geq p} or {\displaystyle |N|\geq q.} Suppose {\displaystyle |M|\geq p=R(r-1,s).} Then either the M subgraph has a red Ks and the proof is complete, or it has a blue Kr – 1 which along with vertex 1 makes a blue Kr. The case {\displaystyle |N|\geq q=R(r,s-1)} is treated similarly.

Level justification

Use even degree sum on an odd number of vertices to choose an even-degree vertex, then parity sharpens the neighbor-count alternative and the Ramsey definition finishes. School finite counting and parity suffice.
