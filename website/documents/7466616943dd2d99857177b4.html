Statement

Let E be a real or complex normed vector space and a_n\in E. If the partial sums of \sum_{n\ge1}a_n converge in E, then \|a_n\|\to0. Completeness of E is not assumed.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Real analysis supplies continuous functions, uniform bounds and norm convergence; linear algebra supplies linearity and finite dimension. The continuous-function norm is the supremum norm, and an isometry preserves it. These direct normed-space definitions suffice without dual or spectral theory.

Proof 1: Difference of consecutive vector partial sums

If s_n=\sum_{k=1}^n a_k are the partial sums of the series, then the assumption that the series converges means that

\lim_{n\to\infty}s_n=L

for some L\in E. Then, by continuity of subtraction in a normed space,

\lim_{n\to\infty}a_n =\lim_{n\to\infty}(s_n-s_{n-1}) =\lim_{n\to\infty}s_n-\lim_{n\to\infty}s_{n-1} =L-L=0.
Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Subtract consecutive convergent partial sums and use continuity of subtraction. Undergraduate normed-space and metric definitions suffice; no completeness or functional-analysis representation theorem is needed.

Proof 2: One-term norm tail

Assuming that the series converges implies that its partial sums are Cauchy: for every \varepsilon>0 there is a number N such that

\|a_{n+1}+a_{n+2}+\cdots+a_{n+p}\|<\varepsilon

holds for all n>N and p\geq1. Setting p=1 recovers the claim

\lim_{n\to\infty}a_n=0.
