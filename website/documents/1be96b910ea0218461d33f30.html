Statement

Let f:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb R or \mathbb C be measurable and bounded, with finite limit \alpha=\lim_{t\downarrow0}f(t). For real s>0 define F(s)=\int_0^\infty f(t)e^{-st}\,dt. Then \lim_{s\to\infty}sF(s)=\alpha.

Proof 1: Rescale the integral and apply dominated convergence

Suppose first that {\displaystyle f} is bounded, and suppose that {\displaystyle \lim _{t\to 0^{+}}f(t)=\alpha }. A change of variable in the integral {\displaystyle \int _{0}^{\infty }f(t)e^{-st}\,dt} shows that

{\displaystyle sF(s)=\int _{0}^{\infty }f\left({\frac {t}{s}}\right)e^{-t}\,dt}.

If |f|\le M, then |f(t/s)e^{-t}|\le Me^{-t}, an integrable majorant, and f(t/s)\to\alpha for each t>0. Since f is bounded, the Dominated Convergence Theorem implies that

{\displaystyle \lim _{s\to \infty }sF(s)=\int _{0}^{\infty }\alpha e^{-t}\,dt=\alpha .}
Level
Beyond undergraduate level
Level justification

Rescale the Lebesgue integral and apply dominated convergence to arbitrary bounded measurable functions. General Lebesgue integration and its convergence theorem are substantial prerequisites beyond elementary improper integration.

Proof 2: Split off an exponentially small tail — outline

The substitution u=st gives sF(s)=\int_0^\infty f(u/s)e^{-u}\,du. Choose A with \int_A^\infty e^{-u}\,du<\varepsilon. On 0<u\le A, f(u/s)\to\alpha uniformly as s\to\infty. This makes the integral of (f(u/s)-\alpha)e^{-u} on (0,A] tend to zero; on the tail its absolute value is at most (M+|\alpha|)\varepsilon, where |f|\le M. Since \varepsilon is arbitrary, the limit is \alpha.
