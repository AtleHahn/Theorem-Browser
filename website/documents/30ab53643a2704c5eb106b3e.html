Noteworthiness
Philosophical significance
Medium

Shows that finite descriptions of algebraic objects do not guarantee algorithmic access to their structural properties: no uniform terminating procedure decides any Markov property for all finite group presentations. It extends the limits of mechanized reasoning into ordinary algebra, without making every individual instance undecidable.

Statement

A property P of finitely presentable groups is a Markov property if it is preserved under group isomorphisms, some finitely presentable group A_+ has P, and there is a finitely presentable group A_- that cannot be embedded as a subgroup of any finitely presentable group having P.

For every Markov property P, there is no algorithm that, on every input consisting of a finite group presentation G=\langle X\mid R\rangle, terminates and correctly decides whether G has P. Here a finite presentation specifies a group by finitely many generators X and finitely many defining relations R; algorithm is understood in the Turing-machine sense.

Level
Beyond undergraduate level
Level justification

Introductory algebra supplies groups and embeddings, but finite presentations as computational inputs, universal embedding obstruction across classes of groups, and Turing undecidability have a cumulative conceptual burden that cannot confidently fit the undergraduate 30-minute allowance. Conservatively beyond undergraduate.

Proof 1: Effective HNN construction and undecidable word problem (external)

A link to the proof can be found here (§8, Theorem 8.3, pp.16–18).

In the presentations on p.17, impose the relations for every 0\le i\le n: the first extensions identify the cyclic groups \langle s_i\rangle and \langle s_i^2\rangle separately. Each s_i has infinite order by free-product normal form. For w\ne1, [w,s_0] has infinite order and its nonzero powers avoid the stable-letter subgroups; Britton’s lemma therefore makes u,[w,s_0] a free pair. These normal-form checks give the embeddings used in the amalgamation. The resulting finite presentation is obtained by substituting the input word, without deciding whether it is trivial.
