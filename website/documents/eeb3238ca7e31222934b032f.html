Statement

A full-rank lattice in \mathbb R^d is L=\{k_1v_1+\cdots+k_dv_d:k_i\in\mathbb Z\} for a basis v_1,\ldots,v_d of \mathbb R^d. If d=2 or d=3, a Euclidean rotation preserving L has order 1,2,3,4 or 6. The order is the smallest positive number of iterations giving the identity.

Proof 1: Shrink regular lattice polygons — outline

Work with lattice displacements, so the rotation induces a linear rotation of the displacement lattice with the same order. The lattice is discrete with a positive minimum distance between distinct points. Its rotation orbits lie on bounded circles, so each orbit is finite. A nonidentity rotation has a lattice displacement in its rotational plane, and its orbit gives a regular polygon of lattice points in that plane.

A rotation symmetry in dimension 2 or 3 must move a lattice point to a succession of other lattice points in the same plane, generating a regular polygon of coplanar lattice points. We now confine our attention to the plane in which the symmetry acts.

Now consider an 8-fold rotation, and the displacement vectors between adjacent points of the polygon. If a displacement exists between any two lattice points, then that same displacement is repeated everywhere in the lattice. So collect all the edge displacements to begin at a single lattice point. The edge vectors become radial vectors, and their 8-fold symmetry implies a regular octagon of lattice points around the collection point. But this is impossible, because the new octagon is smaller than the original. The significance of the shrinking is that it is unlimited. The same construction can be repeated with the new octagon, and again and again until the distance between lattice points is as small as we like; thus no discrete lattice can have 8-fold symmetry. The same argument applies to any k-fold rotation, for k greater than 6.

A shrinking argument also eliminates 5-fold symmetry. Consider a regular pentagon of lattice points. If it exists, then we can take every other edge displacement and (head-to-tail) assemble a 5-point star, with the last edge returning to the starting point. The vertices of such a star are again vertices of a regular pentagon with 5-fold symmetry, but smaller than the original.

Thus the theorem is proved.

Proof 2: Rotate a lattice row in opposite directions — outline

Work with lattice displacements, so the rotation induces a linear rotation of the displacement lattice with the same order. The lattice is discrete with a positive minimum distance between distinct points. Its rotation orbits lie on bounded circles, so each orbit is finite. A nonidentity rotation has a lattice displacement in its rotational plane, and its orbit gives a regular polygon of lattice points in that plane.

Let n be the order of the rotation. Consider a row of consecutive lattice points A-O-B, separated by distance a. Rotate the entire row by θ = +2π/n and θ = −2π/n, with point O kept fixed. After the rotation by +2π/n, A is moved to the lattice point C and after the rotation by -2π/n, B is moved to the lattice point D. Due to the assumed periodicity of the lattice, the two lattice points C and D will be also in a line parallel to the initial row; moreover C and D will have directed separation r = ma, with m an integer. But by trigonometry, the directed separation between these points is:

{\displaystyle 2a\cos {\theta }=2a\cos {\frac {2\pi }{n}}}.

Equating the two relations gives:

{\displaystyle 2\cos {\frac {2\pi }{n}}=m}

This is satisfied by only n = 1, 2, 3, 4, 6.

Proof 3: Use the integer trace in a lattice basis

Pass to the linear rotation of lattice displacements and consider matrix properties. The sum of the diagonal elements of a matrix is called the trace of the matrix. In 2D and 3D every rotation is a planar rotation, and the trace is a function of the angle alone. For a 2D rotation, the trace is 2 cos θ; for a 3D rotation, 1 + 2 cos θ.

Examples

  • Consider a 60° (6-fold) rotation matrix with respect to an orthonormal basis in 2D.
{\displaystyle {\begin{bmatrix}{1/2}&-{{\sqrt {3}}/2}\\{{\sqrt {3}}/2}&{1/2}\end{bmatrix}}}

The trace is precisely 1, an integer.

  • Consider a 45° (8-fold) rotation matrix.
{\displaystyle {\begin{bmatrix}{1/{\sqrt {2}}}&-{1/{\sqrt {2}}}\\{1/{\sqrt {2}}}&{1/{\sqrt {2}}}\end{bmatrix}}}

The trace is 2/√2, not an integer.

Selecting a basis formed from vectors that spans the lattice, neither orthogonality nor unit length is guaranteed, only linear independence. However the trace of the rotation matrix is the same with respect to any basis. The trace is a similarity invariant under linear transformations. In the lattice basis, the rotation operation must map every lattice point into an integer number of lattice vectors, so the entries of the rotation matrix in the lattice basis – and hence the trace – are necessarily integers. Similar as in other proofs, this implies that the only allowed rotational symmetries correspond to 1,2,3,4 or 6-fold invariance. For example, wallpapers and crystals cannot be rotated by 45° and remain invariant, the possible orders are 1, 2, 3, 4 and 6.

Example

  • Consider a 60° (360°/6) rotation matrix with respect to the oblique lattice basis for a tiling by equilateral triangles.
{\displaystyle {\begin{bmatrix}0&-1\\1&1\end{bmatrix}}}

The trace is still 1. The determinant (always +1 for a rotation) is also preserved.
