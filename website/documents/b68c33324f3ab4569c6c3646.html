Statement

Let S^1=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb R^2:x^2+y^2=1\}, and let \gamma:S^1\to\mathbb R^2 be continuous and injective. Put C=\gamma(S^1), called a Jordan curve.

The complement \mathbb R^2\setminus C has exactly two connected components U and V. One is bounded and the other is unbounded, and

\partial U=\partial V=C.

A connected component is a maximal connected subset. The boundary \partial A consists of points whose every open disk meets both A and its complement. Bounded means contained in some disk of finite radius. No smoothness or polygonal assumption is imposed on C.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

The full arbitrary continuous-curve formulation uses introductory real analysis and point-set topology. Connected components and boundaries are baseline undergraduate concepts; no homology is required to understand the assertion.

Proof 1: Boundary retractions and a crossing-path obstruction (external)

A link to the proof can be found here (Lemmas1–2 and concluding proof, pp.641–643).

A continuous injection from compact S^1 into the Hausdorff plane is a homeomorphism onto its image, so the source’s definition applies. In Lemma1, extend an arc parameter A\to[0,1] by Tietze and compose with its inverse to obtain the map onto A. Lemma1 proves the common-boundary clause, while the final crossing-path argument proves there is exactly one bounded component. For completeness, all points outside a disk containing C lie in one connected set; every unbounded component meets it, so there is exactly one unbounded component. The rectangles in Lemma2 include their edges, as required by its endpoint conditions.
