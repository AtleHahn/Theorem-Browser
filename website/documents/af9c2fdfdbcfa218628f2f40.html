Proof 1: Guess and universally verify a SAT decision circuit (outline)

The Karp–Lipton theorem can be restated as a result about Boolean formulas with polynomially-bounded quantifiers. Problems in {\displaystyle \Pi _{2}} are described by formulas of this type, with the syntax

{\displaystyle \phi =\forall x\exists y\;\psi (x,y,u)}

where u is the input and {\displaystyle \psi } is a polynomial-time computable predicate. The Karp–Lipton theorem states that this type of formula can be transformed in polynomial time into an equivalent formula in which the quantifiers appear in the opposite order; such a formula belongs to {\displaystyle \Sigma _{2}}. By the Cook–Levin theorem, the subformula

{\displaystyle \exists y\;\psi (x,y,u)}

has a polynomial-size SAT encoding s(x,u). That is, if c is a valid circuit for SAT, then this subformula is equivalent to the unquantified formula c(s(x,u)). Therefore, the full formula for {\displaystyle \phi } is equivalent (under the assumption that a valid circuit c exists) to the formula

{\displaystyle \exists c\forall (x,z)\;V(c,z)\wedge c(s(x,u))\,}

Here c bundles circuits for all relevant input lengths up to a polynomial bound; the existence of such a polynomial-size bundle follows from the hypothesis. Also \forall z\,V(c,z) is its universal correctness test, where V is the formula used to verify that c really is a valid circuit using self-reducibility, taken here as a prerequisite. This equivalent formula has its quantifiers in the opposite order, as desired. Therefore, the Karp–Lipton assumption allows us to transpose the order of existential and universal quantifiers in formulas of this type, showing \Pi_2^P\subseteq\Sigma_2^P and, by complementation, \Sigma_2^P=\Pi_2^P. Repeating the transposition allows formulas with deeper nesting to be simplified to a form in which they have a single existential quantifier followed by a single universal quantifier, showing that {\displaystyle PH=\Sigma _{2}.}

Proof 2: Guess a satisfying-assignment circuit (outline)

Assume {\displaystyle {\mathsf {NP}}\subseteq {\mathsf {P/poly}}}. Therefore, there exists a family of circuits {\displaystyle C_{n}} that solves satisfiability on input of length n. Using self-reducibility, there exists a family of circuits {\displaystyle D_{n}} which outputs a satisfying assignment on true instances.

All quantifiers below have polynomial length bounds, and the witness circuits include the polynomial-size Cook–Levin encodings and recovery of witnesses. Suppose L is a {\displaystyle \Pi _{2}} set

{\displaystyle L=\{z:\forall x.\exists y.\phi (x,y,z)\}\,}

Since {\displaystyle \exists y.\phi (x,y,z)} can be considered an instance of SAT (by Cook-Levin theorem), there exists a circuit {\displaystyle D_{n}}, depending on {\displaystyle n=|z|}, such that the formula defining L is equivalent to

{\displaystyle \forall x.\phi (x,D_{n}(x,z),z)}\tag{1}

Furthermore, the circuit can be guessed with existential quantification:

{\displaystyle \exists D.\forall x.\phi (x,D(x,z),z)}\tag{2}

Obviously (1) implies (2). If (1) is false, then there exists an x such that {\displaystyle \neg \exists y.\phi (x,y,z)}. In this case, no circuit D can output an assignment making {\displaystyle \phi (x,D(x,z),z)\;} true.

The proof has shown that a {\displaystyle \Pi _{2}} set {\displaystyle L} is in {\displaystyle \Sigma _{2}}.

Complementation gives \Pi_2^P=\Sigma_2^P, and the standard repeated quantifier-transposition collapse gives \mathsf{PH}=\Sigma_2^P.
