Statement

For A=\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\3&4\end{pmatrix}, define the permanent by \operatorname{perm}\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}=ad+bc and q(t)=\operatorname{perm}(tI_2-A). Then q(t)=t^2-5t+10 and q(A)=12I_2\ne0.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Understanding the statement involves Real matrix arithmetic; Locally defined permanent; Matrix polynomial evaluation. These prerequisites fit undergraduate coursework or a brief extension of it. A bright US college student should be able to understand this statement after approximately 30 minutes of focused background research; this assesses understanding the claim, not its proof.

Proof 1: The permanent does not satisfy a Cayley–Hamilton analogue

{\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}q(\lambda )&=\operatorname {perm} (\lambda I_{2}-A)=\operatorname {perm} \!{\begin{pmatrix}\lambda -1&-2\\-3&\lambda -4\end{pmatrix}}\\[6pt]&=(\lambda -1)(\lambda -4)+(-2)(-3)=\lambda ^{2}-5\lambda +10.\end{aligned}}}

Direct multiplication gives A^2=\begin{pmatrix}7&10\\15&22\end{pmatrix}. Hence

{\displaystyle q(A)=A^{2}-5A+10I_{2}=12I_{2}\neq 0.}
