Noteworthiness
Philosophical significance
Minor

Individual reliability above one half does not guarantee that a majority is more reliable when judgments are correlated. This directly qualifies arguments for the wisdom of crowds and epistemic democracy by showing how dependence can overturn the benefit of adding voters.

Statement

There are correlated juries with each juror correct with probability p=0.55 for which simple-majority correctness has values P_3=0.54505 for three jurors and P_5\approx0.5196194 for five jurors. Thus P_5<P_3<p: majority can be worse than one juror, and a larger jury can be worse than a smaller jury.

Proof 1: Evaluate the supplied correlated-vote formula

Let {\displaystyle p} be the probability of a juror voting for the correct alternative and {\displaystyle c} be the (second-order) correlation coefficient between any two correct votes. If all higher-order correlation coefficients in the Bahadur representation of the joint probability distribution of votes equal to zero, and (p,c) is an admissible pair (one yielding a joint probability distribution for n votes), then the probability of the jury collectively reaching the correct decision under simple majority is given by:

{\displaystyle P(n,p,c)=I_{p}\left({\frac {n+1}{2}},{\frac {n+1}{2}}\right)+0.5c(n-1)(0.5-p){\frac {\partial I_{p}({\frac {n+1}{2}},{\frac {n+1}{2}})}{\partial p}},}

where {\displaystyle I_{p}} is the regularized incomplete beta function.

Example: Take a jury of three jurors {\displaystyle (n=3)}, with individual competence {\displaystyle p=0.55} and second-order correlation {\displaystyle c=0.4}. Then {\displaystyle P(3,0.55,0.4)=0.54505}. The competence of the jury is lower than the competence of a single juror, which equals to {\displaystyle 0.55}. Moreover, enlarging the jury by two jurors {\displaystyle (n=5)} decreases the jury competence even further, {\displaystyle P(5,0.55,0.4)\approx0.5196194}. Note that {\displaystyle p=0.55} and {\displaystyle c=0.4} is an admissible pair of parameters. For {\displaystyle n=5} and {\displaystyle p=0.55}, the maximum admissible second-order correlation coefficient equals {\displaystyle \approx 0.43}.

The above example shows that when the individual competence is low but the correlation is high:

  • The collective competence under simple majority may fall below that of a single juror;
  • Enlarging the jury may decrease its collective competence.

The correctness formula and parameter admissibility are supplied by the source, citing Kaniovski and Zaigraev; their derivation and the joint distributions are not given there.
