Statement

For 1<p<\infty, H denotes the Hilbert transform on L^p(\mathbb R), with sign convention Hf(t)=\pi^{-1}\operatorname{p.v.}\int_{\mathbb R}f(\tau)/(t-\tau)\,d\tau. The Fourier transform convention is \widehat f(\xi)=\int f(t)e^{-2\pi it\xi}\,dt, extended to L^2 or tempered distributions as appropriate. Let k\ge1 be an integer. If u and its first k weak derivatives lie in L^p(\mathbb R), then D^kHu=HD^ku.

Proof 1: Hilbert transforms commute with differentiation — outline

Formally, the derivative of the Hilbert transform is the Hilbert transform of the derivative, i.e. these two linear operators commute:

{\displaystyle \operatorname {H} \left({\frac {\mathrm {d} u}{\mathrm {d} t}}\right)={\frac {\mathrm {d} }{\mathrm {d} t}}\operatorname {H} (u)}

Iterating this identity,

{\displaystyle \operatorname {H} \left({\frac {\mathrm {d} ^{k}u}{\mathrm {d} t^{k}}}\right)={\frac {\mathrm {d} ^{k}}{\mathrm {d} t^{k}}}\operatorname {H} (u)}

This is rigorously true as stated provided u and its first k weak derivatives belong to {\displaystyle L^{p}(\mathbb {R} )}. One can check this easily in the frequency domain, where differentiation becomes multiplication by 2\pi i\xi and the Hilbert transform becomes multiplication by -i\operatorname{sgn}\xi. These scalar multiplications commute.

Beyond undergraduate level
Level justification

Commute the scalar Fourier multipliers and interpret the identity using weak derivatives and Sobolev approximation. Sobolev spaces, distributional Fourier transforms and Lp boundedness are substantial graduate prerequisites.
