Statement

Let B\subset\mathbb R^3 be a closed ball of positive radius with center o. Then B and B\setminus\{o\} have finite partitions into corresponding congruent pieces; at most two pieces suffice.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

The statements use balls, finite set partitions and rigid motions. Undergraduate set theory suffices to distinguish arbitrary point subsets from physical solids, and the axiom of choice adds one explicit set-selection principle where invoked. Understanding the assertion does not require the advanced construction of the pieces.

Proof 1: Shift a countable orbit on an interior circle (outline)

It remains to be shown that the ball minus its center point is equidecomposable with the ball. Consider a circle within the ball, containing the point at the center of the ball. A countable set of points on the circle can be rotated to give itself plus one more point.

Explicitly, choose a rotation \rho of the circle whose iterates of the ball's center o are distinct, and let E be the disjoint union of \{\rho^n(o)\} over n=0,1,2,\ldots. Then \rho(E) is the disjoint union over n\ge1 and therefore \rho(E)=E-\{o\}. Now we can conclude

B=E\cup(B-E)\sim\rho(E)\cup(B-E) =(E-\{o\})\cup(B-E)=B-\{o\},

where \sim denotes “is equidecomposable to”.

Note that this involves rotation about a point other than the ball's center.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

The construction uses an infinite forward orbit of an irrational rotation, partitions the ball into that orbit and its complement, and shifts the orbit once. Undergraduate familiarity with sets, sequences and irrationality suffices; equidecomposition here means only the explicitly displayed two-piece partition. No Banach–Tarski theorem, nonmeasurable-set construction or choice principle is invoked. The circle can be chosen inside the ball through its center.
