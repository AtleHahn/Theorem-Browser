Statement

Let R(3,3;2) be the smallest positive integer n such that every red/blue coloring of the edges between all pairs of n vertices contains three vertices whose three joining edges share one color. Then R(3,3;2)=6.

Proof 1: Upper bound and pentagon sharpness (outline)

Upper bound

Choose any one vertex; call it P. There are five edges leaving P. They are each coloured red or blue. The pigeonhole principle says that at least three of them must be of the same colour; for if there are less than three of one colour, say red, then there are at least three that are blue.

Let A, B, C be the other ends of these three edges, all of the same colour, say blue. If any one of AB, BC, CA is blue, then that edge together with the two edges from P to the edge's endpoints forms a blue triangle. If none of AB, BC, CA is blue, then all three edges are red and we have a red triangle, namely, ABC.

Sharpness

The monochromatic-triangle conclusion does not hold for fewer than six vertices. To show this, we give a coloring of K5 with red and blue that does not contain a triangle with all edges the same color. We draw K5 as a pentagon surrounding a star (a pentagram). We color the edges of the pentagon red and the edges of the star blue. Thus, 6 is the smallest number for which we can claim the conclusion of the theorem. In Ramsey theory, we write this fact as:

{\displaystyle R(3,3;2)=6.}

Level justification

Use pigeonhole at one of six vertices for the upper bound and the complementary pentagon cycles for sharpness. School finite counting and diagrams suffice.
