Statement

Let K be a field and let A,B,C be noncollinear points in the affine plane K^2. Choose D on the line BC, E on CA, and F on AB, with none of these points equal to either endpoint of its side. For collinear points, define XY/YZ to be the scalar q\in K for which Y-X=q(Z-Y).

If the three lines AD,BE,CF are concurrent, then

\frac{AF}{FB}\frac{BD}{DC}\frac{CE}{EA}=1.

Conversely, if this product equals 1, the three lines are concurrent or all three are parallel. Concurrent means passing through a common point of K^2; parallel means having the same one-dimensional direction subspace. For K=\mathbb R the ratios are the usual directed segment ratios, positive for a point between the endpoints and negative otherwise.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Introductory algebra and linear algebra give the arbitrary field, vector differences and line directions; the local ratio definition requires no further geometry theory.

Proof 1: Affine coordinates with separate parallel and intersection cases (external)

A link to the proof can be found here (Satz5.12(ii), pp.24–25; Bemerkung5.13(ii)).

Use a=A,b=B,c=C,x=F,y=D,z=E in the source. Its three ratios are the reciprocals of those defined here, so their product is 1 exactly when the stated product is 1. The parameters \alpha,\beta,\gamma all avoid 0,1. In the final converse calculation replace the erroneous intermediate equality involving \alpha(2-\rho-\gamma) by

\alpha(1-\rho)=(1-\alpha)\rho(1-\gamma)\quad\Longrightarrow\quad(1-\rho\gamma)(1-\alpha)=1-\rho.

Then \mu=1-\rho\gamma gives the third cevian through the intersection. The other case in the proof explicitly shows that if the first two cevians are parallel, the product identity forces the third to have their direction. These calculations remain valid in characteristic two.
