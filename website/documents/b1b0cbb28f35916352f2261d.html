Statement

For integers q\geq2 and n>1, define \Phi_n(x)=\prod_{\zeta}(x-\zeta), where \zeta runs over the primitive complex nth roots of unity (those whose least positive power equal to one is the nth). Then |\Phi_n(q)|>q-1.

Level
High school only
Level justification

The assertion is about explicit polynomial substitutions, complex roots, trigonometric identities or finite products. All necessary nonstandard notation is explained, and elementary algebra suffices to understand the claim. This rating concerns understanding the statement, independently of the difficulty of its proof.

Proof 1: Distances from q to primitive roots

In the polynomial identity

{\displaystyle \Phi _{n}(x)=\prod (x-\zeta ),}

where {\displaystyle \zeta } runs over the primitive {\displaystyle n}-th roots of unity, set {\displaystyle x} to be {\displaystyle q} and then take absolute values

{\displaystyle |\Phi _{n}(q)|=\prod |q-\zeta |.}

For {\displaystyle n>1}, we see that for each primitive {\displaystyle n}-th root of unity {\displaystyle \zeta },

{\displaystyle |q-\zeta |>|q-1|}

because of the location of {\displaystyle q}, {\displaystyle 1}, and {\displaystyle \zeta } in the complex plane. Since {\displaystyle q\geq 2}, the term {\displaystyle q-1} is at least {\displaystyle 1}. Thus

{\displaystyle |\Phi _{n}(q)|>q-1.}
Level
High school only
Level justification

Every primitive root for n>1 lies on the unit circle away from one, so its distance to real q>=2 exceeds q-1. Multiply these positive factors, each greater than a number at least one. Only complex-plane geometry and finite products are used.
