Statement

Let G be a nonempty finite simple undirected graph. A proper coloring assigns colors to vertices so adjacent vertices differ; write \chi(G) for the least number of colors. An orientation chooses one direction for every edge. Let L(D) be the largest number of distinct vertices on a directed path of an orientation D. Then \chi(G)=\min_D L(D). The minimum is achieved by an acyclic orientation (one with no directed cycle).

Level
High school only
Level justification

Coloring, orientation, directed-path length and acyclicity are defined locally for a finite graph. The minimum over finitely many orientations and longest finite path counts require elementary combinatorial reasoning.

Proof 1: Increasing-color orientation and maximal acyclic subgraph

To prove that the chromatic number is greater than or equal to the minimum number of vertices in a longest path, suppose that a given graph has a coloring with {\displaystyle k} colors, for some number {\displaystyle k}. Then it may be acyclically oriented by numbering colors and by directing each edge from its lower-numbered endpoint to the higher-numbered endpoint. With this orientation, the numbers are strictly increasing along each directed path, so each path can include at most one vertex of each color, for a total of at most {\displaystyle k} vertices per path.

To prove that the chromatic number is less than or equal to the minimum number of vertices in a longest path, suppose that a given graph has an orientation with at most {\displaystyle k} vertices per simple directed path, for some number {\displaystyle k}. Then the vertices of the graph may be colored with {\displaystyle k} colors by choosing an inclusion-maximal acyclic spanning subgraph of the orientation, and then coloring each vertex by the number of vertices on the longest path in the chosen subgraph that ends at that vertex. Each edge within the subgraph is oriented from a vertex with a lower number to a vertex with a higher number, and is therefore properly colored. For each edge that is not in the subgraph, there must exist a directed path within the subgraph connecting the same two vertices in the opposite direction, for otherwise the edge could have been included in the chosen subgraph; therefore, the edge is oriented from a higher number to a lower number and is again properly colored.

High school only
Level justification

Orient edges by increasing color, then conversely color a maximal acyclic spanning subgraph by longest-path lengths. The missing-edge reverse-path argument uses only finite paths, cycles and integer comparisons, all defined locally.
