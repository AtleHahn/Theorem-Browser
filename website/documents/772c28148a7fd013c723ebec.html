Real algebraic numbers form only a countable subset of the continuum. The familiar numbers specified by rational polynomial equations are therefore exceptional in cardinality, despite their central role in calculation.

Statement

The set A=\{x\in\mathbb R:P(x)=0\text{ for some nonzero }P\in\mathbb Z[t]\} is countably infinite: it admits a bijection with the positive integers.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Countability, infinite cardinality and order isomorphisms require undergraduate set theory; explicit definitions do not make these ordinary high-school number statements. A bright US college student should be able to understand the statement after about 30 minutes of focused background research; this rating concerns understanding the claim, not proving it.

Proof 1: Enumerate polynomial formulas — outline

Assign a Gödel number to each polynomial formula. Use the standard prerequisites that a nonzero polynomial has finitely many real roots and that a countable union of finite sets is countable. This formula-numbering recipe enumerates the real algebraic numbers, which form a countably infinite set.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Encode finite polynomial descriptions, enumerate their finite root sets and use a countable union. Undergraduate countability and polynomial algebra suffice; Gödel numbering here only encodes finite strings and does not invoke arithmetized provability.
