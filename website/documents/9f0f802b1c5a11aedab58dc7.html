If a nonconstant entire function has finite noninteger order \rho, then for every w\in\mathbb C there are infinitely many distinct z with f(z)=w.

Proof 1: A noninteger finite-order entire function takes every value infinitely often (outline)

For any w\in\mathbb C, it suffices to prove f(z)-w has infinitely many roots. Expand f(z)-w to its Hadamard representation

f(z)-w=e^{Q(z)}z^m\prod_k E_p(z/a_k),

where Q is a polynomial, m is the multiplicity at zero, the a_k are the nonzero zeros repeated with multiplicity, and

E_p(\zeta)=(1-\zeta)\exp\left(\sum_{j=1}^p\frac{\zeta^j}{j}\right)

is an elementary canonical-product factor, with the sum empty for p=0.

If the product is finite, the representation is a polynomial times the exponential of a polynomial, which has integer order: zero for a polynomial, otherwise the degree of the exponent after combination. The growth facts used here, including that subtracting a constant preserves the order of a nonconstant entire function, are prerequisites. This contradicts the noninteger order \rho. Thus there are infinitely many zeros, and since every zero has finite multiplicity, infinitely many distinct preimages of w.

Use Hadamard canonical products and entire-function order to exclude finitely many zeros after subtracting a value. The combination of growth order and canonical factorization is not confidently a small extension of ordinary undergraduate complex analysis, so retain beyond undergraduate.
