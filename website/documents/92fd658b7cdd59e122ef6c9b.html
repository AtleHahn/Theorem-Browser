Statement

Put \omega=(-1+i\sqrt3)/2 and R=\mathbb Z[\omega]=\{a+b\omega:a,b\in\mathbb Z\}. Define N(a+b\omega)=a^2-ab+b^2. Let \pi be a prime element of R with N(\pi)\ne3, and let \alpha\in R not be divisible by \pi. Then 3\mid N(\pi)-1, and exactly one element u\in\{1,\omega,\omega^2\} satisfies \alpha^{(N(\pi)-1)/3}\equiv u\pmod\pi.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

The ring of Eisenstein integers and its norm are explicitly given by coordinates. Prime elements, divisibility and congruence are introductory ring theory. The cube-root character is the specified one-of-three congruence, requiring no general number-field norm or ideal-class theory.

Proof 1: Use the prime norm and Fermat congruence — outline

An analogue of Fermat's little theorem is true in {\displaystyle \mathbb {Z} [\omega ]}: if {\displaystyle \alpha } is not divisible by a prime {\displaystyle \pi },

{\displaystyle \alpha ^{N(\pi )-1}\equiv 1{\pmod {\pi }}.}

Now assume that {\displaystyle N(\pi )\neq 3} so that, by the prime-norm classification, {\displaystyle N(\pi )\equiv 1{\pmod {3}}.} Or put differently {\displaystyle 3\mid N(\pi )-1.} Then we can write:

{\displaystyle \alpha ^{\frac {N(\pi )-1}{3}}\equiv \omega ^{k}{\pmod {\pi }},}

for a unique unit {\displaystyle \omega ^{k}.}
