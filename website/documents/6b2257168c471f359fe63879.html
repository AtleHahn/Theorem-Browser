Likely alternative prerequisite level: Undergraduate level; lowest recorded proof level: Beyond undergraduate level. Foata-Han give finite versions derived from the finite q-binomial identity. Substitute q=x^2 and the Laurent parameter appropriate to y^2, then pass to the limit using absolute convergence for |x|<1 and y!=0; Gaussian decay controls the bilateral sum. Their identities cover complex parameters, not only real q. The x=0 case is separately 1. Polynomial induction and elementary series/product convergence replace holomorphic Laurent uniqueness and continuation.

Statement

For complex x,y with |x|<1 and y≠0, \prod_{m=1}^{\infty}(1-x^{2m})(1+x^{2m-1}y^2)(1+x^{2m-1}y^{-2})=\sum_{n\in\mathbb Z}x^{n^2}y^{2n}. At x=0 the n=0 term is 1.

Proof 1: Laurent coefficient recurrence and Durfee-square normalization — outline

First suppose x\ne0.

Let {\displaystyle f_{x}(y)=\prod _{m=1}^{\infty }\left(1-x^{2m}\right)\left(1+x^{2m-1}y^{2}\right)\left(1+x^{2m-1}y^{-2}\right)}

Substituting xy for y and multiplying the new terms out gives

{\displaystyle f_{x}(xy)={\frac {1+x^{-1}y^{-2}}{1+xy^{2}}}f_{x}(y)=x^{-1}y^{-2}f_{x}(y)}

The quotient identity extends through its apparent denominator zeros. Since {\displaystyle f_{x}} is holomorphic and even for {\displaystyle |y|>0}, it has a Laurent series

{\displaystyle f_{x}(y)=\sum _{n=-\infty }^{\infty }c_{n}(x)y^{2n}}

which satisfies

{\displaystyle \sum _{n=-\infty }^{\infty }c_{n}(x)x^{2n+1}y^{2n}=xf_{x}(xy)=y^{-2}f_{x}(y)=\sum _{n=-\infty }^{\infty }c_{n+1}(x)y^{2n}}

so that

{\displaystyle c_{n+1}(x)=c_{n}(x)x^{2n+1}=\dots =c_{0}(x)x^{(n+1)^{2}}}

and hence

{\displaystyle f_{x}(y)=c_{0}(x)\sum _{n=-\infty }^{\infty }x^{n^{2}}y^{2n}}

To show that {\displaystyle c_{0}(x)=1}, use the fact that the infinite expansion

{\displaystyle \prod _{m=1}^{\infty }\left(1+x^{2m-1}y^{2}\right)\left(1+x^{2m-1}y^{-2}\right)}

has the following coefficient at {\displaystyle y^{0}}

{\displaystyle =\sum _{m=0}^{\infty }{\frac {x^{2m^{2}}}{(1-x^{2})^{2}(1-x^{4})^{2}\cdots (1-x^{2m})^{2}}}}

which, by the Durfee square generating-function identity, equals the partition generating function with {\displaystyle x^{2}} instead of {\displaystyle x}.

{\displaystyle =\prod _{m=1}^{\infty }\left(1-x^{2m}\right)^{-1}}

Therefore the coefficient of {\displaystyle y^{0}} in {\displaystyle f_x(y)} is 1, and so {\displaystyle c_{0}(x)=1}.

The two coefficient and generating-function identities used in this normalization are taken as prerequisites. For x=0, both sides of the triple-product identity are 1.
