Statement

Let d\ge1 be an integer and let [0,1]^d denote the real d-tuples with all coordinates in [0,1]. Write \|z\|_\infty=\max_{1\le i\le d}|z_i|. Let f:[0,1]^d\to[0,1]^d satisfy \|f(x)-f(y)\|_\infty\le L\|x-y\|_\infty for all x,y, with L\ge0, and let f(x_0)=x_0. Then \|f(x)-x\|_\infty\le(1+L)\|x-x_0\|_\infty. In particular, \|x-x_0\|_\infty\le\delta implies residual \|f(x)-x\|_\infty\le(1+L)\delta.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Understanding a Lipschitz bound for maps of arbitrary-dimensional cubes and a norm-based residual requires undergraduate multivariable and norm notation. A bright US college student should be able to understand the statement after about 30 minutes of focused background research; this rating concerns understanding the claim, not proving it.

Proof 1: Substitute the fixed point into the Lipschitz inequality

If f is Lipschitz-continuous with constant L, then \|x-x_0\|_\infty\leq\delta implies \|f(x)-f(x_0)\|_\infty\leq L\cdot\delta. Since x_0 is a fixed-point of f, this implies \|f(x)-x_0\|_\infty\leq L\cdot\delta, so \|f(x)-x\|_\infty\leq(1+L)\cdot\delta. Taking \delta=\|x-x_0\|_\infty gives the stated bound.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

The estimate itself uses only substitution and the triangle inequality. However, following the complete argument requires interpreting an arbitrary-dimensional function, a uniform Lipschitz bound in the maximum norm, and a fixed point. Those abstractions are more naturally undergraduate analysis than standard high-school mathematics, even with the coordinate formulas provided. No fixed-point existence theorem or advanced analysis is required.
