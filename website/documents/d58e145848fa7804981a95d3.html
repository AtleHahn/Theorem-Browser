Statement

Let T be a classical first-order theory with numerals \bar n, a natural-number encoding of its finite proofs, a formula q(y,x), and a fixed numeral \bar c. Put G=\forall y\,q(y,\bar c). Assume the following numeralwise proof-detection property for every natural number n: if n codes a T-proof of G, then T\vdash\neg q(\bar n,\bar c); if it does not, then T\vdash q(\bar n,\bar c). If T is consistent, T\nvdash G. If T is omega-consistent, neither G nor \neg G is provable. Here omega-consistency forbids proving an existential sentence while refuting every one of its numeral instances.

Proof 1: Apply consistency and then omega-consistency

Suppose G=\forall y\,q(y,\bar c) is provable. Let n be the Gödel number of a proof of G. Then, by the assumed numeralwise proof-detection property, the formula \neg q(\bar n,\bar c) is provable. Proving both \neg q(\bar n,\bar c) and \forall y\,q(y,\bar c) violates the consistency of the formal theory, since universal instantiation also proves q(\bar n,\bar c). We therefore conclude that G is not provable.

Consider any natural number n. Suppose \neg q(\bar n,\bar c) is provable. Then n must be the Gödel number of a proof of G: otherwise the other clause of the assumed property would prove q(\bar n,\bar c), contradicting consistency. But we have just proved that G is not provable. Since the proof-detection property supplies q(\bar n,\bar c) whenever n is not a proof code, we conclude that, for all natural numbers n, q(\bar n,\bar c) is provable.

Suppose the negation of G, equivalently \exists y\,\neg q(y,\bar c), is provable. Proving both \exists y\,\neg q(y,\bar c) and q(\bar n,\bar c) for all natural numbers n violates \omega-consistency of the formal theory. Thus if the theory is \omega-consistent, \neg G is not provable.
