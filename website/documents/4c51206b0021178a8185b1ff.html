For the stated finite-volume hyperbolic manifolds, topology determines geometry up to isometry. This gives a strong setting in which metric structure is fixed by more abstract structure, subject to the crucial dimension and curvature hypotheses.

Statement

Let M and N be connected complete hyperbolic manifolds of the same dimension n\geq3, each with finite Riemannian volume. Then every homotopy equivalence f:M\to N is homotopic to a unique isometry F:M\to N.

A hyperbolic manifold here is a Riemannian manifold of constant sectional curvature -1; completeness refers to its Riemannian distance. An isometry is a bijection preserving that distance. A homotopy equivalence means a continuous map f with a continuous inverse up to homotopy: a map g:N\to M for which g\circ f and f\circ g are homotopic to the respective identity maps.

Proof 1: Lattice rigidity and descent to hyperbolic manifolds (external)

A link to the proof can be found here (Theorems A–C pp.256–257; Lemma 1.1 and Sections 1–4, proof of Theorem C ending p.285; companion compact proof in Thurston Sections 5.7/5.9.). Supporting sources: Thurston, The Geometry and Topology of Three-Manifolds, §§5.7 and 5.9.

Apply Prasad Theorems A–C: real-rank-one nonuniform lattices satisfy his Q-rank-one hypotheses by Lemma 1.1; SO_0(n,1) for n>=3 avoids the excluded PSL(2,R) case. His Sections 1–4 construct the equivariant pseudo-isometry, concluding Theorem C on p.285, and invoke Mostow's stated rigidity theorem. For the compact case, Thurston gives the corresponding argument in The Geometry and Topology of Three-Manifolds, https://library.slmath.org/nonmsri/gt3m/PDF/Thurston-gt3m.pdf , Sections 5.7 and 5.9 (printed pp.100–112); using analytic and ergodic rigidity results. To include nonorientable manifolds, pass to the finite-index normal subgroup of pi_1(M) lying both in its orientation-preserving subgroup and in the inverse image of that of N. The isomorphism yields an isometry of universal hyperbolic spaces on this subgroup. For each remaining deck element, the conjugated isometry and the prescribed deck isometry differ by an isometry centralizing the finite-index lattice. Such a centralizer is trivial: it fixes the attracting and repelling endpoints of loxodromic elements, dense in the full sphere at infinity for a finite-volume lattice. Thus it descends to M and N. The same argument proves uniqueness up to the inner automorphism corresponding to a change of basepoint. Aspherical K(pi,1) classification identifies the resulting map's homotopy class with that of f. Curvature -1 normalization removes homothety ambiguity.
