Statement

Every orientation-preserving Euclidean isometry of the complex plane is a translation or a rotation. In the form z\mapsto\omega z+a, |\omega|=1, it is a translation when \omega=1 and otherwise a rotation with center p=a/(1-\omega).

Level
High school only
Level justification

A plane isometry preserves distances and orientation preservation distinguishes rotations/translations from reflections. The complex-coordinate form is stated and the companion interprets multiplication by a unit complex number as rotation. This uses elementary plane geometry and complex arithmetic, not an abstract classification of linear operators.

Proof 1: Solve for the complex rotation center

We use the complex-coordinate form of a direct plane isometry, z\mapsto\omega z+a with |\omega|=1.

Euclidean geometry is expressed in the complex plane by points {\displaystyle p=x+yi} where i squared is −1. Rotations result from multiplications by {\displaystyle \omega =\cos t+i\sin t}. Note that a rotation about complex point p is obtained by complex arithmetic with

{\displaystyle z\mapsto \omega (z-p)+p=\omega z+p(1-\omega )}

where the last expression shows the mapping equivalent to rotation at 0 and a translation. Therefore, given direct isometry {\displaystyle z\mapsto \omega z+a,} one can solve {\displaystyle p(1-\omega )=a} to obtain {\displaystyle p=a/(1-\omega )} as the center for an equivalent rotation, provided that {\displaystyle \omega \neq 1}, that is, provided the direct isometry is not a pure translation. As stated by Cederberg, "A direct isometry is either a rotation or a translation."

Level justification

Use the supplied complex affine representation, expand a rotation about p, and solve a linear equation for its center. Complex arithmetic and planar rotations suffice; no linear-operator classification is proved.
