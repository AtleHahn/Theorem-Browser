Statement

Let X_1,X_2,\ldots be iid real random variables with \mathbb E|X_1|^4<\infty and \mu=\mathbb EX_1. Then n^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^nX_j\to\mu almost surely.

Proof 1: Fourth moments, summable tails and Borel–Cantelli

Let us first note that without loss of generality we can assume that {\displaystyle \mu =0} by centering. After centering, write \sigma^2=\mathbb E[X_i^2], \tau=\mathbb E[X_i^4]<\infty, S_n=\sum_{i=1}^nX_i and \overline X_n=S_n/n. In this case, the strong law says that

{\displaystyle \Pr \!\left(\lim _{n\to \infty }{\overline {X}}_{n}=0\right)=1,}

or

{\displaystyle \Pr \left(\omega :\lim _{n\to \infty }{\frac {S_{n}(\omega )}{n}}=0\right)=1.}

It is equivalent to show that

{\displaystyle \Pr \left(\omega :{\frac {S_{n}(\omega )}{n}}\not\longrightarrow 0\right)=0,}

Note that

{\displaystyle {\frac {S_{n}(\omega )}{n}}\not\longrightarrow 0\iff \exists \epsilon >0,\left|{\frac {S_{n}(\omega )}{n}}\right|\geq \epsilon \ {\mbox{infinitely often}},}

and thus to prove the strong law we need to show that for every {\displaystyle \epsilon >0}, we have

{\displaystyle \Pr \left(\omega :|S_{n}(\omega )|\geq n\epsilon {\mbox{ infinitely often}}\right)=0.}

Define the events {\displaystyle A_{n}=\{\omega :|S_{n}|\geq n\epsilon \}}, and if we can show that

{\displaystyle \sum _{n=1}^{\infty }\Pr(A_{n})<\infty ,}

then the Borel-Cantelli Lemma implies the result. So let us estimate {\displaystyle \Pr(A_{n})}.

We compute

{\displaystyle {\mathbb {E} }[S_{n}^{4}]={\mathbb {E} }\left[\left(\sum _{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\right)^{4}\right]={\mathbb {E} }\left[\sum _{1\leq i,j,k,l\leq n}X_{i}X_{j}X_{k}X_{l}\right].}

We first claim that every term of the form {\displaystyle X_{i}^{3}X_{j},X_{i}^{2}X_{j}X_{k},X_{i}X_{j}X_{k}X_{l}} where all subscripts are distinct, must have zero expectation. This is because {\displaystyle {\mathbb {E} }[X_{i}^{3}X_{j}]={\mathbb {E} }[X_{i}^{3}]{\mathbb {E} }[X_{j}]} by independence, and the last term is zero—and similarly for the other terms. Therefore the only terms in the sum with nonzero expectation are {\displaystyle {\mathbb {E} }[X_{i}^{4}]} and {\displaystyle {\mathbb {E} }[X_{i}^{2}X_{j}^{2}]}. Since the {\displaystyle X_{i}} are identically distributed, the expectations within each of these two types are the same, and moreover {\displaystyle {\mathbb {E} }[X_{i}^{2}X_{j}^{2}]=({\mathbb {E} }[X_{i}^{2}])^{2}}.

There are {\displaystyle n} terms of the form {\displaystyle {\mathbb {E} }[X_{i}^{4}]} and {\displaystyle 3n(n-1)} terms of the form {\displaystyle ({\mathbb {E} }[X_{i}^{2}])^{2}}, and so

{\displaystyle {\mathbb {E} }[S_{n}^{4}]=n\tau +3n(n-1)\sigma ^{4}.}

Note that the right-hand side is a quadratic polynomial in {\displaystyle n}, and as such there exists a {\displaystyle C>0} such that {\displaystyle {\mathbb {E} }[S_{n}^{4}]\leq Cn^{2}} for {\displaystyle n} sufficiently large. By Markov,

{\displaystyle \Pr(|S_{n}|\geq n\epsilon )\leq {\frac {1}{(n\epsilon )^{4}}}{\mathbb {E} }[S_{n}^{4}]\leq {\frac {C}{\epsilon ^{4}n^{2}}},}

for {\displaystyle n} sufficiently large, and therefore this series is summable. Apply this to the countably many choices \epsilon=1/m, m\ge1. Their probability-one events have a probability-one intersection, on which S_n/n\to0. Undoing the centering establishes the strong law of large numbers.
