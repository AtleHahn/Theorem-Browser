Statement

Two players choose C or D. At (C,C) each receives R; at (D,D) each receives P; a player choosing D against C receives T, and the cooperator receives S. Assume T>R>P>S. A Nash equilibrium allows no player to improve its expected payoff by changing its own choice or probability distribution alone. Then (D,D) is the unique Nash equilibrium, and switching jointly to (C,C) strictly improves both players' payoffs.

Level
High school only
Level justification

Equilibrium is defined by unilateral payoff improvement in a two-choice game; mixtures are elementary weighted averages.

Proof 1: Compare the available payoffs

If the other player cooperates, defecting gives T>R. If the other player defects, defecting gives P>S. Hence D is strictly better in either case, and also against any probability mixture of those cases. Neither player can assign positive probability to C in an equilibrium. Thus (D,D) is the unique equilibrium. Each receives P there, whereas (C,C) gives each R>P.

Level
High school only
Level justification

Compare payoffs in each of the two cases and their weighted averages, then use the locally defined equilibrium condition. School inequalities and finite probability mixtures suffice.
