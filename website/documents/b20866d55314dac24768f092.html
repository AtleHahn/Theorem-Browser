No right triangle with positive rational side lengths has area equal to the square of a rational number.

Proof 1: Fermat’s right triangle theorem — outline

Multiplying rational sides by a common denominator reduces a hypothetical rational square-area triangle to an integer square-area triangle. We use the fact that a rational number whose square is an integer is itself an integer.

Fermat's proof is a proof by infinite descent. It shows that, from any example of a Pythagorean triangle with square area, one can derive a smaller example. Since Pythagorean triangles have positive integer areas, and there does not exist an infinite descending sequence of positive integers, there also cannot exist a Pythagorean triangle with square area.

In more detail, suppose that {\displaystyle x}, {\displaystyle y}, and {\displaystyle z} are the integer sides of a right triangle with square area. By dividing by any common factors, one can assume that this triangle is primitive and from the known form of all primitive Pythagorean triples, one can set {\displaystyle x=2pq}, {\displaystyle y=p^{2}-q^{2}}, and {\displaystyle z=p^{2}+q^{2}}, by which the problem is transformed into finding coprime integers {\displaystyle p>q>0} (one of which is even) such that the area {\displaystyle pq(p^{2}-q^{2})} is square. For this number to be a square, its four linear factors {\displaystyle p}, {\displaystyle q}, {\displaystyle p+q}, and {\displaystyle p-q} (which are coprime) must themselves be squares; let {\displaystyle p+q=r^{2}} and {\displaystyle p-q=s^{2}}. Both {\displaystyle r} and {\displaystyle s} must be odd since exactly one of {\displaystyle p} or {\displaystyle q} is even and the other is odd. Therefore, both {\displaystyle r-s} and {\displaystyle r+s} are even, and one of them is divisible by 4. Dividing them by two produces two more integers {\displaystyle u=(r-s)/2} and {\displaystyle v=(r+s)/2}, one of which is even by the previous sentence. Because {\displaystyle u^{2}+v^{2}=(r^{2}+s^{2})/2=p} is a square, {\displaystyle u} and {\displaystyle v} are the legs of another primitive Pythagorean triangle whose area is {\displaystyle uv/2=q/4}. Since {\displaystyle q} is itself a square and since {\displaystyle uv} is even, {\displaystyle q/4} is a square. Thus, any Pythagorean triangle with square area leads to a smaller Pythagorean triangle with square area, completing the proof.
