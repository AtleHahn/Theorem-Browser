Statement

Let F_1,\ldots,F_n be independent fair signs: each takes the values -1,1 with probability 1/2, and all 2^n sign sequences have equal probability. Put X_n=\sum_{j=1}^nF_j, where n\ge1. For every t>0,

\mathbb P(X_n>t)\le e^{-t^2/(2n)}.

In particular, for n\ge2, \mathbb P(X_n>\sqrt{2n\ln n})\le1/n.

Level
High school only
Level justification

The statement explicitly defines the finite probability space; probabilities are fractions of the 2^n sign sequences, and all formulas use elementary functions.

Proof 1: Apply Azuma to the partial sums

Symmetric upper-tail Azuma inequality (dependency)

Let (X_k,\mathcal F_k)_{k=0}^N be a real-valued supermartingale, where N\ge1. Let c_1,\ldots,c_N be positive deterministic constants such that |X_k-X_{k-1}|\le c_k almost surely. Then for every \epsilon>0,

\mathbb P(X_N-X_0\ge\epsilon)\le\exp\left(-\frac{\epsilon^2}{2\sum_{k=1}^Nc_k^2}\right).

Argument

Let Fi be a sequence of independent and identically distributed random coin flips (i.e., let Fi be equally likely to be −1 or 1 independent of the other values of Fi). With X_0=0 and the filtration generated by the first i flips, defining {\displaystyle X_{i}=\sum _{j=1}^{i}F_{j}} yields a martingale with |Xk − Xk−1| ≤ 1, allowing us to apply the symmetric upper-tail Azuma inequality stated above. Specifically, we get

{\displaystyle \operatorname {P} (X_{n}>t)\leq \exp \left({\frac {-t^{2}}{2n}}\right).}

For example, if we set t proportional to n, then this tells us that although the maximum possible value of Xn scales linearly with n, the probability that the sum scales linearly with n decreases exponentially fast with n.

For n\ge2, if we set {\displaystyle t={\sqrt {2n\ln n}}} we get:

{\displaystyle \operatorname {P} \left(X_{n}>{\sqrt {2n\ln n}}\right)\leq {\frac {1}{n}},}

which means that the probability of an upward deviation of more than {\displaystyle {\sqrt {2n\ln n}}} approaches 0 as n goes to infinity.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

All random variables live on the finite tree of 2^n equally likely sign sequences. Conditional expectation here is the arithmetic average over continuations of a fixed prefix; each next sign has conditional average zero. Thus the martingale condition and unit increment bound can be checked using elementary undergraduate probability, after learning the finite-tree definition of a martingale. Substitution into the supplied inequality then gives the bound. General measure-theoretic conditional expectation or a proof of Azuma is unnecessary.
