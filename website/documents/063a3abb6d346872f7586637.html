Statement

The subsystem \mathrm{ACA_0} of second-order arithmetic is not omega-complete: it can prove each fixed numerical instance of a family without proving the single universally quantified family. Here RT_{<\infty}^{n} says that, for every finite number of colors, every coloring of the n-element subsets of \mathbb N has an infinite homogeneous set.

Level
Beyond undergraduate level
Level justification

Understanding the formal subsystem ACA0 and its distinction between instance provability and uniform provability requires background in second-order arithmetic and reverse mathematics beyond a short introductory reading. Under the statement-understanding criterion, a bright US college student would need more than approximately 30 minutes of focused background research to understand the full claim.

Proof 1: Compare fixed-arity and uniform provability — outline

For any fixed n\geq3, \mathrm{ACA}_0\vdash RT_{<\infty}^n. However, \mathrm{ACA}_0\not\vdash\forall n\,RT_{<\infty}^n.

Roughly, given some n, the proof of RT_{<\infty}^n can be carried out in \mathrm{ACA}_0, but there is no proof that works uniformly over all n. That is, the “for any n” part was external to the system. This shows that \mathrm{ACA}_0 is not \omega-complete.

Level justification

The proof contrasts a metatheoretic family of individual formal proofs with a single internally universally quantified sentence. Understanding why the distinction establishes omega-incompleteness requires the formal subsystem ACA0, second-order arithmetic, coding of finite colorings and formal provability. These prerequisites exceed a small extension of ordinary undergraduate proof methods, even though the cited Ramsey results are accepted.
