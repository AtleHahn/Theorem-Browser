Statement

A real supermartingale (X_n,\mathcal F_n)_{n\ge1} satisfying |X_n|\le M almost surely for a finite constant M converges almost surely to a finite real random variable.

Proof 1: Exclude rational upcrossings by the bounded-profit estimate — outline

Work outside the null set where the bounds |X_n|\leq M fail for some n. In the event that the sequence X_1,X_2,\ldots does not converge, then \liminf X_n and \limsup X_n differ. If also the sequence is bounded, then there are some real numbers a and b such that a<b and the sequence crosses the interval [a,b] infinitely often. That is, the sequence is eventually less than a, and at a later time exceeds b, and at an even later time is less than a, and so forth ad infinitum. These periods where the sequence starts below a and later exceeds b are called "upcrossings".

Consider a stock market game in which at time t, one may buy or sell shares of the stock at price X_t. On the one hand, it can be shown from the definition of a supermartingale that for any N\in\mathbb N there is no bounded nonnegative predictable holding strategy with positive expected profit after playing this game up to time N. On the other hand, if the prices cross a fixed interval [a,b] very often, then the following strategy seems to do well: buy one unit of the stock when the price drops below a, and sell it when the price exceeds b. Indeed, if U_N is the number of upcrossings in the sequence by time N, then the profit at time N is at least (b-a)U_N-2M: each upcrossing provides at least b-a profit, and if the last action was a "buy", then in the worst case the buying price was at most M and the current price is -M. But this strategy has expected profit at most 0, so necessarily

\mathbb E[U_N]\leq\frac{2M}{b-a}.

By the monotone convergence theorem for expectations, this means that

\mathbb E\left[\lim_{N\to\infty}U_N\right]\leq\frac{2M}{b-a},

so the expected number of upcrossings in the whole sequence is finite.

It follows that the infinite-crossing event for interval [a,b] occurs with probability 0. By a union bound over all rational a and b, with probability 1, no rational interval exists which is crossed infinitely often. If for all a,b\in\mathbb Q with a<b there are finitely many upcrossings of interval [a,b], then the limit inferior and limit superior of the sequence must agree, so the bounded sequence must converge to a finite limit. This shows that the supermartingale converges with probability 1.
