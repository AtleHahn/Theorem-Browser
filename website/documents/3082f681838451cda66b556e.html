Statement

Graphs are finite and simple. A clique is a set of pairwise adjacent vertices; its maximum size is the clique number. A proper coloring assigns different colors to adjacent vertices; the least number of colors is the chromatic number. An induced subgraph keeps a vertex subset and all edges between its vertices. A graph is perfect if these two numbers agree for every induced subgraph. A graph is bipartite if its vertices split into two sets with no edge inside either set. Every bipartite graph is perfect. The empty graph has clique number and chromatic number zero.

Level
High school only
Level justification

Clique, proper coloring, induced subgraph, perfect graph and bipartition are all defined using finite vertices and edges. The nested condition simply asks for equality of two integer counts after any vertex deletion. These local definitions are understandable by finite examples and elementary counting, without an undergraduate graph-theory prerequisite.

Proof 1: The two-color partition in every induced subgraph — outline

In a bipartite graph containing an edge, the optimal number of colors is (by definition) two, and (since bipartite graphs are triangle-free) the maximum clique size is also two. Also, any induced subgraph of a bipartite graph remains bipartite. In a nonempty independent induced subgraph the chromatic number and clique number are both one; for the empty induced subgraph they are both zero. Therefore, bipartite graphs are perfect.

Level
High school only
Level justification

Every induced subgraph inherits the two-part partition. If it has an edge both numbers are two, if it has vertices but no edges both are one, and if empty both are zero. All definitions are supplied and only elementary finite graph reasoning is needed.
