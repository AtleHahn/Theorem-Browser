Statement

Let D be a nonempty connected open subset of \mathbb R or \mathbb C, and let f,g be analytic on D. If f=g on a subset S\subseteq D with an accumulation point c\in D, then f=g throughout D.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Real analysis and calculus supply limits, derivatives, series and integration. Complex differentiability, primitives and logarithms are direct extensions, and circle integrals are parametrized ordinary integrals. The full claims use no Riemann surfaces, residues or abstract differential forms.

Proof 1: Identity theorem for analytic functions — proof

Here neighborhoods may be taken to be intervals in the real case and disks in the complex case. First we show that f=g in a neighborhood of c.

To prove this, it is enough to show that {\displaystyle f^{(n)}(c)=g^{(n)}(c)} for all {\displaystyle n\geq 0}, since both functions are analytic.

If this is not the case, let {\displaystyle m} be the smallest nonnegative integer with {\displaystyle f^{(m)}(c)\neq g^{(m)}(c)}. By analyticity, we have the following Taylor series representation in some open neighborhood U of {\displaystyle c}:

{\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}(f-g)(z)&{}=(z-c)^{m}\cdot \left[{\frac {(f-g)^{(m)}(c)}{m!}}+{\frac {(z-c)\cdot (f-g)^{(m+1)}(c)}{(m+1)!}}+\cdots \right]\\[6pt]&{}=(z-c)^{m}\cdot h(z).\end{aligned}}}

By continuity, {\displaystyle h} is non-zero in some small open neighborhood {\displaystyle B} around {\displaystyle c}. But then {\displaystyle f-g\neq 0} on the punctured set {\displaystyle B-\{c\}}. This contradicts the assumption that {\displaystyle c} is an accumulation point of {\displaystyle \{f=g\}}.

Define the set on which {\displaystyle f} and {\displaystyle g} have the same Taylor expansion:

{\displaystyle T=\left\{z\in D\mathrel {\Big \vert } f^{(k)}(z)=g^{(k)}(z){\text{ for all }}k\geq 0\right\}=\bigcap _{k=0}^{\infty }\left\{z\in D\mathrel {\Big \vert } {\bigl (}f^{(k)}-g^{(k)}{\bigr )}(z)=0\right\}.}

We'll show {\displaystyle T} is nonempty, open, and closed. Then by connectedness of {\displaystyle D}, {\displaystyle T} must be all of {\displaystyle D}, which implies {\displaystyle f=g} on {\displaystyle T=D}.

By the local argument, {\displaystyle f=g} in a neighborhood centered at {\displaystyle c} in {\displaystyle D}, they have the same Taylor series at {\displaystyle c}, so {\displaystyle c\in T}, hence {\displaystyle T} is nonempty.

As {\displaystyle f} and {\displaystyle g} are analytic on {\displaystyle D}, {\displaystyle \forall w\in T}, the Taylor series of {\displaystyle f} and {\displaystyle g} at {\displaystyle w} have non-zero radius of convergence. Therefore, the open neighborhood {\displaystyle B_{r}(w)} also lies in {\displaystyle T} for some {\displaystyle r}. So {\displaystyle T} is open.

By analyticity of {\displaystyle f} and {\displaystyle g}, they have analytic derivatives, so all {\displaystyle \textstyle f^{(n)},g^{(n)}} are continuous. This means that {\displaystyle \textstyle {\bigl \{}z\in D\mathrel {\big \vert } {\bigl (}f^{(k)}-g^{(k)}{\bigr )}(z)=0{\bigr \}}} is closed for all {\displaystyle k}. {\displaystyle T} is an intersection of closed sets, so it's closed.

Use the least nonzero Taylor term to establish local identity and then show the set of all equal derivatives is nonempty, open and closed. Undergraduate power-series analysis and connectedness suffice in both real and complex cases.
