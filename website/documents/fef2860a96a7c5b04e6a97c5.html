Statement

In the Euclidean plane, suppose a single circle of positive radius has been drawn and its center is given. Every point constructible from given geometric data by finitely many compass-and-straightedge operations can be constructed from the same data, this fixed circle and its center using an unmarked straightedge alone. Lines may be drawn through constructed points, their intersections found, and intersections with the given circle used. A circle needed as intermediate data is specified by its center and a point on it; the assertion concerns constructing points and lines, not drawing new circular arcs.

Level
High school only
Level justification

The tools and permitted operations are elementary Euclidean construction vocabulary, explicitly explained.

Proof 1: Parallels and coordinate square roots (external)

A supporting source can be found here (§11.2.2, Theorems 1–6, printed pp.206–207; §11.3.3, printed pp.216–217; own coordinate completion in adaptation.). The deduction below supplies the argument for the stored statement.

Cuppens §11.2.2 constructs arbitrary parallels from one parallelogram, using complete quadrilaterals; two diameters of the given circle supply a rectangle. The following coordinate completion is supplied here. Parallels permit affine addition, subtraction, multiplication and division of directed coordinates by the usual similar-triangle constructions (nonzero divisors only; auxiliary lines chosen transverse). For any chord UV, its antipodal point Uprime gives VUprime perpendicular to UV by Thales; translate with parallels. Thus construct orthogonal axes through the given center O, and use their intersections with the fixed circle as equal unit vectors. Every given point has constructible coordinates by parallel projection. For a positive constructible scalar a, set x=(a-1)/(a+1). The vertical line at x intersects the unit circle at ordinate y with y squared=4a/(a+1)^2. Choose y positive; then sqrt(a)=y(a+1)/2. For a=0 use zero directly. This uses only arithmetic, parallels and intersections with the fixed circle. Any other circle specified by center and a point has an equation whose coefficients are obtained by those arithmetic operations. A line-circle intersection reduces to a quadratic, solvable by the preceding square-root construction. Subtracting equations of two circles gives their radical axis, reducing distinct nonconcentric circles to line-circle intersection. Concentric unequal circles have no intersections; coincident circles supply no isolated new points; tangency uses discriminant zero. Handle vertical lines by exchanging coordinate roles. Simulate each operation in the original finite construction. Auxiliary points and transverse lines can be chosen to avoid the finitely many degenerate configurations at each step. This simulates arbitrary finite initial constructions without drawing new circles.
