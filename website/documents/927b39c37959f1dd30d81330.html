Statement

Let \Lambda\subset\mathbb R^d be a full-rank lattice of fundamental volume L>0. If a bounded measurable convex set X\subset\mathbb R^d is centrally symmetric about 0 and has volume greater than 2^dL, then X contains a nonzero point of \Lambda.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Linear algebra supplies a lattice as the integer span of a basis and its fundamental volume as the absolute basis determinant. Convexity, central symmetry and ordinary volume describe the remaining geometric objects. For bounded convex sets, no arbitrary measurable-function integration is needed.

Proof 1: Scale by one half and take a symmetric midpoint

Local Blichfeldt lemma

Let d\ge1 and let \Lambda=\{\sum_{i=1}^d z_i v_i:z_i\in\mathbb Z\} for a basis v_1,\ldots,v_d of \mathbb R^d. Let L>0 be the volume of a fundamental parallelotope. If S\subset\mathbb R^d is bounded and Lebesgue measurable of volume A, some translate of S contains at least \lceil A/L\rceil distinct points of \Lambda. Equivalently, S contains that many distinct points all of whose pairwise differences lie in \Lambda.

Just as the integer lattice divides the plane into squares, an arbitrary lattice divides its space into fundamental regions (called parallelotopes) with the property that any one of these regions can be translated onto any other of them by adding the coordinates of a unique lattice point. Take these regions half-open so they partition the space.

The basic idea of the proof is to cut S into pieces along the boundaries of these parallelotopes. Then, translate each of those pieces by a lattice vector so that they all move into the same parallelotope. This translation may cause some points of the parallelotope to be covered more than once. If the combined volume of the translated pieces is counted with multiplicity (counting each point by the number of times it is covered) it remains equal to A. Put n=\lceil A/L\rceil. If A=0 the assertion is immediate. Otherwise, if the whole parallelotope were covered with multiplicity at most n-1, the total volume would be at most (n-1)L, less than A. Therefore, some point p of the parallelotope must be covered with multiplicity at least n. A translation that takes p to the origin will take the n points of S that covered p to lattice points, which is what was required.

Instead of asking for a translation for which there are n lattice points, an equivalent form of the theorem states that S itself contains a set of n points, all of whose pairwise differences belong to the lattice.

Apply the lemma

Let {\displaystyle X} be any centrally symmetric set with volume greater than {\displaystyle 2^{d}L}, and scale it down by a factor of two to obtain a set {\displaystyle {\tfrac {1}{2}}X} of volume greater than {\displaystyle L}. By Blichfeldt's theorem, {\displaystyle {\tfrac {1}{2}}X} has two points {\displaystyle p} and {\displaystyle q} whose coordinatewise difference belongs to \Lambda. Reversing the shrinking operation, {\displaystyle 2p} and {\displaystyle 2q} belong to {\displaystyle X}. By symmetry {\displaystyle -2q} also belongs to {\displaystyle X}, and by convexity the midpoint of {\displaystyle 2p} and {\displaystyle -2q} belongs to {\displaystyle X}. But this midpoint is {\displaystyle p-q}, a nonzero point of \Lambda.

Level justification

The last midpoint step is elementary, but the included measurable-set lemma and lattice-volume framework exceed a confidently small extension; retain conservatively.
