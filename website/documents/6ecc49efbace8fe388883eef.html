Statement

Let n,r be positive integers with n\ge2r. If \mathcal A is a family of distinct r-element subsets of an n-element set and every pair of its members intersects, then |\mathcal A|\le\binom{n-1}{r-1}.

Level
High school only
Level justification

The hypothesis and bound concern finite subsets, their intersections and an explicitly finite binomial count.

Proof 1: Katona cyclic-order double counting — outline

Cyclic interval lemma

Let {\displaystyle {\mathcal {A}}} be any intersecting family of {\displaystyle r}-element subsets of an {\displaystyle n}-element set. Arrange all {\displaystyle n} elements into any cyclic order, and consider the sets from {\displaystyle {\mathcal {A}}} that form intervals of length {\displaystyle r} within this chosen cyclic order. For example if {\displaystyle n=8} and {\displaystyle r=3}, one possible cyclic order for the numbers {\displaystyle \{1,2,...,8\}} is the order {\displaystyle (3,1,5,4,2,7,6,8)}, which has eight 3-element intervals (including the ones that wrap around):

{\displaystyle (3,1,5)}, {\displaystyle (1,5,4)}, {\displaystyle (5,4,2)}, {\displaystyle (4,2,7)}, {\displaystyle (2,7,6)}, {\displaystyle (7,6,8)}, {\displaystyle (6,8,3)}, and {\displaystyle (8,3,1)}.

If the interval subfamily is empty, its bound is immediate. Otherwise, only some of these intervals can belong to {\displaystyle {\mathcal {A}}}, because they do not all intersect. Katona's key observation is that at most {\displaystyle r} intervals from a single cyclic order may belong to {\displaystyle {\mathcal {A}}}. This is because, if {\displaystyle (a_{1},a_{2},\dots ,a_{r})} is one of these intervals, then every other interval of the same cyclic order that belongs to {\displaystyle {\mathcal {A}}} separates {\displaystyle a_{i}} from {\displaystyle a_{i+1}}, for some {\displaystyle i}, by containing precisely one of these two elements. The two intervals that separate these elements are disjoint because n\ge2r, so at most one of them can belong to {\displaystyle {\mathcal {A}}}. Thus, the number of intervals in {\displaystyle {\mathcal {A}}} is at most one plus the number {\displaystyle r-1} of pairs that can be separated.

Double count

Based on this idea, it is possible to count the pairs {\displaystyle (S,C)}, where {\displaystyle S} is a set in {\displaystyle {\mathcal {A}}} and {\displaystyle C} is a cyclic order for which {\displaystyle S} is an interval, in two ways. First, for each set {\displaystyle S} one may generate {\displaystyle C} by choosing one of {\displaystyle r!} permutations of {\displaystyle S} and {\displaystyle (n-r)!} permutations of the remaining elements, showing that the number of pairs is {\displaystyle |{\mathcal {A}}|r!(n-r)!}. And second, there are {\displaystyle (n-1)!} cyclic orders, each of which has at most {\displaystyle r} intervals of {\displaystyle {\mathcal {A}}}, so the number of pairs is at most {\displaystyle r(n-1)!}. Comparing these two counts gives the inequality

{\displaystyle |{\mathcal {A}}|r!(n-r)!\leq r(n-1)!}

and dividing both sides by {\displaystyle r!(n-r)!} gives the result

{\displaystyle |{\mathcal {A}}|\leq {\frac {r(n-1)!}{r!(n-r)!}}={n-1 \choose r-1}.}
