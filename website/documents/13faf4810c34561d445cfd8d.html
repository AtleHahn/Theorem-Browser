Statement

Let P be a sentence of Peano arithmetic \mathrm{PA}, and let \operatorname{Prov}_{\mathrm{PA}}(\ulcorner P\urcorner) be its usual arithmetized provability assertion. If \mathrm{PA}\vdash\operatorname{Prov}_{\mathrm{PA}}(\ulcorner P\urcorner)\to P, then \mathrm{PA}\vdash P.

Proof 1: Apply diagonalization and the provability conditions

Write \Box A for \operatorname{Prov}_{\mathrm{PA}}(\ulcorner A\urcorner) and \vdash for PA derivability. The diagonal lemma gives a sentence \Psi with \vdash\Psi\leftrightarrow(\Box\Psi\to P). The Hilbert–Bernays provability conditions give necessitation, \vdash\Box A\to\Box\Box A, and \vdash\Box(A\to B)\to(\Box A\to\Box B).

Much of the proof does not make use of the assumption {\displaystyle \Box P\to P}, so for ease of understanding, the proof below is subdivided to leave the parts depending on {\displaystyle \Box P\to P} until the end.

Let {\displaystyle P} be the arithmetic sentence in the statement.

  1. Use the diagonal lemma to choose a sentence {\displaystyle \Psi } such that {\displaystyle \vdash \Psi \leftrightarrow (\Box \Psi \rightarrow P)}.

  2. {\displaystyle \vdash \Psi \rightarrow (\Box \Psi \rightarrow P)}, from 1.

  3. {\displaystyle \vdash \Box (\Psi \rightarrow (\Box \Psi \rightarrow P))}, from 2 by the necessitation rule.

  4. {\displaystyle \vdash \Box \Psi \rightarrow \Box (\Box \Psi \rightarrow P)}, from 3 and the box distributivity rule.

  5. {\displaystyle \vdash \Box (\Box \Psi \rightarrow P)\rightarrow (\Box \Box \Psi \rightarrow \Box P)}, box distributivity rule " {\displaystyle \vdash \Box (A\rightarrow B)\rightarrow (\Box A\rightarrow \Box B)} " with {\displaystyle A=\Box \Psi } and {\displaystyle B=P}.

  6. {\displaystyle \vdash \Box \Psi \rightarrow (\Box \Box \Psi \rightarrow \Box P)}, from 4 and 5.

  7. {\displaystyle \vdash \Box \Psi \rightarrow \Box \Box \Psi }, internal necessitation rule.

  8. {\displaystyle \vdash \Box \Psi \rightarrow \Box P}, from 6 and 7.

    Now comes the part of the proof where the hypothesis is used.

  9. Assume that {\displaystyle \vdash \Box P\rightarrow P}. Roughly speaking, it is a theorem that if {\displaystyle P} is provable, then it is, in fact true. This is a claim of soundness.

  10. {\displaystyle \vdash \Box \Psi \rightarrow P}, from 8 and 9.

  11. {\displaystyle \vdash (\Box \Psi \rightarrow P)\rightarrow \Psi }, from 1.

  12. {\displaystyle \vdash \Psi }, from 10 and 11.

  13. {\displaystyle \vdash \Box \Psi }, from 12 by the necessitation rule.

  14. {\displaystyle \vdash P}, from 13 and 10.

Proof 2: Adjoin the negation and invoke second incompleteness — outline

Use the formalized deduction theorem: inside T=\mathrm{PA}+\neg P, a T-proof of contradiction can be converted into a PA-proof of \neg P\to\bot, hence of P. Gödel’s second incompleteness theorem applies to consistent recursively axiomatized extensions of PA.

  1. Since {\displaystyle {\mathit {PA}}\vdash {\mathrm {Prov} _{PA}(P)\rightarrow P}} by assumption, we also have {\displaystyle {\mathit {PA}}\vdash {\neg P\rightarrow \neg \mathrm {Prov} _{PA}(P)}}, which implies {\displaystyle \{{\mathit {PA}},\neg P\}\vdash {\neg \mathrm {Prov} _{PA}(P)}}.
  2. Now, the hybrid theory {\displaystyle \{{\mathit {PA}},\neg P\}} can reason as follows:
    1. Suppose {\displaystyle \{{\mathit {PA}},\neg P\}} is inconsistent, then PA proves {\displaystyle \neg P\to \bot {}}, which is the same as {\displaystyle P}.
    2. However, {\displaystyle \{{\mathit {PA}},\neg P\}} already knows that {\displaystyle \neg \mathrm {Prov} _{PA}(P)}, a contradiction.
    3. Therefore, {\displaystyle \{{\mathit {PA}},\neg P\}} is consistent.
  3. The hybrid theory thus proves its own consistency. By Gödel's second incompleteness theorem, this implies {\displaystyle \{{\mathit {PA}},\neg P\}} is inconsistent.
  4. Thus, PA proves {\displaystyle \neg P\to \bot {}}, which is the same as {\displaystyle P}.
