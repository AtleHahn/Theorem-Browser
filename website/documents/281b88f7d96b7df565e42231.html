If X is Hausdorff and S\subseteq X is compact, then S is closed in X.

Proof 1: Separated neighborhoods and finite subcover

Let S be a subset of a Hausdorff space X. Observe first the following: if a is a limit point of S, then any finite collection C of open sets, such that each open set U\in C is disjoint from some neighborhood V_U of a, fails to be a cover of S. Indeed, the intersection of the finite family of sets V_U is a neighborhood W of a in X. Since a is a limit point of S, W must contain a point x in S. This x\in S is not covered by the family C, because every U in C is disjoint from V_U and hence disjoint from W, which contains x.

If S is compact but not closed, then it has a limit point a\notin S. Consider a collection C' consisting of an open neighborhood N(x) for each x\in S, chosen by the Hausdorff property to not intersect some neighborhood V_x of a. Then C' is an open cover of S, but any finite subcollection of C' has the form of C discussed previously, and thus cannot be an open subcover of S. This contradicts the compactness of S. Hence, every limit point of S is in S, so S is closed.

Level justification

Separate an external limit point from each point of the compact set, then a finite subcover gives a neighborhood missing the set, a contradiction. This is a standard Hausdorff compactness proof.
