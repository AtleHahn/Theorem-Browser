Statement

Let \mathcal F be any family of positive-radius balls of uniformly bounded radii in a separable metric space, using either open balls throughout or closed balls throughout. There is a finite or countably infinite disjoint subfamily \mathcal G such that every B\in\mathcal F meets some C\in\mathcal G with B\subseteq5C. In particular \bigcup\mathcal F\subseteq\bigcup_{C\in\mathcal G}5C. Here cB denotes the ball with the same center as B and radius multiplied by c.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Metric balls, bounded radii, disjointness and separability are basic analysis/topology. The enlarged balls are explicitly radius multiples, and all covering or multiplicity conditions are ordinary set inclusions/counts, without measure integration.

Proof 1: Dyadic bands and maximal disjoint families

The empty family is immediate. Otherwise put

R:=\sup\{\operatorname{rad}(B):B\in\mathcal F\}.

Consider the partition of \mathcal F into subcollections \mathcal F_n, n\ge0, defined by

\mathcal F_n=\{B\in\mathcal F:2^{-n-1}R<\operatorname{rad}(B)\le2^{-n}R\}.

That is, \mathcal F_n consists of the balls B whose radius is in (2^{-n-1}R,2^{-n}R]. A sequence \mathcal G_n, with \mathcal G_n\subseteq\mathcal F_n, is defined inductively as follows. First, set \mathcal H_0=\mathcal F_0 and let \mathcal G_0 be a maximal disjoint subcollection of \mathcal H_0 (such a subcollection exists by Zorn's lemma). Assuming that \mathcal G_0,\ldots,\mathcal G_n have been selected, let

\mathcal H_{n+1}= \{B\in\mathcal F_{n+1}: B\cap C=\varnothing \text{ for every }C\in\mathcal G_0\cup\mathcal G_1\cup\cdots\cup\mathcal G_n\},

and let \mathcal G_{n+1} be a maximal disjoint subcollection of \mathcal H_{n+1}. The subcollection

\mathcal G:=\bigcup_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathcal G_n

of \mathcal F satisfies the requirements of the theorem: \mathcal G is a disjoint collection, and is thus countable since the given metric space is separable. Indeed, the balls have pairwise disjoint nonempty open interiors, each meeting a fixed countable dense subset.

Moreover, every ball B\in\mathcal F intersects a ball C\in\mathcal G such that B\subseteq5C. Indeed, if we are given some B\in\mathcal F, there must be some n such that B belongs to \mathcal F_n. Either B does not belong to \mathcal H_n, which implies n>0 and means that B intersects a ball from the union of \mathcal G_0,\ldots,\mathcal G_{n-1}, or B\in\mathcal H_n and by maximality of \mathcal G_n, B intersects a ball in \mathcal G_n. In any case, B intersects a ball C that belongs to the union of \mathcal G_0,\ldots,\mathcal G_n. Such a ball C must have a radius larger than 2^{-n-1}R. Since the radius of B is less than or equal to 2^{-n}R, we can conclude by the triangle inequality that B\subseteq5C, as claimed. From this

\bigcup_{B\in\mathcal F}B\subseteq\bigcup_{C\in\mathcal G}5C

immediately follows.
