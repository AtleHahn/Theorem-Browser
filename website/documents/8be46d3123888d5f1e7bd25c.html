Statement

For positive integers a\ge b of at most h binary digits, the Euclidean algorithm using classical long division has total bit-operation cost O(h^2). Use the standard division bound O(s(\ell+1)) for an s-bit dividend and an \ell-bit quotient.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Understanding the statement involves Integer remainder algorithm; Binary digit lengths; Bit-operation complexity. These prerequisites fit undergraduate coursework or a brief extension of it. A bright US college student should be able to understand this statement after approximately 30 minutes of focused background research; this assesses understanding the claim, not its proof.

Proof 1: Bound and telescope the digit-cost sum

Combining Lamé’s bound of O(h) divisions with the division-cost bound in the statement shows that the Euclid's algorithm grows quadratically (h2) with the maximum number of binary digits h in the initial two numbers a and b. Let h_0,h_1,\ldots,h_N represent the bit lengths of the successive positive numbers, starting with a,b and ending with the last nonzero remainder; N is the number of divisions. Since the number of steps N grows linearly with h, the running time is bounded by

{\displaystyle O{\Big (}\sum _{i<N}h_{i}(h_{i}-h_{i+1}+2){\Big )}\subseteq O{\Big (}h\sum _{i<N}(h_{i}-h_{i+1}+2){\Big )}\subseteq O(h(h_{0}+2N))\subseteq O(h^{2}).}
