Statement

Let Q be an indefinite quadratic form with rational coefficients in n\ge5 variables. Then Q(x)=0 has a nonzero rational solution.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Understanding the statement involves Rational quadratic forms; Real and rational zeros; Indefiniteness. These prerequisites fit undergraduate coursework or a brief extension of it. A bright US college student should be able to understand this statement after approximately 30 minutes of focused background research; this assesses understanding the claim, not its proof.

Proof 1: Combine p-adic isotropy with the local-global theorem

Meyer's theorem is deduced from the Hasse–Minkowski theorem and the following statement:

A rational quadratic form in five or more variables represents zero nontrivially over the field \mathbb Q_p of p-adic numbers for all primes p.

Here the Hasse–Minkowski theorem says that a rational quadratic form has a nonzero rational zero if it has nonzero zeros over \mathbb R and every \mathbb Q_p. Indefiniteness supplies the real zero.

Level
Beyond undergraduate level
Level justification

Combine p-adic isotropy at all primes with the Hasse–Minkowski local-global theorem. Understanding p-adic fields and isotropy over completions requires substantial algebraic number theory.
