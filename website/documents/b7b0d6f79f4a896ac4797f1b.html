Statement

For an odd prime p, there are exactly (p-1)/2 nonzero square residue classes modulo p, represented distinctly by 1^2,2^2,\ldots,((p-1)/2)^2. The remaining (p-1)/2 nonzero classes are nonsquares.

Level
High school only
Level justification

The claim counts an explicitly listed finite family of square residue classes.

Mathematical Field
High School Mathematics
Required topics

Review notes
none

Proof 1: Count roots and pair opposite inputs

The proof uses the fact that the residue classes modulo a prime number are a field.

Because the modulus is prime, Lagrange's theorem applies: a nonzero polynomial of degree k can only have at most k roots. In particular, x2 ≡ a (mod p) has at most 2 solutions for each a. This immediately implies that besides 0 there are at least \frac{p-1}{2} distinct quadratic residues modulo p: each of the p − 1 possible values of x can only be accompanied by one other to give the same residue.

In fact, {\displaystyle (p-x)^{2}\equiv x^{2}{\pmod {p}}.} This is because {\displaystyle (p-x)^{2}\equiv p^{2}-{2}{x}{p}+x^{2}\equiv x^{2}{\pmod {p}}.} So, the {\displaystyle {\tfrac {p-1}{2}}} distinct quadratic residues are: {\displaystyle 1^{2},2^{2},...,({\tfrac {p-1}{2}})^{2}{\pmod {p}}.}

Proof 2: Count squares in the cyclic multiplicative group — outline

Use the fact that the multiplicative group of nonzero residues modulo p is cyclic of order p-1. The equality of the numbers of quadratic residues and nonresidues follows from this cyclic structure. To identify representatives, use the observation (p-x)^2\equiv x^2\pmod p: each opposite pair of nonzero inputs has its unique representative among 1,\ldots,(p-1)/2. Thus these (p-1)/2 squares cover all square values; because the count is exactly (p-1)/2, they are distinct.
