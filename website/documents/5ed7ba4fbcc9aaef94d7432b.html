Statement

Let F_0=0, F_1=1, and F_{j+1}=F_j+F_{j-1} for j\geq1. Every positive integer has a unique representation N=\sum_{i=0}^kF_{c_i} with c_0\geq2 and c_{i+1}>c_i+1. The largest-available-Fibonacci greedy construction produces this representation.

Level
High school only
Level justification

The statement specifies a Fibonacci recurrence and finite sums with restrictions on their indices; uniqueness and the greedy selection rule are elementary discrete concepts.

Proof 1: Greedy induction and largest-term uniqueness

Existence

The existence part of Zeckendorf's theorem can be proven by induction. The base case {\displaystyle n=1} has the Zeckendorf representation {\displaystyle n=1=F_{2}}. If n=F_j, the single term F_j is a Zeckendorf representation. Otherwise, assuming that all {\displaystyle k<n} have a Zeckendorf representation let {\displaystyle j} be such that {\displaystyle F_{j}<n<F_{j+1}}. Then, {\displaystyle n-F_{j}<n}. Therefore, there exists a Zeckendorf representation for {\displaystyle n-F_{j}}. The assumption {\displaystyle F_{j}<n<F_{j+1}} also implies that {\displaystyle F_{j-1}=F_{j+1}-F_{j}>n-F_{j}}. Therefore, the largest Fibonacci number in the Zeckendorf representation of {\displaystyle n-F_{j}} must be strictly less than {\displaystyle F_{j-1}}. This implies that the Zeckendorf representation of {\displaystyle n-F_{j}} together with {\displaystyle F_{j}} is a valid Zeckendorf representation for {\displaystyle n}.

Uniqueness

To prove uniqueness, one can use the identity

{\displaystyle \sum _{i=0}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor -1}F_{n-2i}=F_{n+1}-1}

This can be proven by induction, or by a counting argument in which one realizes both sides of the identity are two ways of counting the number of ways of writing {\displaystyle n} using sums of {\displaystyle 1} and at least one {\displaystyle 2}.

To get a contradiction, assume there are two different representations, with integers {\displaystyle c_{i}}, for {\displaystyle i=0,1,\ldots ,r}, and {\displaystyle d_{i}}, for {\displaystyle i=0,1,\ldots ,s}, such that {\displaystyle c_{0},d_{0}\geq 2}, {\displaystyle c_{i+1}>c_{i}+1}, {\displaystyle d_{i+1}>d_{i}+1}, and

{\displaystyle \sum _{i=0}^{r}F_{c_{i}}=\sum _{i=0}^{s}F_{d_{i}}}

Choose such a pair with the smallest possible total number of summands, r+s+2.

If {\displaystyle c_{r}=d_{s}}, that is if the largest terms {\displaystyle F_{c_{r}}} and {\displaystyle F_{d_{s}}} of the two Zeckendorf representations are equal, then they can be deleted from both representations and obtain two new Zeckendorf representations that are equal and have fewer number of summands. This contradicts minimality of the total number of summands. Therefore, {\displaystyle c_{r}\neq d_{s}}. By swapping the names {\displaystyle c} and {\displaystyle d}, if necessary, one can assume that {\displaystyle c_{r}>d_{s}}. However, the sum

{\displaystyle \sum _{i=0}^{s}F_{d_{i}}\leq \sum _{i=0}^{\lfloor d_{s}/2\rfloor -1}F_{d_{s}-2i}=F_{d_{s}+1}-1\leq F_{c_{r}}-1<F_{c_{r}}}

Here the first inequality compares the sum with that of the Zeckendorf representation, which largest term is {\displaystyle F_{d_{s}}}, and uses the largest Fibonacci numbers allowed. That is, using every second Fibonacci number descending from {\displaystyle F_{d_{s}}} and stopping at {\displaystyle F_{2}} or {\displaystyle F_{3}}. The equation is the identity above. This shows that, even if the sum {\displaystyle \sum _{i=0}^{s}F_{d_{i}}} uses all the largest Fibonacci numbers allowed, its sum is still strictly less than the largest term {\displaystyle F_{c_{r}}} in the sum {\displaystyle \sum _{i=0}^{r}F_{c_{i}}}. Therefore, one cannot have two different Zeckendorf representations giving the same sum.
