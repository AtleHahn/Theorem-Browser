Statement

Consider a first-order Hilbert calculus whose propositional schemes include K and S as displayed below, whose only inference rules are modus ponens and generalization (from K infer \forall vK), and whose quantifier schemes include

(\forall v(H\to K))\to(H\to\forall vK)

whenever v is not free in H. Here K is the scheme P\to(Q\to P) and S is (P\to(Q\to R))\to((P\to Q)\to(P\to R)). A derivation from premises is a finite sequence of premises, axiom instances and applications of those inference rules. The notation T\vdash G means that such a sequence ends in G.

Let T be a first-order theory and F,G formulas. If a derivation of G from T\cup\{F\} never generalizes on any variable free in F, then T\vdash F\to G.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

The calculus and finite derivations are explicitly specified. The extra preparation is free/bound-variable syntax and its relation to the stated generalization restriction, a small extension of undergraduate quantified reasoning and induction rather than general metamathematics.

Proof 1: Extend the line transformation across universal generalization

Local propositional line-transformation lemma

From the axiom schemes K and S one can quickly deduce the theorem scheme P\to P (reflexivity of implication), which is used below:

  1. (P\to((Q\to P)\to P))\to((P\to(Q\to P))\to(P\to P)) from S with P,(Q\to P),P.
  2. P\to((Q\to P)\to P) from K with P,(Q\to P).
  3. (P\to(Q\to P))\to(P\to P) from modus ponens applied to step 2 and step 1.
  4. P\to(Q\to P) from K with P,Q.
  5. P\to P from modus ponens applied to step 4 and step 3.

Suppose that \Gamma and H together prove C using axioms and modus ponens, and we wish to show that \Gamma proves H\to C. For each step S in the deduction that is a premise in \Gamma or an axiom, we can apply modus ponens to K, S\to(H\to S), to get H\to S. If the step is H itself, we apply the theorem scheme to get H\to H. If the step is the result of applying modus ponens to A and A\to S, we first make sure that these have been converted to H\to A and H\to(A\to S) and then we take S in the form

(H\to(A\to S))\to((H\to A)\to(H\to S)),

and apply modus ponens to get (H\to A)\to(H\to S) and then again to get H\to S. At the end of the proof we will have H\to C as required, except that now it only depends on \Gamma, not on H.

Apply the transformation and handle generalization

In order to convert a proof of G from T\cup\{F\} to one of F\to G from T, one deals with steps of the proof of G that are axioms, premises in T, or result from application of modus ponens in the same way as in the local propositional lemma; a step F is handled by F\to F. Steps that result from application of the rule of generalization are dealt with via the following quantifier axiom (valid whenever the variable v is not free in formula H):

(\forall v(H\to K))\to(H\to\forall vK).

The restriction that F be a closed formula can be relaxed given that the free variables in F have not been varied in the deduction of G from T\cup\{F\}. Under the stated restriction, v is not free in F at each generalization step, so we can take H to be F. This axiom allows one to deduce F\to\forall vK from F\to K and generalization, which is just what is needed whenever the rule of generalization is applied to some K in the proof of G.
