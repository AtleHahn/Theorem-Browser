Statement

The real algebraic group G=\{x+iy:x,y\in\mathbb R,\ x^2+y^2=1\} under complex multiplication is connected and commutative, hence solvable. Its real representation \rho(x+iy)=\begin{pmatrix}x&y\\-y&x\end{pmatrix} on \mathbb R^2 has no invariant real line. Thus the algebraic-closure hypothesis in the invariant-line form of Lie–Kolchin cannot be omitted.

Level
Beyond undergraduate level
Level justification

The group-level counterexample requires real algebraic groups, while its invariant-line obstruction uses elementary real linear algebra.

Proof 1: Evaluate the supplied representation at i — outline

The standard unit circle, viewed as the set of complex numbers {\displaystyle \{x+iy\in \mathbb {C} \mid x^{2}+y^{2}=1\}} of absolute value one is a 1-dimensional commutative (and therefore solvable) linear algebraic group over the real numbers which has a 2-dimensional representation into the special orthogonal group SO(2) without an invariant (real) line. Here the image {\displaystyle \rho (z)} of {\displaystyle z=x+iy} is the orthogonal matrix

{\displaystyle {\begin{pmatrix}x&y\\-y&x\end{pmatrix}}.}

The source asserts that this representation has no invariant real line; it does not supply the eigenline calculation. Connectedness of the real algebraic circle is implicit in its use as a counterexample.

Level justification

The missing eigenline calculation is elementary, but the example also asserts algebraic-group connectedness and a rational representation to test the Lie–Kolchin hypothesis. Those algebraic-geometric group concepts are not replaced by ordinary circle topology; retain beyond undergraduate provisionally because their full meaning is not supplied.
