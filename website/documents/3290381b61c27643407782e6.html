Statement

In every nondegenerate Euclidean triangle with positive side lengths a,b,c, each side is shorter than the sum of the other two. Equivalently, |a-b|<c<a+b, and the longest side is less than the semiperimeter (a+b+c)/2.

Level
High school only
Level justification

The conclusion compares ordinary Euclidean side lengths and their sums.

Proof 1: Extend a side to an isosceles triangle

Beginning with triangle ABC, an isosceles triangle is constructed with one side taken as BC and the other equal leg BD along the extension of side AB beyond B. The base angles satisfy \angle BCD=\angle BDC=\angle ADC, and \angle ACD>\angle BCD. Thus \angle ACD has larger measure than \angle ADC, so side AD is longer than side AC. However,

\overline{AD}=\overline{AB}+\overline{BD} =\overline{AB}+\overline{BC},

so the sum of the lengths of sides AB and BC is larger than the length of AC. Relabeling gives the other two inequalities.

For a proper triangle, this literally translates into three inequalities:

a+b>c,\qquad b+c>a,\qquad c+a>b.

A more succinct form of this inequality system is

|a-b|<c<a+b.

Another way to state it is

\max(a,b,c)<a+b+c-\max(a,b,c),

implying

2\max(a,b,c)<a+b+c,

and thus that the longest side length is less than the semiperimeter.
