Statement

Let K be an algebraically closed field and let P_1,\ldots,P_6 be distinct projective points. Write L_{ij} for the line through P_i,P_j. Set

C_1=L_{12}\cup L_{34}\cup L_{56},\qquad C_2=L_{23}\cup L_{45}\cup L_{61},

and

Q_1=L_{12}\cap L_{45},\quad Q_2=L_{23}\cap L_{56},\quad Q_3=L_{34}\cap L_{61}.

Assume all these lines and intersection points are defined, and C_1\cap C_2 consists of the nine distinct points P_1,\ldots,P_6,Q_1,Q_2,Q_3. Assume also that the six P_i lie on a conic Q, and none of the Q_i lies on Q. Then Q_1,Q_2,Q_3 are collinear.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Projective points, lines and conics are homogeneous linear/quadratic equations over a field, a limited extension of undergraduate algebra. Distinct intersections remove any need to understand multiplicity.

Proof 1: Adjoin the line through two intersections to the conic

The two cubics are unions of the alternating three side-lines, and their nine intersection points are the six vertices and the three opposite-side intersections. Let M be the line through Q_1,Q_2. The union of the conic through the six vertices and M is a cubic through eight of the nine points. By Cayley–Bacharach it also contains Q_3. Since Q_3 is not on the conic, it lies on M.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Multiply a conic equation by the line through two remaining points and apply the explicit nine-point cubic theorem. Undergraduate polynomial algebra and homogeneous projective coordinates suffice; no multiplicity theory is needed.
