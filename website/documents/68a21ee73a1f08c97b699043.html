Statement

Let K be a number field with ring of integers \mathcal O_K, let \mathfrak p be a nonzero prime ideal, and let \zeta\in\mathcal O_K be a primitive nth root of unity, where n\ge1 and n\notin\mathfrak p. Put q=|\mathcal O_K/\mathfrak p|. Let A=\{a_1,\ldots,a_m\} represent the cosets of the subgroup generated by \zeta in (\mathcal O_K/\mathfrak p)^\times, so m=(q-1)/n, and let \gamma\in\mathcal O_K\setminus\mathfrak p. For each i let \pi(i)\in\{1,\ldots,m\} and b(i)\in\{0,\ldots,n-1\} be uniquely specified by \gamma a_i\equiv\zeta^{b(i)}a_{\pi(i)}\pmod{\mathfrak p}. If (\gamma/\mathfrak p)_n is the unique nth root of unity congruent to \gamma^m modulo \mathfrak p, then \pi is a permutation and (\gamma/\mathfrak p)_n=\zeta^{\sum_i b(i)}.

Proof 1: Gauss's lemma for power residues — proof

Put \zeta_n=\zeta, \mu_n=\{1,\zeta_n,\ldots,\zeta_n^{n-1}\} and \mathrm N\mathfrak p=q. Then A\mu_n represents all nonzero residue classes.

The existence of the integers π(i) and b(i), and their uniqueness in the index and exponent ranges in the statement, come from the fact that A\mu_n is a representative set.

Assume that π(i) = π(j) = p, i.e.

{\displaystyle \gamma a_{i}\equiv \zeta _{n}^{r}a_{p}{\pmod {\mathfrak {p}}}}

and

{\displaystyle \gamma a_{j}\equiv \zeta _{n}^{s}a_{p}{\pmod {\mathfrak {p}}}.}

Then

{\displaystyle \zeta _{n}^{s-r}\gamma a_{i}\equiv \zeta _{n}^{s}a_{p}\equiv \gamma a_{j}{\pmod {\mathfrak {p}}}}

Because γ and {\displaystyle {\mathfrak {p}}} are coprime both sides can be divided by γ, giving

{\displaystyle \zeta _{n}^{s-r}a_{i}\equiv a_{j}{\pmod {\mathfrak {p}}},}

which, since A represents the cosets of \mu_n, implies s = r and i = j, showing that π is a permutation of the set {1, 2, …, m}.

Then on the one hand, by the definition of the power residue symbol,

{\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}(\gamma a_{1})(\gamma a_{2})\dots (\gamma a_{m})&=\gamma ^{\frac {\mathrm {N} {\mathfrak {p}}-1}{n}}a_{1}a_{2}\dots a_{m}\\&\equiv \left({\frac {\gamma }{\mathfrak {p}}}\right)_{n}a_{1}a_{2}\dots a_{m}{\pmod {\mathfrak {p}}},\end{aligned}}}

and on the other hand, since π is a permutation,

{\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}(\gamma a_{1})(\gamma a_{2})\dots (\gamma a_{m})&\equiv {\zeta _{n}^{b(1)}a_{\pi (1)}}{\zeta _{n}^{b(2)}a_{\pi (2)}}\dots {\zeta _{n}^{b(m)}a_{\pi (m)}}&{\pmod {\mathfrak {p}}}\\&\equiv \zeta _{n}^{b(1)+b(2)+\dots +b(m)}a_{\pi (1)}a_{\pi (2)}\dots a_{\pi (m)}&{\pmod {\mathfrak {p}}}\\&\equiv \zeta _{n}^{b(1)+b(2)+\dots +b(m)}a_{1}a_{2}\dots a_{m}&{\pmod {\mathfrak {p}}},\end{aligned}}}

so

{\displaystyle \left({\frac {\gamma }{\mathfrak {p}}}\right)_{n}a_{1}a_{2}\dots a_{m}\equiv \zeta _{n}^{b(1)+b(2)+\dots +b(m)}a_{1}a_{2}\dots a_{m}{\pmod {\mathfrak {p}}},}

and since for all 1 ≤ i ≤ m, ai and {\displaystyle {\mathfrak {p}}} are coprime, a1a2…am can be cancelled from both sides of the congruence,

{\displaystyle \left({\frac {\gamma }{\mathfrak {p}}}\right)_{n}\equiv \zeta _{n}^{b(1)+b(2)+\dots +b(m)}{\pmod {\mathfrak {p}}},}

and the theorem follows from the fact that no two distinct nth roots of unity can be congruent (mod {\displaystyle {\mathfrak {p}}}).
