Statement

Let n\ge1 and a_i,b_i\ge0, and set a=\sum_i a_i, b=\sum_i b_i. Define H(u,v)=u\log(u/v) when u,v>0, H(0,v)=0 for v\ge0, and H(u,0)=+\infty for u>0. Then \sum_iH(a_i,b_i)\ge H(a,b). If b>0 and the left side is finite, equality holds exactly when a_i/b_i=a/b for every index with b_i>0.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

The extended-real-valued logarithmic function, zero-mass conventions and equality cases require undergraduate analysis beyond ordinary logarithm arithmetic. A bright US college student should be able to understand the statement after about 30 minutes of focused background research; this rating concerns understanding the claim, not proving it.

Proof 1: Apply Jensen with weights proportional to the denominators

First suppose b>0 and no index has a_i>0,b_i=0. Delete terms with a_i=b_i=0, which contribute zero. All remaining b_i are positive. Set f(0)=0 when extending f(x)=x\log x to [0,\infty).

Notice that after setting {\displaystyle f(x)=x\log x} we have

{\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}\sum _{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\log {\frac {a_{i}}{b_{i}}}&{}=\sum _{i=1}^{n}b_{i}f\left({\frac {a_{i}}{b_{i}}}\right)=b\sum _{i=1}^{n}{\frac {b_{i}}{b}}f\left({\frac {a_{i}}{b_{i}}}\right)\\&{}\geq bf\left(\sum _{i=1}^{n}{\frac {b_{i}}{b}}{\frac {a_{i}}{b_{i}}}\right)=bf\left({\frac {1}{b}}\sum _{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\right)=bf\left({\frac {a}{b}}\right)\\&{}=a\log {\frac {a}{b}},\end{aligned}}}

where the inequality follows from Jensen's inequality since {\displaystyle {\frac {b_{i}}{b}}\geq 0}, {\displaystyle \sum _{i=1}^{n}{\frac {b_{i}}{b}}=1}, and {\displaystyle f} is convex.

The function is strictly convex; on (0,\infty), f''(x)=1/x>0. Jensen's equality condition is that all ratios with positive weight are equal, necessarily to a/b.

If a=0, both sides of the inequality are zero. If b>0 but some a_i>0 has b_i=0, the left side is +\infty and the right side is finite. If b=0<a, both sides are +\infty. These are exactly the boundary conventions in the statement.

Level justification

Handle the zero and infinite boundary cases, differentiate x log x twice, and apply strict Jensen with its equality condition. Undergraduate calculus and finite weighted inequalities suffice.
