Statement

A subgroup N of a group G is normal if and only if it is the kernel of some group homomorphism from G.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Introductory group theory supplies normal subgroups, cosets, kernels, actions and automorphisms. Complement, normalizer, centralizer or fixed-point sets are explicitly defined by these operations, so no representation or extension-field theory is needed.

Proof 1: Construct the natural quotient projection

For any subgroup N of a group G, form the set G/N of all left cosets of N in G. The natural way to turn this into a group is to define an operation on left cosets by

uN\cdot vN=(uv)N.

This operation is well defined if and only if the subgroup N is closed under conjugation under G: if g\in G and n\in N, then gng^{-1}\in N. Furthermore, the operation being well defined is sufficient for the quotient to be a group. Subgroups satisfying this property are called normal subgroups.

Every kernel of a group homomorphism is a normal subgroup, and for a given normal subgroup N of a group G, the natural projection

\pi:G\to G/N,\qquad \pi(g)=gN,

is a homomorphism with \ker\pi=N, so the normal subgroups are precisely the subgroups which are kernels.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Construct the quotient homomorphism and use conjugation invariance for the converse. Standard undergraduate group theory suffices.
