Statement

For an alphabet of q\ge2 symbols, let Z_1,Z_2,\ldots be independent uniform symbols. Every specified finite nonempty word w almost surely occurs as a consecutive substring somewhere in this sequence. “Almost surely” means with probability one.

Proof 1: Let the independent block-failure probability tend to zero

Recall that if two events are statistically independent, then the probability of both happening equals the product of the first and second events' probabilities.

Let us consider the probability of typing the word w, of length m, on a typewriter with q keys. The keys are pressed independently and uniformly at random, meaning that each key has an equal chance of being pressed regardless of what keys had been pressed previously. The chance that the first letter typed is the first letter of w is 1/q, and the chance that the second letter typed is the second letter of w is also 1/q, and so on. Therefore, the probability of the first m letters spelling w is

\underbrace{(1/q)\times\cdots\times(1/q)}_{m\text{ factors}}=q^{-m}.

The result may be very small, but is not zero.

From the above, the chance of not typing w in a given block of m letters is 1-q^{-m}. Because each nonoverlapping block is typed independently, the chance X_n of not typing w in any of the first n blocks of m letters is

X_n=(1-q^{-m})^n.

As n grows, X_n gets smaller. As n approaches infinity, the probability X_n approaches zero; that is, by making n large enough, X_n can be made as small as is desired, and the chance of typing w approaches 100\%. Thus, the probability of the word w appearing at some point in an infinite sequence of keystrokes is equal to one.

Proof 2: Use almost-everywhere normality of base-q expansions — outline

Identify the alphabet symbols with the base-q digits 0,\ldots,q-1. The infinitely long string thus produced corresponds to the base-q digits of a particular real number between 0 and 1, with the uniform distribution.

The “largest” subset, in the sense of full Lebesgue measure, of these real numbers is that which not only contains the given word, but also contains every other possible finite string, and with equal distribution of strings of the same length. These numbers are called normal in base q. Here normality means that each word of length m has limiting frequency q^{-m}.

Because almost all numbers are normal, almost all possible strings contain all possible finite substrings. Hence, the probability of typing a normal number is 1. The same principles apply regardless of the number of keys from which the monkey can choose; a 90-key keyboard can be seen as a generator of numbers written in base 90.
