Statement

Consider a class \mathcal D of divisor apportionment rules. Such rules multiply the agents’ entitlements by a common factor and round according to fixed thresholds d(k)\in[k,k+1] between counts k and k+1. Jefferson’s rule uses d(k)=k+1 (rounding down), and Adams’s uses d(k)=k (rounding up); denote these distinct rules by J and A. For an allocation of h seats among agents with positive weights t_i, put q_i=ht_i/\sum_jt_j. Lower quota means a_i\geq\lfloor q_i\rfloor for every agent and input, and upper quota means a_i\leq\lceil q_i\rceil for every agent and input. Assume the classification facts that J is the only member of \mathcal D satisfying lower quota and A is the only member satisfying upper quota. Then no member of \mathcal D satisfies both requirements on all inputs.

Level
High school only
Level justification

The quotas, floor/ceiling bounds, divisor thresholds and uniqueness assumptions are explicit arithmetic definitions. Understanding their incompatibility requires elementary reasoning about functions and inequalities.

Proof 1: Compare the two unique classified rules

If a rule satisfied both requirements, lower-quota uniqueness would make it J and upper-quota uniqueness would make it A. This would give J=A, contrary to their assumed distinctness.

Level
High school only
Level justification

The assumed two uniqueness classifications would force one rule to equal two distinct rules. All quota and rounding definitions use elementary real arithmetic.
