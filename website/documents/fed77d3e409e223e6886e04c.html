Formalizes how evidence changes the relative probabilities of competing hypotheses by combining prior probabilities with likelihoods. It underlies Bayesian accounts of confirmation and learning, while leaving the choice of priors, hypotheses and statistical model to additional judgments.

Statement

Let (A_i)_{i\in I} be a finite or countable partition into events with P(A_i)>0, and let P(B)>0. For events E,F with P(F)>0, conditional probability means P(E\mid F)=P(E\cap F)/P(F). Then, for each i\in I,

P(A_i\mid B)=\frac{P(A_i)P(B\mid A_i)}{\sum_{j\in I}P(A_j)P(B\mid A_j)}.
Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Conditional probabilities or conditional densities are explicitly defined as ratios. Calculus supplies densities and sums or integrals, and elementary set operations describe the events. These are small extensions sufficient for understanding the Bayes identity, without general conditional expectation.

Proof 1: Substitute the total-probability denominator

Bayes' theorem may be derived from the relation between joint and conditional probabilities. The joint probability of the events {\displaystyle A} and {\displaystyle B} both happening, written {\displaystyle P(A\cap B)}, is equal to the conditional probability of {\displaystyle A} given {\displaystyle B} times the probability of {\displaystyle B}:

{\displaystyle P(A\cap B)=P(A\vert B)P(B).}

Likewise,

{\displaystyle P(A\cap B)=P(B\vert A)P(A).}

The two products must therefore be equal to each other:

{\displaystyle P(A\vert B)P(B)=P(B\vert A)P(A),}

and dividing both sides by {\displaystyle P(B)} gives Bayes' theorem:

{\displaystyle P(A\vert B)={\frac {P(B\vert A)P(A)}{P(B)}},{\text{ if }}P(B)\neq 0.}

If events A1, A2, ..., are mutually exclusive and exhaustive, i.e., one of them is certain to occur but no two can occur together, then, by the law of total probability,

{\displaystyle P(B)=\sum _{i}P(B|A_{i})P(A_{i})}

for any event B. Substituting this expression for {\displaystyle P(B)} into the denominator of the Bayes formula just derived gives:

{\displaystyle P(A_{i}|B)={\frac {P(A_{i})P(B|A_{i})}{\sum _{j}P(A_{j})P(B|A_{j})}}.}
Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

The Bayes algebra is elementary, but the full statement permits a countably infinite partition and a convergent sum of event probabilities. Undergraduate sequences and series together with countable additivity suffice; this is a small extension of finite probability, not general conditional-expectation theory. The total-probability denominator equals the positive number P(B).
