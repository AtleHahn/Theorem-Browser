Statement

For a positive integer n, let \varphi(n) count the integers 1\le k\le n with \gcd(k,n)=1. For every positive integer n, \varphi(n)=n\prod_{p\mid n}(1-1/p), where the product is over distinct prime divisors. If n=\prod_{i=1}^r p_i^{k_i} is its prime factorization, the equivalent formula is \varphi(n)=\prod_{i=1}^r p_i^{k_i-1}(p_i-1). Empty products equal 1.

High school only

Proof 1: Combine local multiplicativity and prime-power counts — outline

Local multiplicativity calculation

If \gcd(m,n)=1 and m,n>1, let A,B,C be the sets of positive integers which are coprime to and less than m,n,mn, respectively, so that |A|=\varphi(m), etc. Then there is a bijection between A\times B and C by the Chinese remainder theorem. Thus \varphi(m)\varphi(n)=\varphi(mn). If one modulus is 1, use \varphi(1)=1: the sole allowed integer is 1, with \gcd(1,1)=1.

Local prime-power count

For prime p and integer k\ge1,

\varphi(p^k)=p^k-p^{k-1}=p^{k-1}(p-1)=p^k(1-1/p).

For 1\le m\le p^k, since p is a prime number, the only possible values of {\displaystyle \gcd(p^{k},m)} are {\displaystyle 1,p,p^{2},\dots ,p^{k}}, and the only way to have {\displaystyle \gcd(p^{k},m)>1} is if m is a multiple of p, that is, {\displaystyle m\in \{p,2p,3p,\ldots ,p^{k-1}p=p^{k}\}}, and there are {\displaystyle p^{k-1}} such multiples not greater than {\displaystyle p^{k}}. Therefore, the other {\displaystyle p^{k}-p^{k-1}} numbers are all relatively prime to {\displaystyle p^{k}}.

Assemble the prime factors

The fundamental theorem of arithmetic states that if n > 1 there is a unique expression {\displaystyle n=p_{1}^{k_{1}}p_{2}^{k_{2}}\cdots p_{r}^{k_{r}},} where p1 < p2 < ... < pr are prime numbers and each ki ≥ 1. (The case n = 1 corresponds to the empty product.) Repeatedly using the multiplicative property of φ and the formula for φ(pk) gives

{\displaystyle {\begin{array}{rcl}\varphi (n)&=&\varphi (p_{1}^{k_{1}})\,\varphi (p_{2}^{k_{2}})\cdots \varphi (p_{r}^{k_{r}})\\[.1em]&=&p_{1}^{k_{1}}\left(1-{\frac {1}{p_{1}}}\right)p_{2}^{k_{2}}\left(1-{\frac {1}{p_{2}}}\right)\cdots p_{r}^{k_{r}}\left(1-{\frac {1}{p_{r}}}\right)\\[.1em]&=&p_{1}^{k_{1}}p_{2}^{k_{2}}\cdots p_{r}^{k_{r}}\left(1-{\frac {1}{p_{1}}}\right)\left(1-{\frac {1}{p_{2}}}\right)\cdots \left(1-{\frac {1}{p_{r}}}\right)\\[.1em]&=&n\left(1-{\frac {1}{p_{1}}}\right)\left(1-{\frac {1}{p_{2}}}\right)\cdots \left(1-{\frac {1}{p_{r}}}\right).\end{array}}}

This gives both versions of Euler's product formula.

Proof 2: Exclude the sets divisible by each prime factor — outline

Apply inclusion–exclusion to \{1,2,\ldots,n\}, excluding the sets of integers divisible by the distinct prime divisors of n. This gives \varphi(n)=n\prod_{p\mid n}(1-1/p).
