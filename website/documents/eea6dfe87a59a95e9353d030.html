Proof 1: Split the difference quotient

Let h(x) = f(x)g(x) and suppose that f and g are each differentiable at x. We want to prove that h is differentiable at x and that its derivative, h′(x), is given by f′(x)g(x) + f(x)g′(x). To do this, {\displaystyle f(x)g(x+\Delta x)-f(x)g(x+\Delta x)} (which is zero, and thus does not change the value) is added to the numerator to permit its factoring, and then properties of limits are used.

{\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}h'(x)&=\lim _{\Delta x\to 0}{\frac {h(x+\Delta x)-h(x)}{\Delta x}}\\[5pt]&=\lim _{\Delta x\to 0}{\frac {f(x+\Delta x)g(x+\Delta x)-f(x)g(x)}{\Delta x}}\\[5pt]&=\lim _{\Delta x\to 0}{\frac {f(x+\Delta x)g(x+\Delta x)-f(x)g(x+\Delta x)+f(x)g(x+\Delta x)-f(x)g(x)}{\Delta x}}\\[5pt]&=\lim _{\Delta x\to 0}{\frac {{\big [}f(x+\Delta x)-f(x){\big ]}\cdot g(x+\Delta x)+f(x)\cdot {\big [}g(x+\Delta x)-g(x){\big ]}}{\Delta x}}\\[5pt]&=\lim _{\Delta x\to 0}{\frac {f(x+\Delta x)-f(x)}{\Delta x}}\cdot \lim _{\Delta x\to 0}g(x+\Delta x)+\lim _{\Delta x\to 0}f(x)\cdot \lim _{\Delta x\to 0}{\frac {g(x+\Delta x)-g(x)}{\Delta x}}\\[5pt]&=f'(x)g(x)+f(x)g'(x).\end{aligned}}}

The fact that {\displaystyle \lim _{\Delta x\to 0}g(x+\Delta x)=g(x)} follows from the fact that differentiable functions are continuous.

Proof 2: Multiply the linear approximations

By definition, if {\displaystyle f,g:\mathbb {R} \to \mathbb {R} } are differentiable at {\displaystyle x}, then we can write linear approximations:

{\displaystyle f(x+h)=f(x)+f'(x)h+\varepsilon _{1}(h)}

and

{\displaystyle g(x+h)=g(x)+g'(x)h+\varepsilon _{2}(h),}

where the error terms are small with respect to h: that is, {\textstyle \lim _{h\to 0}{\frac {\varepsilon _{1}(h)}{h}}=\lim _{h\to 0}{\frac {\varepsilon _{2}(h)}{h}}=0,} also written {\displaystyle \varepsilon _{1},\varepsilon _{2}=o(h)}. Then:

{\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}f(x+h)g(x+h)-f(x)g(x)&=(f(x)+f'(x)h+\varepsilon _{1}(h))(g(x)+g'(x)h+\varepsilon _{2}(h))-f(x)g(x)\\[.5em]&=f(x)g(x)+f'(x)g(x)h+f(x)g'(x)h-f(x)g(x)+{\text{error terms}}\\[.5em]&=f'(x)g(x)h+f(x)g'(x)h+o(h).\end{aligned}}}

The "error terms" consist of items such as {\displaystyle f(x)\varepsilon _{2}(h),f'(x)g'(x)h^{2}} and {\displaystyle hf'(x)\varepsilon _{2}(h)} which are easily seen to have magnitude {\displaystyle o(h).} Dividing by {\displaystyle h} and taking the limit {\displaystyle h\to 0} gives the result.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Multiply the two first-order approximations and collect terms whose ratio to h vanishes. The remainder estimate is ordinary one-variable differentiability.

Proof 3: Quarter-square identity and chain rule

Write u=f and v=g.

This proof uses the chain rule and the quarter square function {\displaystyle q(x)={\tfrac {1}{4}}x^{2}} with derivative {\displaystyle q'(x)={\tfrac {1}{2}}x}. We have:

{\displaystyle uv=q(u+v)-q(u-v),}

and differentiating both sides gives:

{\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}(uv)'&=q'(u+v)(u'+v')-q'(u-v)(u'-v')\\[4pt]&=\left({\tfrac {1}{2}}(u+v)(u'+v')\right)-\left({\tfrac {1}{2}}(u-v)(u'-v')\right)\\[4pt]&={\tfrac {1}{2}}(uu'+vu'+uv'+vv')-{\tfrac {1}{2}}(uu'-vu'-uv'+vv')\\[4pt]&=vu'+uv'.\end{aligned}}}

Level justification

Proof 4: Apply the multivariable chain rule

The product rule can be considered a special case of the chain rule for several variables, applied to the multiplication function {\displaystyle m(u,v)=uv}:

{\displaystyle {d(uv) \over dx}={\frac {\partial (uv)}{\partial u}}{\frac {du}{dx}}+{\frac {\partial (uv)}{\partial v}}{\frac {dv}{dx}}=v{\frac {du}{dx}}+u{\frac {dv}{dx}}.}
