Statement

Write R(n_1,\ldots,n_c) for the least positive integer N such that every assignment of c colors to all unordered pairs of an N-element vertex set contains, for some i, a subset of size n_i whose pairs all have color i. A one-vertex subset is monochromatic in every color. R(n_1,\ldots,n_c) exists for every positive integer c and every list of positive integer targets.

Level
High school only
Level justification

Finite graph terminology is defined by pairs of vertices; the statements require only finite counting and elementary logic.

Proof 1: Combine two-color induction with repeated color merging — outline

Write K_j for the complete graph on j vertices. The cases with a target of one follow from the singleton convention.

The theorem for the 2-colour case can be proved by induction on r + s. It is clear from the definition that for all n, R(n, 2) = R(2, n) = n. This starts the induction. We prove that R(r, s) exists by finding an explicit bound for it. By the inductive hypothesis R(r − 1, s) and R(r, s − 1) exist.

{\displaystyle R(r,s)\leq R(r-1,s)+R(r,s-1).}

Consider a complete graph on R(r − 1, s) + R(r, s − 1) vertices whose edges are coloured with two colours. Pick a vertex v from the graph, and partition the remaining vertices into two sets M and N, such that for every vertex w, w is in M if edge (vw) is blue, and w is in N if (vw) is red. Because the graph has {\displaystyle R(r-1,s)+R(r,s-1)=|M|+|N|+1} vertices, it follows that either {\displaystyle |M|\geq R(r-1,s)} or {\displaystyle |N|\geq R(r,s-1).} In the former case, if M has a red Ks then so does the original graph and we are finished. Otherwise M has a blue Kr − 1 and so {\displaystyle M\cup \{v\}} has a blue Kr by the definition of M. The latter case is analogous. Thus the claim is true and we have completed the proof for 2 colours.

For one colour, the target number itself suffices. For c>2, the induction on the number of colours proceeds as follows.

Consider a complete graph of {\displaystyle R(n_{1},\dots ,n_{c-2},R(n_{c-1},n_{c}))} vertices and colour its edges with c colours. Now 'go colour-blind' and pretend that c − 1 and c are the same colour. Thus the graph is now (c − 1)-coloured. Due to the definition of {\displaystyle R(n_{1},\dots ,n_{c-2},R(n_{c-1},n_{c})),} such a graph contains either a Kni mono-chromatically coloured with colour i for some 1 ≤ i ≤ c − 2 or a KR(nc − 1, nc)-coloured in the 'blurred colour'. In the former case we are finished. In the latter case, we recover our sight again and see from the definition of R(nc − 1, nc) we must have either a (c − 1)-monochrome Knc − 1 or a c-monochrome Knc. In either case the proof is complete.

The two-colour recurrence implies that any R(r,s) is finite. The right hand side of the inequality obtained by merging the last two colours expresses a Ramsey number for c colours in terms of Ramsey numbers for fewer colours. Therefore, any R(n1, …, nc) is finite for any number of colours. This proves the theorem.
