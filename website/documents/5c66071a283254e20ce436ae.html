Statement

Fix a nondegenerate spherical triangle ABC on the unit sphere with area \varepsilon and its Lexell circle l through A^*,B^*,C. As the apex approaches B^* along the same arc of l, the limiting lune tangent to l has equal angles \theta=\varepsilon/2. The notation A^*=-A, B^*=-B denotes antipodal points, and all triangle sides are minor great-circle arcs.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Three-dimensional geometry and calculus supply spheres, planes, angles and surface area. Great circles are central plane sections and small circles are other plane sections; a lune is the region between two semicircles. The explicit antipodes and arcs specify the configurations without abstract differential geometry.

Proof 1: Degenerate the triangle to a lune — outline

As the apex {\displaystyle C} approaches either of the points antipodal to the base vertices – say {\displaystyle B^{*~\!\!}} – along Lexell's circle {\displaystyle l,} in the limit the triangle degenerates to a lune tangent to {\displaystyle l} at {\displaystyle B^{*~\!\!}} and tangent to the antipodal small circle {\displaystyle l^{*~\!\!}} at {\displaystyle B,} and having the same excess {\displaystyle \varepsilon } as any of the triangles with apex on the same arc of {\displaystyle l.} As a degenerate triangle, it has a straight angle at {\displaystyle A} (i.e. {\displaystyle \angle A=\pi ,} a half turn) and equal angles {\displaystyle B=B^{*~\!\!}={\tfrac {1}{2}}\varepsilon .}

Here l^* is the antipodal image of l. Area constancy is the equal-area conclusion of Lexell's theorem; continuity of area in this degeneration is used. Girard's formula gives

\varepsilon=\pi+2\theta-\pi=2\theta,

so \theta=\varepsilon/2.

Level
Beyond undergraduate level
Level justification

Degenerate the spherical triangle along its Lexell arc and use continuity of area and Girard to read the lune angle. The tangency and degeneration geometry is not established in enough elementary detail for a confident undergraduate rating.
