Statement

Let \theta range over an open real interval and let X have a density f(x;\theta) on a parameter-independent support S\subseteq\mathbb R. Suppose the score V=\partial_\theta\log f(X;\theta) exists almost surely and I(\theta)=E_\theta[V^2] is finite and positive. Let T=t(X) have finite variance and differentiable mean \psi(\theta)=E_\theta T. Assume differentiation and integration commute for both normalization and the estimator mean: \partial_\theta\int_S f(x;\theta)\,dx=\int_S\partial_\theta f(x;\theta)\,dx,\qquad \partial_\theta\int_S t(x)f(x;\theta)\,dx=\int_S t(x)\partial_\theta f(x;\theta)\,dx. Then E_\theta V=0 and \operatorname{Cov}_\theta(V,T)=\psi^{\prime}(\theta).

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Score and information are explicitly derivatives and expected squares of a density; expectation/covariance add linked scalar integral definitions. Both required differentiation interchanges are assumptions.

Proof 1: Differentiate normalization and the estimator mean

Let {\displaystyle X} be a random variable with probability density function {\displaystyle f(x;\theta )}. Here {\displaystyle T=t(X)} is a statistic, which is used as an estimator for {\displaystyle \psi (\theta )}. Define {\displaystyle V} as the score:

{\displaystyle V={\frac {\partial }{\partial \theta }}\ln f(X;\theta )={\frac {1}{f(X;\theta )}}{\frac {\partial }{\partial \theta }}f(X;\theta )}

where the chain rule is used in the final equality above. Then the expectation of {\displaystyle V}, written {\displaystyle \operatorname {E} (V)}, is zero. This is because:

{\displaystyle \operatorname {E} (V)=\int_S f(x;\theta )\left[{\frac {1}{f(x;\theta )}}{\frac {\partial }{\partial \theta }}f(x;\theta )\right]\,dx={\frac {\partial }{\partial \theta }}\int_S f(x;\theta )\,dx=0}

where the integral and partial derivative have been interchanged (justified by the normalization interchange in the statement).

If we consider the covariance {\displaystyle \operatorname {cov} (V,T)} of {\displaystyle V} and {\displaystyle T}, we have {\displaystyle \operatorname {cov} (V,T)=\operatorname {E} (VT)}, because {\displaystyle \operatorname {E} (V)=0}. Expanding this expression we have

{\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}\operatorname {cov} (V,T)&=\operatorname {E} \left(T\cdot \left[{\frac {1}{f(X;\theta )}}{\frac {\partial }{\partial \theta }}f(X;\theta )\right]\right)\\[6pt]&=\int_S t(x)\left[{\frac {1}{f(x;\theta )}}{\frac {\partial }{\partial \theta }}f(x;\theta )\right]f(x;\theta )\,dx\\[6pt]&={\frac {\partial }{\partial \theta }}\left[\int_S t(x)f(x;\theta )\,dx\right]={\frac {\partial }{\partial \theta }}E(T)=\psi ^{\prime }(\theta )\end{aligned}}}

again because the integration and differentiation operations commute (the estimator-mean interchange in the statement).
