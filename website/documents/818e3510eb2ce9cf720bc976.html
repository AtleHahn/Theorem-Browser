Statement

In the Poincaré upper-half-plane model, let a<b<c<d be finite boundary points and let p,q be the geodesic semicircles with diameters [a,b] and [c,d]. There is a unique hyperbolic geodesic perpendicular to both p and q.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

The hyperbolic model’s geodesics, angle interpretation and isometries are supplied concretely. These add a limited geometric model to undergraduate analytic geometry.

Proof 1: Normalize the boundary points and compute the radius — outline

Use the standard half-plane model facts: geodesics are vertical lines or semicircles orthogonal to the real boundary, hyperbolic angles are Euclidean angles, and real translations, circle inversion and real boundary scalings extend to hyperbolic isometries.

Let

{\displaystyle a<b<c<d}

be four distinct points on the abscissa of the Cartesian plane. Let {\displaystyle p} and {\displaystyle q} be semicircles above the abscissa with diameters {\displaystyle ab} and {\displaystyle cd} respectively. Then in the Poincaré half-plane model HP, {\displaystyle p} and {\displaystyle q} represent ultraparallel lines.

Compose the following two hyperbolic motions:

{\displaystyle x\to x-a}{\displaystyle {\mbox{inversion in the unit semicircle.}}}

Then {\displaystyle a\to \infty ,\quad b\to (b-a)^{-1},\quad c\to (c-a)^{-1},\quad d\to (d-a)^{-1}.}

Now continue with these two hyperbolic motions:

{\displaystyle x\to x-(b-a)^{-1}}{\displaystyle x\to \left[(c-a)^{-1}-(b-a)^{-1}\right]^{-1}x}

These formulas describe boundary coordinates; a negative real scaling is extended by reflecting the horizontal coordinate and scaling heights by its absolute value.

Then {\displaystyle a} stays at {\displaystyle \infty }, {\displaystyle b\to 0}, {\displaystyle c\to 1}, {\displaystyle d\to z} (say). Here z=((d-a)^{-1}-(b-a)^{-1})/((c-a)^{-1}-(b-a)^{-1})>1. The transformed p is the vertical line through 0, so a geodesic perpendicular to it must be a semicircle centered at 0. The unique semicircle, with center at the origin, perpendicular to the one on {\displaystyle 1z} must have a radius perpendicular to the radius of the other at their intersection. The right triangle formed by the abscissa and the perpendicular radii has hypotenuse of length {\displaystyle {\begin{matrix}{\frac {1}{2}}\end{matrix}}(z+1)}. Since {\displaystyle {\begin{matrix}{\frac {1}{2}}\end{matrix}}(z-1)} is the radius of the semicircle on {\displaystyle 1z}, the common perpendicular sought has radius-square

{\displaystyle {\frac {1}{4}}\left[(z+1)^{2}-(z-1)^{2}\right]=z.}

For z>1, the circles with these centers and radii intersect in the upper half-plane and satisfy the orthogonality relation.

The four hyperbolic motions that produced {\displaystyle z} above can each be inverted and applied in reverse order to the semicircle centered at the origin and of radius {\displaystyle {\sqrt {z}}} to yield the unique hyperbolic line perpendicular to both ultraparallels {\displaystyle p} and {\displaystyle q}.
