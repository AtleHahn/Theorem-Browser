Likely alternative prerequisite level: High school only; lowest recorded proof level: Undergraduate level. Use the elementary straight-segment/random-subset incidence proof, then write the resulting bound with explicit constants. For sufficiently large k, absorb the linear m term in km; at least one remaining term bounds a fixed fraction of km, giving m<=C n^2/k^3 or m<=C n/k. For bounded k use the finite pair count, and m=0 is immediate. Thus ordinary finite counting and algebra cover every k>=2 without big-O prerequisites or an advanced incidence theorem.

Statement

There is an absolute constant C such that, for any n distinct planar points and integer k\ge2, at most C(n^2/k^3+n/k) lines contain at least k of those points.

Level
High school only
Level justification

Incidence means that a point lies on a line; the inequalities use finite counts and real arithmetic only.

Mathematical Field
Convex and discrete geometry
Required topics
Finite point-line incidence countingElementary real powers and uniform constants
Review notes
none

Proof 1: Incidence bound and dominant-term alternatives

Since every pair of points can be connected by at most one line, there can be at most n(n − 1)/2 lines which can connect at k or more points, since k ≥ 2. This bound will prove the theorem when k is small (e.g. if k ≤ C for some absolute constant C). Thus, we need only consider the case when k is large, say k ≥ C.

The case m=0 is immediate. Suppose that there are m>0 lines that each contain at least k points. These lines generate at least mk incidences, and so by the Szemerédi–Trotter incidence bound, we have

{\displaystyle mk=O\left(n^{2/3}m^{2/3}+n+m\right),}

and so at least one of the statements {\displaystyle mk=O(n^{2/3}m^{2/3}),mk=O(n)}, or {\displaystyle mk=O(m)} is true. The third possibility is ruled out since k was assumed to be large, so we are left with the first two. But in either of these two cases, some elementary algebra will give the bound {\displaystyle m=O(n^{2}/k^{3}+n/k)} as desired.
