Statement

There are identities built from variables, the constant 1, addition, multiplication and exponentiation that hold for all positive integer substitutions but are not derivable in equational logic from the following eleven identities:

\begin{gathered} x+y=y+x,\qquad (x+y)+z=x+(y+z),\\ x\cdot1=x,\qquad xy=yx,\qquad (xy)z=x(yz),\qquad x(y+z)=xy+xz,\\ 1^x=1,\qquad x^1=x,\qquad x^{y+z}=x^yx^z,\qquad (xy)^z=x^zy^z,\qquad (x^y)^z=x^{yz}. \end{gathered}

In particular, Wilkie's identity

\begin{aligned} &\big((1+x)^y+(1+x+x^2)^y\big)^x\big((1+x^3)^x+(1+x^2+x^4)^x\big)^y\\ ={}&\big((1+x)^x+(1+x+x^2)^x\big)^y\big((1+x^3)^y+(1+x^2+x^4)^y\big)^x \end{aligned}

is true for all positive integers x,y, but cannot be derived from those eleven identities.

Equational derivation permits substitution of terms for variables, replacement of equals within a larger term, and reflexivity, symmetry and transitivity of equality. Numerals abbreviate sums of 1; subtraction and extra arithmetic axioms are not among the allowed rules.

Proof availability

Source verification and nonderivability limitation

Put A=1+x, B=1+x+x^2 and Q=1-x+x^2. The factorizations 1+x^3=AQ and 1+x^2+x^4=BQ make both sides equal to

Q^{xy}(A^y+B^y)^x(A^x+B^x)^y.

This is the source's ordinary-arithmetic verification of truth.

For nonderivability, the article gives an intuition based on the absence of subtraction from the allowed language. That does not exclude every possible equational derivation by another route. The source attributes the nonderivability theorem to Wilkie but does not supply its proof or a countermodel. The truth calculation is therefore only a partial proof of the stated result.
