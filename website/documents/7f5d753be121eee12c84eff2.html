Statement

Let (M,g) be a smooth Riemannian surface homeomorphic to \mathbb{RP}^2. Let \operatorname{sys}(M,g) be the infimum of lengths of closed curves not contractible in M. Then \operatorname{Area}(M,g)\ge (2/\pi)\operatorname{sys}(M,g)^2, with equality precisely for a round projective-plane metric. A round projective-plane metric means the metric induced by a round sphere of some positive radius under antipodal identification.

The statement requires differential geometry and noncontractible loops.

Proof 1: Average the conformal factor over sphere rotations — outline

Put L=\operatorname{sys}(M,g). The proof relies on the uniformization theorem and employs an averaging argument, as follows.

By uniformization, the Riemannian surface {\displaystyle (M,g)} is conformally diffeomorphic to a round projective plane. This means that we may assume that the surface {\displaystyle M} is obtained from the Euclidean unit sphere {\displaystyle S^{2}} by identifying antipodal points, and the Riemannian length element at each point {\displaystyle x} is

{\displaystyle \mathrm {dLength} =f(x)\mathrm {dLength} _{\text{Euclidean}},}

where {\displaystyle \mathrm {dLength} _{\text{Euclidean}}} is the Euclidean length element and the function {\displaystyle f:S^{2}\to (0,+\infty )}, called the conformal factor, satisfies {\displaystyle f(-x)=f(x)}.

More precisely, the universal cover of {\displaystyle M} is {\displaystyle S^{2}}, a loop {\displaystyle \gamma \subseteq M} is noncontractible if and only if its lift {\displaystyle {\widetilde {\gamma }}\subseteq S^{2}} goes from one point to its opposite, and the length of each curve {\displaystyle \gamma } is

{\displaystyle \operatorname {Length} (\gamma )=\int _{\widetilde {\gamma }}f\,\mathrm {dLength} _{\text{Euclidean}}.}

Subject to the restriction that each of these lengths is at least {\displaystyle L}, we want to find an {\displaystyle f} that minimizes the

{\displaystyle \operatorname {Area} (M,g)=\int _{S_{+}^{2}}f(x)^{2}\,\mathrm {dArea} _{\text{Euclidean}}(x),}

where {\displaystyle S_{+}^{2}} is the upper half of the sphere.

A key observation is that if we average several different {\displaystyle f_{i}} that satisfy the length restriction and have the same area {\displaystyle A}, then we obtain a better conformal factor {\displaystyle f_{\text{new}}={\frac {1}{n}}\sum _{0\leq i<n}f_{i}}, that also satisfies the length restriction and has

{\displaystyle \operatorname {Area} (M,g_{\text{new}})=\int _{S_{+}^{2}}\left({\frac {1}{n}}\sum _{i}f_{i}(x)\right)^{2}\mathrm {dArea} _{\text{Euclidean}}(x)}{\displaystyle \qquad \qquad \leq {\frac {1}{n}}\sum _{i}\left(\int _{S_{+}^{2}}f_{i}(x)^{2}\mathrm {dArea} _{\text{Euclidean}}(x)\right)=A,}

and the inequality is strict unless the functions {\displaystyle f_{i}} are equal.

A way to improve any non-constant {\displaystyle f} is to obtain the different functions {\displaystyle f_{i}} from {\displaystyle f} using rotations of the sphere {\displaystyle R_{i}\in SO(3)}, defining {\displaystyle f_{i}(x)=f(R_{i}(x))}. If we average over all possible rotations with respect to invariant probability measure on SO(3), then we get an {\displaystyle f_{\text{new}}} that is constant over all the sphere. We can further reduce this constant to minimum value {\displaystyle r={\frac {L}{\pi }}} allowed by the length restriction. Then we obtain, up to isometry, the unique metric that attains the minimum area {\displaystyle 2\pi r^{2}={\frac {2}{\pi }}L^{2}}.
