Statement

Let ABC be a right triangle with right angle at C, and let D be the foot of its altitude on AB. Set p=AD, q=DB and h=CD, all positive. Then h^2=pq, equivalently h=\sqrt{pq}.

Proof 1: Similarity of the altitude triangles

The triangles ADC, CDB are similar, since:

  • Consider triangles ABC, ACD; here we have

    \angle ACB=\angle ADC=90^\circ,\qquad \angle BAC=\angle CAD;

    therefore by the AA postulate

    \triangle ABC\sim\triangle ACD.

  • Further, consider triangles ABC, CBD; here we have

    \angle ACB=\angle BDC=90^\circ,\qquad \angle ABC=\angle CBD;

    therefore by the AA postulate

    \triangle ABC\sim\triangle CBD.

Therefore, both triangles ACD, CBD are similar to ABC and to one another, i.e.

\triangle ACD\sim\triangle ABC\sim\triangle CBD.

Because of the similarity we get the following equality of ratios and its algebraic rearrangement yields the theorem:

\frac hp=\frac qh\iff h^2=pq\iff h=\sqrt{pq}\qquad(h,p,q>0).

Proof 2: Combine three Pythagorean identities

Put a=BC, b=AC and c=AB=p+q. In the setting of the geometric mean theorem there are three right triangles ABC, ADC and DBC in which the Pythagorean theorem yields:

\begin{aligned} h^2&=a^2-q^2,\\ h^2&=b^2-p^2,\\ c^2&=a^2+b^2. \end{aligned}

Adding the first two equations and then using the third leads to:

\begin{aligned} 2h^2&=a^2+b^2-p^2-q^2\\ &=c^2-p^2-q^2\\ &=(p+q)^2-p^2-q^2\\ &=2pq,\\ \therefore\quad h^2&=pq, \end{aligned}

which finally yields the formula of the geometric mean theorem.

Statement

Let ABC be a right triangle with right angle at C, and let D be the foot of its altitude on AB. Set p=AD, q=DB and h=CD, all positive. Then h^2=pq, equivalently h=\sqrt{pq}.

Proof 1 — Similarity of the altitude triangles

The triangles ADC, CDB are similar, since:

  • Consider triangles ABC, ACD; here we have

    \angle ACB=\angle ADC=90^\circ,\qquad \angle BAC=\angle CAD;

    therefore by the AA postulate

    \triangle ABC\sim\triangle ACD.

  • Further, consider triangles ABC, CBD; here we have

    \angle ACB=\angle BDC=90^\circ,\qquad \angle ABC=\angle CBD;

    therefore by the AA postulate

    \triangle ABC\sim\triangle CBD.

Therefore, both triangles ACD, CBD are similar to ABC and to one another, i.e.

\triangle ACD\sim\triangle ABC\sim\triangle CBD.

Because of the similarity we get the following equality of ratios and its algebraic rearrangement yields the theorem:

\frac hp=\frac qh\iff h^2=pq\iff h=\sqrt{pq}\qquad(h,p,q>0).

Proof 2 — Combine three Pythagorean identities

Put a=BC, b=AC and c=AB=p+q. In the setting of the geometric mean theorem there are three right triangles ABC, ADC and DBC in which the Pythagorean theorem yields:

\begin{aligned} h^2&=a^2-q^2,\\ h^2&=b^2-p^2,\\ c^2&=a^2+b^2. \end{aligned}

Adding the first two equations and then using the third leads to:

\begin{aligned} 2h^2&=a^2+b^2-p^2-q^2\\ &=c^2-p^2-q^2\\ &=(p+q)^2-p^2-q^2\\ &=2pq,\\ \therefore\quad h^2&=pq, \end{aligned}

which finally yields the formula of the geometric mean theorem.

Proof 3 — Apply intersecting chords in the circumcircle (outline)

By the converse of Thales’ theorem, AB is a diameter of the circumcircle of ABC. Extend the altitude line to meet that circle again at E. Since the diameter through the center is perpendicular to chord CE at D, it bisects the chord, so DE=CD=h. The source’s intersecting-chords relation is CD\,DE=AD\,DB. Substitution gives h^2=pq.
