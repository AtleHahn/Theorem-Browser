Statement

Let x_0<x_1 and a<b be real. Suppose f is real-valued and f,f_x are continuous on [x_0,x_1]\times[a,b], with f_x the partial derivative in the first variable. For x_0<x<x_1, U(x)=\int_a^b f(x,t)\,dt is differentiable and U'(x)=\int_a^b f_x(x,t)\,dt.

Proof 1: Interchange continuous iterated integrals and apply the fundamental theorem

We first prove the case of constant limits of integration a and b.

We use Fubini's theorem to change the order of integration. For every x and h, such that h is nonzero and both x and x +h are within [x0,x1], we have (using oriented integrals for h<0):

{\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}\int _{x}^{x+h}\int _{a}^{b}f_{x}(s,t)\,dt\,ds&=\int _{a}^{b}\int _{x}^{x+h}f_{x}(s,t)\,ds\,dt\\[2ex]&=\int _{a}^{b}\left(f(x+h,t)-f(x,t)\right)\,dt\\[2ex]&=\int _{a}^{b}f(x+h,t)\,dt-\int _{a}^{b}f(x,t)\,dt\end{aligned}}}

Note that the integrals at hand are well defined since {\displaystyle f_{x}(x,t)} is continuous at the closed rectangle {\displaystyle [x_{0},x_{1}]\times [a,b]} and thus also uniformly continuous there; thus its integrals by either dt or dx are continuous in the other variable and also integrable by it (essentially this is because for uniformly continuous functions, one may pass the limit through the integration sign, as elaborated below).

Therefore:

{\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}{\frac {\int _{a}^{b}f(x+h,t)\,dt-\int _{a}^{b}f(x,t)\,dt}{h}}&={\frac {1}{h}}\int _{x}^{x+h}\int _{a}^{b}f_{x}(s,t)\,dt\,ds\\[2ex]&={\frac {F(x+h)-F(x)}{h}}\end{aligned}}}

Where we have defined:

{\displaystyle F(u):=\int _{x_{0}}^{u}\int _{a}^{b}f_{x}(s,t)\,dt\,ds}

(we may replace x0 here by any other point between x0 and x)

F is differentiable with derivative {\textstyle \int _{a}^{b}f_{x}(x,t)\,dt}, so we can take the limit where h approaches zero. For the left hand side this limit is:

{\displaystyle {\frac {d}{dx}}\int _{a}^{b}f(x,t)\,dt}

For the right hand side, we get:

{\displaystyle F'(x)=\int _{a}^{b}f_{x}(x,t)\,dt}

And we thus prove the desired result:

{\displaystyle {\frac {d}{dx}}\int _{a}^{b}f(x,t)\,dt=\int _{a}^{b}f_{x}(x,t)\,dt}

Proof 2: Uniform control of the difference quotient

For this proof write g(t,\alpha)=f(\alpha,t), so that \alpha is the parameter and t the integration variable.

Suppose a and b are constant, and that g(t) involves a parameter α which is constant in the integration but may vary to form different integrals. Assume that g(t, α) is a continuous function of t and α in the compact set {(t, α) : α0 ≤ α ≤ α1 and a ≤ t ≤ b}, and that the partial derivative gα(t, α) exists and is continuous. If one defines:

{\displaystyle \varphi (\alpha )=\int _{a}^{b}g(t,\alpha )\,dt,}

then {\displaystyle \varphi } may be differentiated with respect to α by differentiating under the integral sign, i.e.,

{\displaystyle {\frac {d\varphi }{d\alpha }}=\int _{a}^{b}{\frac {\partial }{\partial \alpha }}g(t,\alpha )\,dt.}

By the Heine–Cantor theorem g is uniformly continuous in that set. In other words, for any ε > 0, all sufficiently small |\Delta\alpha| satisfy for all values of t in [a, b],

{\displaystyle |g(t,\alpha +\Delta \alpha )-g(t,\alpha )|<\varepsilon .}

On the other hand,

\begin{aligned} &=|\varphi(\alpha+\Delta\alpha)-\varphi(\alpha)|\\ &=\left|\int_a^b g(t,\alpha+\Delta\alpha)\,dt-\int_a^b g(t,\alpha)\,dt\right|\\ &=\left|\int_a^b[g(t,\alpha+\Delta\alpha)-g(t,\alpha)]\,dt\right|\\ &\le\varepsilon(b-a). \end{aligned}

Hence φ(α) is a continuous function.

Similarly if {\displaystyle {\frac {\partial }{\partial \alpha }}g(t,\alpha )} exists and is continuous, then the mean value theorem and uniform continuity of this derivative give, for every ε > 0 and all sufficiently small nonzero |\Delta\alpha|:

{\displaystyle \forall t\in [a,b],\quad \left|{\frac {g(t,\alpha +\Delta \alpha )-g(t,\alpha )}{\Delta \alpha }}-{\frac {\partial g}{\partial \alpha }}\right|<\varepsilon .}

Therefore,

{\displaystyle {\frac {\Delta \varphi }{\Delta \alpha }}=\int _{a}^{b}{\frac {g(t,\alpha +\Delta \alpha )-g(t,\alpha )}{\Delta \alpha }}\,dt=\int _{a}^{b}{\frac {\partial g(t,\alpha )}{\partial \alpha }}\,dt+R,}

where

{\displaystyle |R|<\int _{a}^{b}\varepsilon \,dt=\varepsilon (b-a).}

Since ε is arbitrary, letting Δα → 0 gives

{\displaystyle \lim _{{\Delta \alpha }\to 0}{\frac {\Delta \varphi }{\Delta \alpha }}={\frac {d\varphi }{d\alpha }}=\int _{a}^{b}{\frac {\partial }{\partial \alpha }}g(t,\alpha )\,dt.}

This is the formula we set out to prove.

Proof 3: Apply bounded convergence to difference quotients

Regard the continuous integrands on [a,b] as Lebesgue integrands; their Lebesgue and Riemann integrals agree.

Let

{\displaystyle u(x)=\int _{a}^{b}f(x,t)\,dt.}

By the definition of the derivative, it suffices to establish the following limit:

{\displaystyle u'(x)=\lim _{h\to 0}{\frac {u(x+h)-u(x)}{h}}.}

Substitute the definition of u into this difference quotient. The difference of two integrals equals the integral of the difference, and 1/h is a constant, so

{\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}u'(x)&=\lim _{h\to 0}{\frac {\int _{a}^{b}f(x+h,t)\,dt-\int _{a}^{b}f(x,t)\,dt}{h}}\\&=\lim _{h\to 0}{\frac {\int _{a}^{b}\left(f(x+h,t)-f(x,t)\right)\,dt}{h}}\\&=\lim _{h\to 0}\int _{a}^{b}{\frac {f(x+h,t)-f(x,t)}{h}}\,dt.\end{aligned}}}

We now show that the limit can be passed through the integral sign.

We claim that the passage of the limit under the integral sign is valid by the bounded convergence theorem (a corollary of the dominated convergence theorem). For each sufficiently small nonzero δ, consider the difference quotient

{\displaystyle f_{\delta }(x,t)={\frac {f(x+\delta ,t)-f(x,t)}{\delta }}.}

For t fixed, the mean value theorem implies there exists z between x and x + δ such that

{\displaystyle f_{\delta }(x,t)=f_{x}(z,t).}

Continuity of fx(x, t) and compactness of the domain together imply that fx(x, t) is bounded. The above application of the mean value theorem therefore gives a uniform (independent of {\displaystyle t}) bound on {\displaystyle f_{\delta }(x,t)}. The difference quotients converge pointwise to the partial derivative fx by the assumption that the partial derivative exists.

The above argument shows that for every sequence of nonzero increments {δn} → 0, the sequence {\displaystyle \{f_{\delta _{n}}(x,t)\}} is uniformly bounded and converges pointwise to fx. The bounded convergence theorem states that if a sequence of functions on a set of finite measure is uniformly bounded and converges pointwise, then passage of the limit under the integral is valid. In particular, the limit and integral may be exchanged for every sequence of nonzero increments {δn} → 0. Therefore, the limit as δ → 0 may be passed through the integral sign.

Thus

u'(x)=\int_a^b f_x(x,t)\,dt.
