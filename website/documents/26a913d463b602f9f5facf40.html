Statement

A Gaussian integer is u+vi with u,v\in\mathbb Z and i^2=-1; put N(u+vi)=u^2+v^2. For Gaussian integers \alpha,\beta with \beta\ne0, choose q by rounding both real coordinates of \alpha/\beta to nearest integers. Then r=\alpha-q\beta satisfies N(r)<N(\beta). Repeating these divisions terminates with a common divisor of greatest norm, unique up to multiplication by one of 1,-1,i,-i. Divisibility means equality to a Gaussian-integer multiple.

Level
High school only
Level justification

Gaussian integers, norm, divisibility and rounding are all defined by integer coordinates and complex arithmetic. The statement needs a descending nonnegative integer and elementary divisor comparisons, not abstract Euclidean-domain theory.

Proof 1: Round both quotient coordinates and descend in norm — outline

The Euclidean algorithm developed for two Gaussian integers α and β is nearly the same as that for ordinary integers, but differs in two respects. We set r−2 = α and r−1 = β, and the task at each step k is to identify a quotient qk and a remainder rk such that

{\displaystyle r_{k}=r_{k-2}-q_{k}r_{k-1},}

where every remainder is strictly smaller than its predecessor: |rk| < |rk−1|. The first difference is that the quotients and remainders are themselves Gaussian integers, and thus are complex numbers. The quotients qk are generally found by rounding the real and imaginary parts of the exact ratio (such as the complex number α/β) to the nearest integers. The second difference lies in the necessity of defining how one complex remainder can be "smaller" than another. To do this, a norm function f(u + vi) = u2 + v2 is defined, which converts every Gaussian integer u + vi into an ordinary integer. After each step k of the Euclidean algorithm, the norm of the remainder f(rk) is smaller than the norm of the preceding remainder, f(rk−1). Since the norm is a nonnegative integer and decreases with every step, the Euclidean algorithm for Gaussian integers ends in a finite number of steps. The final nonzero remainder is gcd(α, β), the Gaussian integer of largest norm that divides both α and β; it is unique up to multiplication by a unit, ±1 or ±i.
