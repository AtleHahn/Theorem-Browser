Statement

For x>1, let \pi(x) count primes at most x and let \Pi(x)=\sum_{n\ge1}\pi(x^{1/n})/n. Then \pi(x)=\sum_{n\ge1}\mu(n)\Pi(x^{1/n})/n, where \mu is the Möbius function. These sums are finite for each x. Here \mu(1)=1, \mu(n)=(-1)^k when n is a product of k distinct primes, and \mu(n)=0 when n is divisible by a prime square.

Level
High school only
Level justification

Prime counting, integer roots, and the locally stated prime-factor rule for mu make both displayed sums finite arithmetic expressions.

Proof 1: Recovering prime counts by Möbius inversion (outline)

The function

\Pi(x)=\pi(x)+\frac{\pi(x^{1/2})}{2}+\frac{\pi(x^{1/3})}{3} +\frac{\pi(x^{1/4})}{4}+\frac{\pi(x^{1/5})}{5}+\frac{\pi(x^{1/6})}{6}+\cdots

counts the primes and prime powers up to x, counting a prime power p^n as 1/n. The number of primes can be recovered from this function by using the Möbius inversion formula:

\begin{aligned} \pi(x)&=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\mu(n)}{n}\Pi(x^{1/n})\\ &=\Pi(x)-\frac12\Pi(x^{1/2})-\frac13\Pi(x^{1/3}) -\frac15\Pi(x^{1/5})+\frac16\Pi(x^{1/6})-\cdots, \end{aligned}

where \mu is the Möbius function. These sums have only finitely many nonzero terms for each x. The source invokes Möbius inversion without expanding its divisor-sum cancellation.

Level
High school only
Level justification

Apply the stated finite weighted inversion identity to the prime-power count. All sums terminate for each x, and the Mobius function is explicitly given by prime divisibility; school finite sums and integer arithmetic suffice for this application.
