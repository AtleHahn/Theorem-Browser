Statement

In an abelian category let A\xrightarrow f B\xrightarrow g C\to0 and 0\to A'\xrightarrow{f'}B'\xrightarrow{g'}C' be exact rows, with vertical maps a:A\to A', b:B\to B', c:C\to C' satisfying bf=f' a and cg=g' b. There is a connecting morphism d:\ker c\to\operatorname{coker}a making \ker f\to\ker a\to\ker b\to\ker c\xrightarrow d\operatorname{coker}a\to\operatorname{coker}b\to\operatorname{coker}c\to\operatorname{coker}g' exact. In particular, if f is monic then \ker a\to\ker b is monic, and if g' is epic then \operatorname{coker}b\to\operatorname{coker}c is epic.

Level
Beyond undergraduate level
Level justification

The abstract abelian-category formulation and its kernel-cokernel connecting sequence require substantial categorical homological algebra. Proof 1: Lift an element, descend to a cokernel, and use the embedding theorem — outline

The maps between the kernels and the maps between the cokernels are induced in a natural manner by the given (horizontal) maps because of the commuting equations bf=f'a and cg=g'b. The exactness of the two induced sequences follows in a straightforward way from the exactness of the rows of the original diagram. The important statement of the lemma is that a connecting homomorphism {\displaystyle d} exists which completes the exact sequence.

In the case of abelian groups or modules over some ring, the map {\displaystyle d} can be constructed as follows:

Pick an element {\displaystyle x} in {\displaystyle \operatorname {ker} c} and view it as an element of {\displaystyle C}. Since {\displaystyle g} is surjective, there exists {\displaystyle y} in {\displaystyle B} with {\displaystyle g(y)=x}. By commutativity of the diagram, we have {\displaystyle g'(b(y))=c(g(y))=c(x)=0} (since {\displaystyle x} is in the kernel of {\displaystyle c}), and therefore {\displaystyle b(y)} is in the kernel of {\displaystyle g'}. Since the bottom row is exact, we find an element {\displaystyle z} in {\displaystyle A'} with {\displaystyle f'(z)=b(y)}. By injectivity of {\displaystyle f'}, {\displaystyle z} is unique. We then define {\displaystyle d(x)=z+\operatorname {im} (a)}. Now one has to check that {\displaystyle d} is well-defined (i.e., {\displaystyle d(x)} only depends on {\displaystyle x} and not on the choice of {\displaystyle y}), that it is a homomorphism, and that the resulting long sequence is indeed exact. One may routinely verify the exactness by diagram chasing.

Once that is done, the theorem is proven for abelian groups or modules over a ring. For the general abelian-category case, one may invoke Mitchell’s embedding theorem.
