Statement

Let V_1,V_2 be distinct probability measures on (U,\Sigma). There is Z\in\Sigma with V_1(Z)>V_2(Z), and then V_2(U\setminus Z)>V_1(U\setminus Z) and V_1(Z)+V_2(U\setminus Z)>1. For any w_1,w_2\ge0 with w_1+w_2\le1, at least one of V_1(Z)>w_1 and V_2(U\setminus Z)>w_2 holds.

Level
Beyond undergraduate level
Level justification

The measures and arbitrary measurable pieces require measure theory.

Proof 1: Compare complementary values — outline

Since V_1\ne V_2, there is some measurable piece, {\displaystyle Z\subseteq U}, such that {\displaystyle V_{1}(Z)>V_{2}(Z)}. Let {\displaystyle {\overline {Z}}} be the complement of {\displaystyle Z}; then {\displaystyle V_{2}({\overline {Z}})>V_{1}({\overline {Z}})}. This means that {\displaystyle V_{1}(Z)+V_{2}({\overline {Z}})>1}. However, {\displaystyle w_{1}+w_{2}\leq 1}. Hence, either {\displaystyle V_{1}(Z)>w_{1}} or {\displaystyle V_{2}({\overline {Z}})>w_{2}}.

Level justification

The numerical comparison only uses the complement identity, but the full statement involves distinct measures on an arbitrary measurable space. Retain beyond undergraduate provisionally: understanding sigma-algebras and arbitrary probability measures adds measure-theoretic foundations beyond elementary finite probability.
