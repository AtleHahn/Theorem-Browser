Under the stated uniqueness assumptions for divisor rules, an apportionment rule cannot be neutral to every coalition merger. This isolates a conflict between two forms of resistance to strategic regrouping, showing why compatible-sounding fairness requirements may be jointly impossible in a specified institutional model.

Statement

Consider a class \mathcal D of divisor apportionment rules. Such rules multiply the agents’ entitlements by a common factor and round according to fixed thresholds d(k)\in[k,k+1] between counts k and k+1. Jefferson’s rule uses d(k)=k+1 (rounding down), and Adams’s uses d(k)=k (rounding up); denote these distinct rules by J and A. A rule is split-proof if, whenever two agents receiving a_i,a_j merge their weights, every allowed new coalition allocation is at least a_i+a_j. It is merge-proof if every such allocation is at most that sum. Assume that J is the unique split-proof member of \mathcal D and A the unique merge-proof member. Then no member of \mathcal D always gives every merged coalition exactly the sum of its two former allocations.

Level
High school only
Level justification

Rounding thresholds and the two coalition inequalities are supplied. Quantifying over allocation rules adds no prerequisite beyond elementary weights, integer rounding, equality and uniqueness.

Proof 1: Exact preservation implies both incompatible classifications

Exact preservation of the sum implies both the split-proof lower bound and the merge-proof upper bound. By the two uniqueness assumptions the rule would have to be both J and A, contradicting their distinctness.

Exact preservation implies both supplied inequalities, forcing the rule to equal each of two distinct rules. The apportionment definitions involve only real weights, rounding and finite allocations.
