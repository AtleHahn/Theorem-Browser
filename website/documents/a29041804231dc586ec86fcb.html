Statement

The following two universal assertions are equivalent. (A) If \alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_n are complex algebraic numbers linearly independent over \mathbb Q, then e^{\alpha_1},\ldots,e^{\alpha_n} are algebraically independent over \mathbb Q: no nonzero polynomial in \mathbb Q[X_1,\ldots,X_n] vanishes at that tuple. (B) If \alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_n are distinct complex algebraic numbers and a_1,\ldots,a_n are algebraic numbers not all zero, then \sum_{i=1}^n a_i e^{\alpha_i}\ne0.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Polynomial roots, rational linear independence and complex exponentials belong to undergraduate algebra and calculus. Algebraic numbers are polynomial roots over Q; algebraic independence is the explicitly given absence of a polynomial relation. These definitions suffice without number-field or Galois theory.

Proof 1: Compare exponential monomials and clear rational-basis denominators

Assertion (B) implies assertion (A). Indeed, if {\displaystyle \alpha (1),\ldots ,\alpha (n)} are algebraic numbers that are linearly independent over {\displaystyle \mathbb {Q} }, and

{\displaystyle P(x_{1},\ldots ,x_{n})=\sum b_{i_{1},\ldots ,i_{n}}x_{1}^{i_{1}}\cdots x_{n}^{i_{n}}}

is a polynomial with rational coefficients vanishing at the tuple of exponentials, then we have

{\displaystyle P\left(e^{\alpha (1)},\dots ,e^{\alpha (n)}\right)=\sum b_{i_{1},\dots ,i_{n}}e^{i_{1}\alpha (1)+\cdots +i_{n}\alpha (n)},}

and since {\displaystyle \alpha (1),\ldots ,\alpha (n)} are algebraic numbers which are linearly independent over the rationals, the numbers {\displaystyle i_{1}\alpha (1)+\cdots +i_{n}\alpha (n)} are algebraic and they are distinct for distinct n-tuples {\displaystyle (i_{1},\dots ,i_{n})}. So from (B) we get {\displaystyle b_{i_{1},\ldots ,i_{n}}=0} for all n-tuples {\displaystyle (i_{1},\dots ,i_{n})}.

Now assume that (A) holds. Discard zero coefficients from any putative nontrivial vanishing relation. For {\displaystyle n=1} (B) is trivial, so let us assume that {\displaystyle n>1}, and let {\displaystyle a(1),\ldots ,a(n)} be non-zero algebraic numbers, and {\displaystyle \alpha (1),\ldots ,\alpha (n)} distinct algebraic numbers such that:

{\displaystyle a(1)e^{\alpha (1)}+\cdots +a(n)e^{\alpha (n)}=0.}

For every i ∈ {1, ..., n}, a(i) is algebraic, so it is a root of an irreducible polynomial with integer coefficients of degree d(i). Let us denote the distinct roots of this polynomial a(i)1, ..., a(i)d(i), with a(i)1 = a(i).

Let S be the functions σ which choose one element from each of the sequences (1, ..., d(1)), (1, ..., d(2)), ..., (1, ..., d(n)), so that for every 1 ≤ i ≤ n, σ(i) is an integer between 1 and d(i). We form the polynomial in the variables {\displaystyle x_{11},\dots ,x_{1d(1)},\dots ,x_{n1},\dots ,x_{nd(n)},y_{1},\dots ,y_{n}}

{\displaystyle Q(x_{11},\dots ,x_{nd(n)},y_{1},\dots ,y_{n})=\prod \nolimits _{\sigma \in S}\left(x_{1\sigma (1)}y_{1}+\dots +x_{n\sigma (n)}y_{n}\right).}

Since the product is over all the possible choice functions σ, Q is symmetric in {\displaystyle x_{i1},\dots ,x_{id(i)}} for every i. Therefore Q is a polynomial with integer coefficients in elementary symmetric polynomials of the above variables, for every i, and in the variables yi. Each of the latter symmetric polynomials is a rational number when evaluated in {\displaystyle a(i)_{1},\dots ,a(i)_{d(i)}}.

The evaluated polynomial {\displaystyle Q(a(1)_{1},\dots ,a(n)_{d(n)},e^{\alpha (1)},\dots ,e^{\alpha (n)})} vanishes because one of the choices is just σ(i) = 1 for all i, for which the corresponding factor vanishes according to our assumption above. Thus, the evaluated polynomial is a sum of the form

{\displaystyle b(1)e^{\beta (1)}+b(2)e^{\beta (2)}+\cdots +b(M)e^{\beta (M)}=0,}

where we already grouped the terms with the same exponent. So in the left-hand side we have distinct values β(1), ..., β(M), each of which is still algebraic (being a sum of algebraic numbers) and coefficients {\displaystyle b(1),\dots ,b(M)\in \mathbb {Q} }. The sum is nontrivial: if {\displaystyle \alpha (i)} is maximal in the lexicographic order on real and then imaginary parts, the coefficient of {\displaystyle e^{|S|\alpha (i)}} is just a product of a(i)j's (with possible repetitions), which is non-zero.

Each exponent \beta(m) is an integer linear combination of the \alpha(i). Then, since {\displaystyle n>1} and {\displaystyle \alpha (1),\ldots ,\alpha (n)} are pairwise distinct, the {\displaystyle \mathbb {Q} }-vector subspace {\displaystyle V} of {\displaystyle \mathbb {C} } generated by {\displaystyle \alpha (1),\ldots ,\alpha (n)} is not trivial and we can pick {\displaystyle \alpha (i_{1}),\ldots ,\alpha (i_{k})} to form a basis for {\displaystyle V.} For each {\displaystyle m=1,\dots ,M}, we have

{\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}\beta (m)=q_{m,1}\alpha (i_{1})+\cdots +q_{m,k}\alpha (i_{k}),&&q_{m,j}={\frac {c_{m,j}}{d_{m,j}}};\qquad c_{m,j}\in \mathbb {Z},\ d_{m,j}\in\mathbb Z_{>0} .\end{aligned}}}

For each {\displaystyle j=1,\ldots ,k,} let {\displaystyle d_{j}} be the least common multiple of all the {\displaystyle d_{m,j}} for {\displaystyle m=1,\ldots ,M}, and put {\displaystyle v_{j}={\tfrac {1}{d_{j}}}\alpha (i_{j})}. Then {\displaystyle v_{1},\ldots ,v_{k}} are algebraic numbers, they form a basis of {\displaystyle V}, and each {\displaystyle \beta (m)} is a linear combination of the {\displaystyle v_{j}} with integer coefficients. By multiplying the relation

{\displaystyle b(1)e^{\beta (1)}+b(2)e^{\beta (2)}+\cdots +b(M)e^{\beta (M)}=0,}

by {\displaystyle e^{N(v_{1}+\cdots +v_{k})}}, where {\displaystyle N} is a large enough positive integer, all coordinates become nonnegative integers. Since distinct exponents give distinct monomials, we get a non-trivial algebraic relation with rational coefficients connecting {\displaystyle e^{v_{1}},\cdots ,e^{v_{k}}}, against (A).
