Statement

The language L=\{a^nb^n:n\ge0\} over the two-letter alphabet \{a,b\} is not regular.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Regular means accepted by a finite-state automaton. That concrete finite-memory model can be learned with a small transition diagram; the equal-block language is explicitly given, so understanding the impossibility assertion requires no broader language theory.

Proof 1: Pump the initial block of a letters

  1. Assume that some constant {\displaystyle p\geq 1} exists as required by the pumping lemma for a regular language.
  2. Let {\displaystyle w} in {\displaystyle L} be given by {\displaystyle w=a^{p}b^{p}}, which is a string longer than {\displaystyle p}.
  3. By the pumping lemma, there must exist a decomposition {\displaystyle w=xyz} with {\displaystyle |xy|\leq p} and {\displaystyle |y|\geq 1} such that {\displaystyle xy^{i}z} in {\displaystyle L} for every {\displaystyle i\geq 0}.
  4. Since {\displaystyle |xy|\leq p}, the string {\displaystyle y} only consists of instances of {\displaystyle a}.
  5. Because {\displaystyle |y|\geq 1}, it contains at least one instance of the letter {\displaystyle a}.
  6. Pumping {\displaystyle y} to give {\displaystyle xy^{2}z} gives a word with more instances of the letter {\displaystyle a} than the letter {\displaystyle b}, since some instances of {\displaystyle a} but none of {\displaystyle b} were added.
  7. Therefore, {\displaystyle xy^{2}z} is not in {\displaystyle L} which contradicts the pumping lemma.
  8. Therefore, {\displaystyle L} cannot be regular.

Level justification

Finite automata and regular languages are a modest discrete-mathematical extension. Apply the fully stated pumping lemma to the first block, whose repetition upsets the equal letter counts. No general computability or formal arithmetic theory is needed.
