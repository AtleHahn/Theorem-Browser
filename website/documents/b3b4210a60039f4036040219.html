The four-color theorem became a landmark in debates over computer-assisted proof, because its proof relies on extensive finite case verification. Its philosophical importance concerns the roles of inspectability, trusted computation and explanatory understanding in mathematical knowledge, rather than a limitation stated by the coloring result itself.

Statement

Every finite loopless planar undirected graph G=(V,E) has a proper coloring with at most four colors. Explicitly, there is a map

c:V\longrightarrow\{1,2,3,4\}

such that c(u)\ne c(v) whenever an edge joins u and v.

Loopless means that no edge joins a vertex to itself; parallel edges are allowed. Planar means that the vertices and edges can be drawn in the plane so that edge interiors do not intersect each other or any vertex. A proper coloring assigns different colors to adjacent vertices.

Level
High school only
Level justification

The finite objects, adjacency and coloring rule are explicitly defined and require only elementary reasoning. No proof methods or infinite-graph compactness are prerequisites for the statement.

Proof 1: Reducible configurations and discharging in planar hypermaps (external)

A link to the proof can be found here (Sections5.2–5.6, especially5.4–5.5; accompanying formal proof).

The formal proof files supply the configuration checks supporting the report, including combinatorial4ct.v, reducibility.v and unavoidability.v. Delete parallel copies of edges and color connected components separately. A component with no edges is immediate; otherwise its plane dual has no bridges because the original has no loops. Faces of that dual correspond to original vertices, so the planar bridgeless hypermap theorem gives the required vertex coloring.

Level justification

The statement uses finite graphs and planar drawings. The linked proof requires undergraduate discrete mathematics: planar duality and Euler counting, permutation representations of hypermaps, Kempe chains, reducible configurations, and discharging. Understanding why the finite coloring and unavoidability checks suffice also requires structural induction and correctness arguments for finite algorithms. This cumulative conceptual framework exceeds high-school background and is assessed at advanced undergraduate level; the extensive computation increases the work required to follow the proof without itself requiring graduate mathematics.
