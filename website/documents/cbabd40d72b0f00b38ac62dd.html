Statement

If p=2m+1 is an odd prime, then (m!)^2\equiv(-1)^{m+1}\pmod p. Congruence means equality up to an integer multiple of p.

Proof 1: Pairing opposite residues after the local Wilson argument

Local prime-factorial identity

For a prime p, (p-1)!\equiv-1\pmod p.

The result is trivial when {\displaystyle p=2}, so assume {\displaystyle p} is an odd prime, {\displaystyle p\geq 3}. Since the residue classes modulo {\displaystyle p} form a field, every non-zero residue {\displaystyle a} has a unique multiplicative inverse {\displaystyle a^{-1}}. Euclid's lemma implies that the only values of {\displaystyle a} for which {\displaystyle a\equiv a^{-1}{\pmod {p}}} are {\displaystyle a\equiv \pm 1{\pmod {p}}}. Therefore, with the exception of {\displaystyle \pm 1}, the factors in the expanded form of {\displaystyle (p-1)!} can be arranged in disjoint pairs such that product of each pair is congruent to 1 modulo {\displaystyle p}. This proves the prime-factorial identity.

For the self-inverse residues, a\equiv a^{-1}\pmod p implies p\mid(a^2-1)=(a-1)(a+1). Euclid's lemma gives a\equiv\pm1\pmod p.

Pairing opposite residues

Using Wilson's Theorem, for any odd prime p = 2m + 1, we can rearrange the left hand side of

{\displaystyle 1\cdot 2\cdots (p-1)\ \equiv \ -1\ {\pmod {p}}}

to obtain the equality

{\displaystyle 1\cdot (p-1)\cdot 2\cdot (p-2)\cdots m\cdot (p-m)\ \equiv \ 1\cdot (-1)\cdot 2\cdot (-2)\cdots m\cdot (-m)\ \equiv \ -1{\pmod {p}}.}

This becomes

{\displaystyle \prod _{j=1}^{m}\ j^{2}\ \equiv (-1)^{m+1}{\pmod {p}}}

or

{\displaystyle (m!)^{2}\equiv (-1)^{m+1}{\pmod {p}}.}
