Statement

For real x with \cos x\ne0, with angles measured in radians, \frac{d}{dx}\tan x=1+\tan^2x=\sec^2x=1/\cos^2x.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Understanding the statement involves Tangent; Real derivatives; Real radian arguments. These prerequisites fit undergraduate coursework or a brief extension of it. A bright US college student should be able to understand this statement after approximately 30 minutes of focused background research; this assesses understanding the claim, not its proof.

Proof 1: Substitute in the quotient rule

The quotient rule can be used to find the derivative of {\textstyle \tan x={\frac {\sin x}{\cos x}}} as follows:

{\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}{\frac {d}{dx}}\tan x&={\frac {d}{dx}}\left({\frac {\sin x}{\cos x}}\right)\\&={\frac {\left({\frac {d}{dx}}\sin x\right)(\cos x)-(\sin x)\left({\frac {d}{dx}}\cos x\right)}{\cos ^{2}x}}\\&={\frac {(\cos x)(\cos x)-(\sin x)(-\sin x)}{\cos ^{2}x}}\\&={\frac {\cos ^{2}x+\sin ^{2}x}{\cos ^{2}x}}\\&={\frac {1}{\cos ^{2}x}}=\sec ^{2}x.\end{aligned}}}
Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Differentiate sine divided by cosine and simplify the numerator with the Pythagorean identity. The nonzero cosine ensures the quotient rule applies.

Proof 2: Use the tangent addition difference quotient

Write \theta=x.

To calculate the derivative of the tangent function tan θ, we use first principles. By definition:

{\displaystyle {\frac {\operatorname {d} }{\operatorname {d} \!\theta }}\,\tan \theta =\lim _{\delta \to 0}\left({\frac {\tan(\theta +\delta )-\tan \theta }{\delta }}\right).}

Using the well-known angle formula tan(α+β) = (tan α + tan β) / (1 - tan α tan β), we have:

{\displaystyle {\frac {\operatorname {d} }{\operatorname {d} \!\theta }}\,\tan \theta =\lim _{\delta \to 0}\left[{\frac {{\frac {\tan \theta +\tan \delta }{1-\tan \theta \tan \delta }}-\tan \theta }{\delta }}\right]=\lim _{\delta \to 0}\left[{\frac {\tan \theta +\tan \delta -\tan \theta +\tan ^{2}\theta \tan \delta }{\delta \left(1-\tan \theta \tan \delta \right)}}\right].}

Using the fact that the limit of a product is the product of the limits:

{\displaystyle {\frac {\operatorname {d} }{\operatorname {d} \!\theta }}\,\tan \theta =\lim _{\delta \to 0}{\frac {\tan \delta }{\delta }}\times \lim _{\delta \to 0}\left({\frac {1+\tan ^{2}\theta }{1-\tan \theta \tan \delta }}\right).}

Using \lim_{\delta\to0}\tan\delta/\delta=1, and the fact that tan δ tends to 0 as δ tends to 0:

{\displaystyle {\frac {\operatorname {d} }{\operatorname {d} \!\theta }}\,\tan \theta =1\times {\frac {1+\tan ^{2}\theta }{1-0}}=1+\tan ^{2}\theta .}

We see immediately that:

{\displaystyle {\frac {\operatorname {d} }{\operatorname {d} \!\theta }}\,\tan \theta =1+{\frac {\sin ^{2}\theta }{\cos ^{2}\theta }}={\frac {\cos ^{2}\theta +\sin ^{2}\theta }{\cos ^{2}\theta }}={\frac {1}{\cos ^{2}\theta }}=\sec ^{2}\theta \,.}
