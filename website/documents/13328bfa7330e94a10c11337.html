Statement

If X is a real random variable with |X|\le T almost surely for some T>0, then \mathbb P(|X|\ge t)\le2e^{-ct^2} for all t\ge0, with c=\log(2)/T^2. Thus X is sub-Gaussian in the tail-bound sense.

Level
High school only
Level justification

The assertion gives an explicit bound on the probability that a real-valued random quantity exceeds a threshold. Almost surely means the exceptional outcomes have probability zero, and the sub-Gaussian tail meaning is supplied by the formula itself. Probabilities between zero and one, logarithms and exponential inequalities suffice; neither expectations nor a general theory of measures is needed.

Proof 1: Separate thresholds below and above the bound

For any bounded variable X,

\mathbb P(|X|\geq t)=0\leq2e^{-ct^2}

for t>T. Then 2e^{-cT^2}\leq2e^{-ct^2} for all 0\leq t\leq T, so taking c=\log(2)/T^2 yields

\mathbb P(|X|\geq t)\leq1\leq2e^{-cT^2}\leq2e^{-ct^2}

for 0\leq t\leq T. So every bounded variable is sub-Gaussian.

Level
High school only
Level justification

The complete statement is a tail-probability inequality, with sub-Gaussian defined by the displayed bound. The almost-sure hypothesis makes the probability zero for t>T. For 0<=t<=T, c=log(2)/T^2 gives 2 exp(-ct^2)>=1, which bounds every probability. Only probabilities between zero and one and high-school exponential monotonicity are required.
