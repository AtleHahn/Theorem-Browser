Excluded middle is equivalent to choice on inhabited images of a doubleton

A seemingly modest choice principle for sets presented as two-element images already entails excluded middle in this constructive setting. It shows that existence principles can carry hidden logical commitments and that classical and constructive notions of choice require careful distinction.

Statement

Work in constructive set theory with pairing, extensionality and separation for the formulas used below, and with the distinct finite ordinals 0=\varnothing, 1=\{0\} and 2=\{0,1\}. Assume full separation. The law of excluded middle is equivalent to the following principle: every inhabited set Y that is the image of a surjection 2\to Y has a choice function whenever all its members are inhabited.

Proof 1: Use finite choice in one direction and the two-set construction in the other — outline

Choice is valid in all finite sets. Given that in classical set theory the sets under consideration here are provably all finite (with exactly either the cardinalities one or two), the forward direction of the equivalence is thus established.

Let P be any proposition. To prove the backward-direction one considers two subsets of any doubleton with two distinguishable members. As {\displaystyle 0\neq 1}, a convenient choice is {\displaystyle \{0,1\}}. So, using Separation, let

{\displaystyle U=\{x\in \{0,1\}\mid (x=0)\vee P\}}

and

{\displaystyle V=\{x\in \{0,1\}\mid (x=1)\lor P\}.}

Both {\displaystyle U} and {\displaystyle V} are inhabited, as witnessed by {\displaystyle 0\in U} and {\displaystyle 1\in V}. If the proposition {\displaystyle P} can be proven, then both of these sets equal {\displaystyle \{0,1\}}. In particular, {\displaystyle P\,\to \,U=V} by extensionality. In turn, for any mathematical function {\displaystyle g} that can take both of these sets as an argument, one finds {\displaystyle P\,\to \,g(U)=g(V)}, the contrapositive of which is {\displaystyle g(U)\neq g(V)\,\to \,\neg P}.

Assume the stated choice principle. The rest of the proof concerns the pair {\displaystyle X:=\{U,V\}}, a set of inhabited sets. (Indeed, {\displaystyle X} is itself inhabited and even validates {\displaystyle X\leq ^{*}2}, meaning it is the surjective image of 2, under 0\mapsto U and 1\mapsto V. However, note that when excluded middle is not assumed, {\displaystyle X} need not be provably finite, in the bijection sense.)

The assumed principle supplies a choice function f on X. A choice function {\displaystyle f} on {\displaystyle X} by definition has to map into the general union, which here is {\displaystyle \{0,1\}}, and fulfill

{\displaystyle f(U)\in U\,\land \,f(V)\in V.}

Using the definition of the two subsets and the function's established codomain, this reduces to

{\displaystyle {\big (}f(U)=0\lor P{\big )}\land {\big (}f(V)=1\lor P{\big )}.}

Using the law of distributivity, this in turn implies {\displaystyle P\lor {\big (}f(U)\neq f(V){\big )}}. By the implication g(U)\ne g(V)\to\neg P proved above, the existence of a choice function on this set thus implies the disjunction {\displaystyle P\lor \neg P}. This concludes the proof.
