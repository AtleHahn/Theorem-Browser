Statement

Let f:[0,1]\to\mathbb R equal 1 at 0 and 0 on (0,1]. Then f'=0 on (0,1) but no interior derivative equals the endpoint secant slope.

Proof 1: Compare the zero derivative with the nonzero secant slope

The necessity of endpoint continuity can be seen by the counterexample where the function {\displaystyle f} given by

{\displaystyle f(x)={\begin{cases}1,&{\text{at }}x=0\\0,&{\text{if }}x\in (0,1]\end{cases}}}

is differentiable on the interior since {\displaystyle f'(x)=0} on {\displaystyle (0,1)} but is not continuous at the endpoint since {\displaystyle f} is not right-continuous at {\displaystyle 0}. The average rate of change of {\displaystyle f} over {\displaystyle [0,1]} is given by

{\displaystyle {\frac {f(1)-f(0)}{1-0}}=-1}

but there doesn't exist {\displaystyle x\in (0,1)} such that {\displaystyle f'(x)=-1}, so the theorem doesn't hold.
