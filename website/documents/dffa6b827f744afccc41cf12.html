Statement

Let a<b, let g:[a,b]\to\mathbb R be continuous, and put S=\{x\in[a,b]:\text{there exists }y\in(x,b]\text{ with }g(y)>g(x)\}. Write E=S\cap(a,b). Then E is open and is a countable union of pairwise disjoint open intervals (a_k,b_k). On every such interval g(x)<g(b_k). Its endpoint values satisfy g(a_k)=g(b_k), except that if a_k=a\in S then g(a)<g(b_k).

Proof 1: Decompose the open shaded set and apply the interval lemma — outline

We need a lemma: Suppose [c,d)\subset S, but d\notin S. Then g(c)<g(d). To prove this, suppose g(c)\ge g(d). Then g achieves its maximum on [c,d] at some point z<d. Since z\in S, there is a y in (z,b] with g(z)<g(y). If y\le d, then g would not reach its maximum on [c,d] at z. Thus, y\in(d,b], and g(d)\le g(z)<g(y). This means that d\in S, which is a contradiction, thus establishing the lemma.

The set E is open, so it is composed of a countable union of disjoint intervals (a_k,b_k). Their right endpoints are not in S; this includes b, which is not in S by definition.

It follows immediately from the lemma that g(x)<g(b_k) for x in (a_k,b_k). Since g is continuous, we must also have g(a_k)\le g(b_k).

If a_k\ne a or a\notin S, then a_k\notin S, so g(a_k)\ge g(b_k), for otherwise a_k\in S. Thus, g(a_k)=g(b_k) in these cases.

Finally, if a_k=a\in S, the lemma tells us that g(a)<g(b_k).
