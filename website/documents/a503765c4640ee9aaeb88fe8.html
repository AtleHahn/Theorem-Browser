Statement

Let m_0,m_1 be positive integers, 0\le p_0,p_1\le1, and let X\sim B(m_0,p_0) and Y\sim B(m_1,p_1) be independent binomial random variables. For Z=X+Y, \operatorname{Var}(Z)\le E[Z](1-E[Z]/(m_0+m_1)). Equivalently, its variance is at most that of a binomial variable with m_0+m_1 trials and success probability (m_0p_0+m_1p_1)/(m_0+m_1).

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

The variables are finite Bernoulli trials or their binomial sums. Independence, expectation and variance are accessible through finite weighted sums, while the displayed comparison is algebraic. No infinite probability-space construction is needed to understand the statement.

Proof 1: Reduce the difference to a square

We wish to prove that

{\displaystyle Var(Z)\leqslant E[Z](1-{\frac {E[Z]}{m_{0}+m_{1}}})}

We will prove this inequality by finding an expression for Var(Z) and substituting it on the left-hand side, then showing that the inequality always holds.

For comparison, if W has a binomial distribution with parameters n and p, then the expected value of W is given by E[W] = np and the variance of W is given by Var[W] = np(1 – p). Taking E[W]=E[Z], letting n = m0 + m1 and substituting E[W] for np gives

{\displaystyle Var(W)=E[W](1-{\frac {E[W]}{m_{0}+m_{1}}})}

The random variables X and Y are independent, so the variance of the sum is equal to the sum of the variances, that is

{\displaystyle Var(Z)=E[X](1-{\frac {E[X]}{m_{0}}})+E[Y](1-{\frac {E[Y]}{m_{1}}})}

In order to prove the theorem, it is therefore sufficient to prove that

{\displaystyle E[X](1-{\frac {E[X]}{m_{0}}})+E[Y](1-{\frac {E[Y]}{m_{1}}})\leqslant E[Z](1-{\frac {E[Z]}{m_{0}+m_{1}}})}

Substituting E[X] + E[Y] for E[Z] gives

{\displaystyle E[X](1-{\frac {E[X]}{m_{0}}})+E[Y](1-{\frac {E[Y]}{m_{1}}})\leqslant (E[X]+E[Y])(1-{\frac {E[X]+E[Y]}{m_{0}+m_{1}}})}

Multiplying out the brackets and subtracting E[X] + E[Y] from both sides yields

{\displaystyle -{\frac {E[X]^{2}}{m_{0}}}-{\frac {E[Y]^{2}}{m_{1}}}\leqslant -{\frac {(E[X]+E[Y])^{2}}{m_{0}+m_{1}}}}

Equivalently, before subtracting the expectations, multiplying out the brackets yields

{\displaystyle E[X]-{\frac {E[X]^{2}}{m_{0}}}+E[Y]-{\frac {E[Y]^{2}}{m_{1}}}\leqslant E[X]+E[Y]-{\frac {(E[X]+E[Y])^{2}}{m_{0}+m_{1}}}}

Subtracting E[X] and E[Y] from both sides and multiplying by -1 gives

{\displaystyle {\frac {E[X]^{2}}{m_{0}}}+{\frac {E[Y]^{2}}{m_{1}}}\geqslant {\frac {(E[X]+E[Y])^{2}}{m_{0}+m_{1}}}}

Expanding the right-hand side gives

{\displaystyle {\frac {E[X]^{2}}{m_{0}}}+{\frac {E[Y]^{2}}{m_{1}}}\geqslant {\frac {E[X]^{2}+2E[X]E[Y]+E[Y]^{2}}{m_{0}+m_{1}}}}

Multiplying by {\displaystyle m_{0}m_{1}(m_{0}+m_{1})} yields

{\displaystyle (m_{0}m_{1}+{m_{1}}^{2}){E[X]^{2}}+({m_{0}}^{2}+m_{0}m_{1}){E[Y]^{2}}\geqslant m_{0}m_{1}({E[X]}^{2}+2E[X]E[Y]+{E[Y]^{2}})}

Deducting the right-hand side gives the relation

{\displaystyle {m_{1}}^{2}{E[X]^{2}}-2m_{0}m_{1}E[X]E[Y]+{m_{0}}^{2}{E[Y]^{2}}\geqslant 0}

or equivalently

{\displaystyle (m_{1}E[X]-m_{0}E[Y])^{2}\geqslant 0}

The square of a real number is always greater than or equal to zero, so this is true for all independent binomial distributions that X and Y could take. This is sufficient to prove the theorem.
