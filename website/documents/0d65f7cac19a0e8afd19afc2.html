Proof 1: Inverse pairing and negative residue pairs

Put K=h!=\prod_{k=1}^{(p-1)/2}k.

Firstly it follows from Euclid's Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic that {\displaystyle ab\equiv 0{\pmod {p}}\iff a\equiv 0{\pmod {p}}\ \ {\hbox{or}}\ \ b\equiv 0{\pmod {p}}}. Consequently, {\displaystyle a^{2}\equiv 1{\pmod {p}}\iff a\equiv \pm 1{\pmod {p}}}: that is, {\displaystyle \pm 1} are their own inverses modulo {\displaystyle p} and this property is unique to them. It then follows from the validity of Euclidean division in the integers, and the fact that {\displaystyle p} is prime, that for every {\displaystyle 2\leq a\leq p-2} the gcd of {\displaystyle a} and {\displaystyle p} may be expressed via the Euclidean algorithm yielding a unique and distinct inverse {\displaystyle a^{-1}\neq a} of {\displaystyle a} modulo {\displaystyle p}. In particular therefore the product of all non-zero residues modulo {\displaystyle p} is {\displaystyle -1}. Let {\displaystyle L=\prod _{l={\frac {p+1}{2}}}^{p-1}l}: from what has just been observed, {\displaystyle KL\equiv -1{\pmod {p}}}. But by definition, since each term in {\displaystyle K} may be paired with its negative in {\displaystyle L}, {\displaystyle L\equiv(-1)^{\frac {p-1}{2}}K\pmod p}. Thus (-1)^hK^2\equiv-1\pmod p, or K^2\equiv(-1)^{h+1}\pmod p, as required.

Level justification

Pair each nonzero residue with its inverse, leaving plus and minus one, then pair the upper half of residues with negatives of the lower half. Elementary integer congruences, prime divisibility and products suffice.
