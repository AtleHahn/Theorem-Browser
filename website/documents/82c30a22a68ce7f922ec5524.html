Statement

Let R_1,\ldots,R_r be pairwise disjoint nonempty finite sets of distinct complex roots, each the full root set of a nonconstant integer polynomial. The numbers \sum_{\alpha\in R_t}e^\alpha, 1\le t\le r, are linearly independent over \mathbb Q.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Polynomial roots, rational linear independence and complex exponentials belong to undergraduate algebra and calculus. Algebraic numbers are polynomial roots over Q; algebraic independence is the explicitly given absence of a polynomial relation. These definitions suffice without number-field or Galois theory.

Proof 1: Compare factorial divisibility with exponential integral bounds — outline

Clear denominators in a putative rational relation and discard groups with zero coefficient. Write R_t=\{\gamma(t)_1,\ldots,\gamma(t)_{m(t)}\}, and let c(t) be the remaining nonzero integer coefficients.

To simplify the notation set:

{\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}&n_{0}=0,&&\\&n_{i}=\sum \nolimits _{k=1}^{i}m(k),&&i=1,\ldots ,r\\&n=n_{r},&&\\&\alpha _{n_{i-1}+j}=\gamma (i)_{j},&&1\leq i\leq r,\ 1\leq j\leq m(i)\\&\beta _{n_{i-1}+j}=c(i).\end{aligned}}}

Then the statement becomes

{\displaystyle \sum _{k=1}^{n}\beta _{k}e^{\alpha _{k}}\neq 0.}

Let p be a sufficiently large prime number and define the following polynomials:

{\displaystyle f_{i}(x)={\frac {\ell ^{np}(x-\alpha _{1})^{p}\cdots (x-\alpha _{n})^{p}}{(x-\alpha _{i})}},}

where ℓ is a non-zero integer such that {\displaystyle \ell \alpha _{1},\ldots ,\ell \alpha _{n}} are all algebraic integers. Notice ℓ does not depend on p. Define

{\displaystyle I_{i}(s)=\int _{0}^{s}e^{s-x}f_{i}(x)\,dx.}

Using integration by parts we arrive at

{\displaystyle I_{i}(s)=e^{s}\sum _{j=0}^{np-1}f_{i}^{(j)}(0)-\sum _{j=0}^{np-1}f_{i}^{(j)}(s),}

where {\displaystyle np-1} is the degree of {\displaystyle f_{i}}, and {\displaystyle f_{i}^{(j)}} is the j-th derivative of {\displaystyle f_{i}}. This also holds for s complex (in this case the integral has to be intended as a contour integral, for example along the straight segment from 0 to s) because

{\displaystyle -e^{s-x}\sum _{j=0}^{np-1}f_{i}^{(j)}(x)}

is a primitive of {\displaystyle e^{s-x}f_{i}(x)}.

Consider the following sum:

{\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}J_{i}&=\sum _{k=1}^{n}\beta _{k}I_{i}(\alpha _{k})\\[5pt]&=\sum _{k=1}^{n}\beta _{k}\left(e^{\alpha _{k}}\sum _{j=0}^{np-1}f_{i}^{(j)}(0)-\sum _{j=0}^{np-1}f_{i}^{(j)}(\alpha _{k})\right)\\[5pt]&=\left(\sum _{j=0}^{np-1}f_{i}^{(j)}(0)\right)\left(\sum _{k=1}^{n}\beta _{k}e^{\alpha _{k}}\right)-\sum _{k=1}^{n}\sum _{j=0}^{np-1}\beta _{k}f_{i}^{(j)}(\alpha _{k})\\[5pt]&=-\sum _{k=1}^{n}\sum _{j=0}^{np-1}\beta _{k}f_{i}^{(j)}(\alpha _{k})\end{aligned}}}

In the last line we assumed that the conclusion of the Lemma is false. In order to complete the proof we need to reach a contradiction. We will do so by estimating {\displaystyle |J_{1}\cdots J_{n}|} in two different ways.

First {\displaystyle f_{i}^{(j)}(\alpha _{k})} is an algebraic integer which is divisible by p! for {\displaystyle j\geq p} and vanishes for {\displaystyle j<p} unless {\displaystyle j=p-1} and {\displaystyle k=i}, in which case it equals

{\displaystyle \ell ^{np}(p-1)!\prod _{k\neq i}(\alpha _{i}-\alpha _{k})^{p}.}

The exceptional term, even after multiplication by the nonzero integer coefficient \beta_i, is not divisible by p for sufficiently large primes avoiding the fixed coefficients and the integers below. Indeed, putting

{\displaystyle \delta _{i}=\prod _{k\neq i}(\ell \alpha _{i}-\ell \alpha _{k})}

(which is a non-zero algebraic integer) and calling d_i\in\mathbb Z its nonzero norm (the product of its algebraic conjugates), exclude the primes dividing \ell\beta_i d_i. The exceptional term is \ell^p(p-1)!\delta_i^p; its norm, and the norm after multiplication by \beta_i, are not divisible by p. Thus that term cannot be divisible by p in the algebraic integers.

So {\displaystyle J_{i}} is a non-zero algebraic integer divisible by (p − 1)!. Now

{\displaystyle J_{i}=-\sum _{j=0}^{np-1}\sum _{t=1}^{r}c(t)\left(f_{i}^{(j)}(\alpha _{n_{t-1}+1})+\cdots +f_{i}^{(j)}(\alpha _{n_{t}})\right).}

The common numerator of the f_i has integer coefficients: its coefficients are rational by symmetry of the full root sets and algebraic integers by the choice of \ell.

Since each {\displaystyle f_{i}(x)} is obtained by dividing a fixed polynomial with integer coefficients by {\displaystyle (x-\alpha _{i})}, it is of the form

{\displaystyle f_{i}(x)=\sum _{m=0}^{np-1}g_{m}(\alpha _{i})x^{m},}

where {\displaystyle g_{m}} is a polynomial (with integer coefficients) independent of i. The same holds for the derivatives {\displaystyle f_{i}^{(j)}(x)}.

Hence, by the fundamental theorem of symmetric polynomials,

{\displaystyle f_{i}^{(j)}(\alpha _{n_{t-1}+1})+\cdots +f_{i}^{(j)}(\alpha _{n_{t}})}

is a fixed polynomial with rational coefficients evaluated in {\displaystyle \alpha _{i}} (this is seen by grouping the same powers of {\displaystyle \alpha _{n_{t-1}+1},\dots ,\alpha _{n_{t}}} appearing in the expansion and using the fact that these algebraic numbers form the full root set of an integer polynomial). So the same is true of {\displaystyle J_{i}}, i.e. it equals {\displaystyle G(\alpha _{i})}, where G is a polynomial with rational coefficients independent of i.

Finally {\displaystyle J_{1}\cdots J_{n}=G(\alpha _{1})\cdots G(\alpha _{n})} is rational (again by the fundamental theorem of symmetric polynomials) and is a non-zero algebraic integer divisible by {\displaystyle (p-1)!^{n}} (since the {\displaystyle J_{i}}'s are algebraic integers divisible by {\displaystyle (p-1)!}). Therefore

{\displaystyle |J_{1}\cdots J_{n}|\geq (p-1)!^{n}.}

However one clearly has:

{\displaystyle |I_{i}(\alpha _{k})|\leq {|\alpha _{k}|}e^{|\alpha _{k}|}F_{i}({|\alpha _{k}|}),}

where Fi is the polynomial whose coefficients are the absolute values of those of fi (this follows directly from the definition of {\displaystyle I_{i}(s)}). Thus

{\displaystyle |J_{i}|\leq \sum _{k=1}^{n}\left|\beta _{k}\alpha _{k}\right|e^{|\alpha _{k}|}F_{i}\left(\left|\alpha _{k}\right|\right)}

and so by the construction of the {\displaystyle f_{i}}'s we have {\displaystyle |J_{1}\cdots J_{n}|\leq C^{p}} for a sufficiently large C independent of p, which contradicts the previous inequality. This proves the statement. ∎
