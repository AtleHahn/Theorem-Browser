Statement

For n≥1 and variables x_1,\ldots,x_n, let p_k=\sum_jx_j^k for k≥1, let e_0=1, and let e_k=\sum_{j_1<\cdots<j_k}x_{j_1}\cdots x_{j_k}, with e_k=0 for k>n. As polynomial identities, for every k≥1, ke_k=\sum_{i=1}^k(-1)^{i-1}e_{k-i}p_i.

Level
High school only
Level justification

All symmetric sums and power sums are explicitly finite polynomials. The assertion is an indexed polynomial identity, understandable through elementary algebra.

Proof 1: Logarithmic derivative and coefficient comparison

Fix arbitrary real values of the variables and take |t| sufficiently small that the following series converge and 1-x_jt>0. We use the generating functions

\begin{aligned} E(t)&=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(-1)^ke_k(x_1,\ldots,x_n)t^k =\prod_{j=1}^n(1-x_jt),\\ P(t)&=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}p_k(x_1,\ldots,x_n)t^k =\sum_{j=1}^n\bigl(x_jt+(x_jt)^2+\cdots\bigr) =\sum_{j=1}^n\frac{x_jt}{1-x_jt}. \end{aligned}

The equation

\frac1{E(t)}\frac{dE(t)}{dt} =\frac{d}{dt}\ln E(t) =\sum_{j=1}^n\frac{d}{dt}\ln(1-x_jt) =\sum_{j=1}^n\frac{-x_j}{1-x_jt} =-\frac{P(t)}t

gives us

-t\frac{dE(t)}{dt}=P(t)E(t),

and equality of the coefficient of t^k on each side of that equation gives

(-1)^{k+1}ke_k =\sum_{j=0}^{\min(n,k-1)}(-1)^je_jp_{k-j}.

The sum stops at k-1 because P(t) has zero constant coefficient. Reindexing gives the stated identity. Since it holds for arbitrary real values of the variables, it is a polynomial identity.

Proof 2: Cancel hook-shaped monomial sums

For k=1, the identity is e_1=p_1. Fix some k\geq2, and define the symmetric function rk(i) for 2 ≤ i ≤ k as the sum of all distinct monomials of degree k obtained by multiplying one variable raised to the power i with k − i distinct other variables. In particular rk(k) = pk; for rk(1) the description would amount to that of ek, but this case was excluded since here monomials no longer have any distinguished variable. All products piek−i can be expressed in terms of the rk(j) with the first and last case being somewhat special. One has

{\displaystyle p_{i}e_{k-i}=r_{k}(i)+r_{k}(i+1)\quad {\text{for }}1<i<k}

since each product of terms on the left involving distinct variables contributes to rk(i), while those where the variable from pi already occurs among the variables of the term from ek−i contributes to rk(i + 1), and all terms on the right are so obtained exactly once. For i = k one multiplies by e0 = 1, giving trivially

{\displaystyle p_{k}e_{0}=p_{k}=r_{k}(k).}

Finally the product p1ek−1 for i = 1 gives contributions to rk(i + 1) = rk(2) like for other values i < k, but the remaining contributions produce k times each monomial of ek, since any one of the variables may come from the factor p1; thus

{\displaystyle p_{1}e_{k-1}=ke_{k}+r_{k}(2).}

The k-th Newton identity is now obtained by taking the alternating sum of these equations, in which all terms of the form rk(i) cancel out.
