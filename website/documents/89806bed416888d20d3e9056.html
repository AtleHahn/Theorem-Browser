Statement

Let R be a commutative ring with identity. Let n\ge1 be an integer, A\in M_n(R), and p(t)=\det(tI_n-A)=\sum_{i=0}^n c_it^i with c_n=1. If A is invertible, then A^{-1}=(-1)^{n-1}(\det A)^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^n c_i A^{i-1}.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Matrices, determinants, kernels, linear independence and bases are standard undergraduate linear algebra. Over an algebraically closed field, the extra condition simply guarantees polynomial roots; infinite dimensions mean cardinalities of bases. The specific matrix or dimension claim adds no specialist construction.

Proof 1: An inverse matrix as a characteristic-coefficient polynomial

For a general {\displaystyle n\times n} invertible matrix A, A−1 can be written as an {\displaystyle (n-1)} order polynomial expression in A. The Cayley–Hamilton theorem amounts to the identity

{\displaystyle p(A)=A^{n}+c_{n-1}A^{n-1}+\cdots +c_{1}A+(-1)^{n}\det(A)I_{n}=0.}

The Cayley–Hamilton identity can be written as

{\displaystyle -(-1)^{n}\det(A)I_{n}=A(A^{n-1}+c_{n-1}A^{n-2}+\cdots +c_{1}I_{n}),}

and, by multiplying both sides by A^{-1} and dividing by the unit \det A (note -(-1)^n=(-1)^{n-1}), one is led to an expression for the inverse of A,

A^{-1}=\frac{(-1)^{n-1}}{\det A}\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_iA^{i-1}.

The characteristic-coefficient polynomial in the factorization is the finite sum \sum_{i=1}^n c_iA^{i-1}, also when n=1.

Level justification

Factor out A in Cayley–Hamilton and divide by the determinant unit. Undergraduate commutative rings and matrix algebra suffice.
