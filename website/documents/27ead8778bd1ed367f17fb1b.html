Statement

Every finite group G of order n is isomorphic to a subgroup of S_n, the permutations of n labels.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Introductory group and ring theory supplies subgroups, quotients, commutators and homomorphisms. Any added terms such as perfect, nilpotent or virtually free describe explicit subgroup conditions; they are small algebraic extensions without representation or geometric-group theory.

Proof 1: Label the underlying set in Cayley’s representation — outline

If g is any element of a group G with operation ∗, consider the function fg : G → G, defined by fg(x) = g ∗ x. By the existence of inverses, this function has also an inverse, {\displaystyle f_{g^{-1}}}. So multiplication by g acts as a bijective function. Thus, fg is a permutation of G, and so is a member of Sym(G).

The set K = {fg : g ∈ G} is a subgroup of Sym(G) that is isomorphic to G. The fastest way to establish this is to consider the function T : G → Sym(G) with T(g) = fg for every g in G. T is a group homomorphism because (using · to denote composition in Sym(G)):

{\displaystyle (f_{g}\cdot f_{h})(x)=f_{g}(f_{h}(x))=f_{g}(h*x)=g*(h*x)=(g*h)*x=f_{g*h}(x),}

for all x in G, and hence:

{\displaystyle T(g)\cdot T(h)=f_{g}\cdot f_{h}=f_{g*h}=T(g*h).}

The homomorphism T is injective since T(g) = idG (the identity element of Sym(G)) implies that g ∗ x = x for all x in G, and taking x to be the identity element e of G yields g = g ∗ e = e, i.e. the kernel is trivial. Alternatively, T is also injective since g ∗ x = g′ ∗ x implies that g = g′ (because every group is cancellative).

Thus G is isomorphic to the image of T, which is the subgroup K.

If |G|=n, label its underlying elements by 1,\ldots,n. Its symmetric group becomes S_n, giving the stated finite-degree embedding.
