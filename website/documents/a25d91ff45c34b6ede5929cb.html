Statement

Let G be a finite group and \rho:G\to\mathrm{GL}_n(\mathbb C) an irreducible representation with character \chi. If g\in G and \chi(g)/n is a nonzero algebraic integer, then \rho(g) is a scalar matrix.

Proof 1: Algebraic norm and equality in the triangle inequality

Let {\displaystyle \zeta } be the complex number {\displaystyle \chi (g)/n}. By hypothesis it is a nonzero algebraic integer, so its norm {\displaystyle N(\zeta )} (i.e. the product of its conjugates, that is the roots of its minimal polynomial over {\displaystyle \mathbb {Q} }) is a nonzero integer. Now {\displaystyle \zeta } is the average of roots of unity (the eigenvalues of {\displaystyle \rho (g)}), hence so are its conjugates, so they all have an absolute value less than or equal to 1. Because the absolute value of their product {\displaystyle N(\zeta )} is greater than or equal to 1, their absolute value must all be 1, in particular {\displaystyle \zeta }, which means that the eigenvalues of {\displaystyle \rho (g)} are all equal, so, since a finite-order complex matrix is diagonalizable, {\displaystyle \rho (g)} is a homothety (a scalar matrix).

Level justification

Combine finite-group character eigenvalues, algebraic-integer norms and all algebraic conjugates, then use equality in the triangle inequality. The embedding/conjugate argument together with representation theory and algebraic integrality is a cumulative prerequisite burden; retain beyond undergraduate conservatively.
