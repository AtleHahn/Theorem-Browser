Statement

Let k\ge1 be an integer and let f:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R have derivatives through order k-1 near a, with f^{(k-1)} differentiable at a. Put P_k(x)=\sum_{j=0}^k f^{(j)}(a)(x-a)^j/j!. There is a function h_k with h_k(a)=0 and h_k(x)\to0 as x\to a, such that f(x)=P_k(x)+(x-a)^kh_k(x).

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Understanding the statement involves higher derivatives at a point; Taylor polynomials; vanishing normalized remainder. These prerequisites fit undergraduate coursework or a brief extension of it. A bright US college student should be able to understand this statement after approximately 30 minutes of focused background research; this assesses understanding the claim, not its proof.

Proof 1: Repeated l’Hôpital reduction to a derivative

Let

{\displaystyle h_{k}(x)={\begin{cases}{\frac {f(x)-P_k(x)}{(x-a)^{k}}}&x\not =a\\0&x=a\end{cases}}}

where, as in the statement of Taylor's theorem,

{\displaystyle P_k(x)=f(a)+f'(a)(x-a)+{\frac {f''(a)}{2!}}(x-a)^{2}+\cdots +{\frac {f^{(k)}(a)}{k!}}(x-a)^{k}.}

It is sufficient to show that

{\displaystyle \lim _{x\to a}h_{k}(x)=0.}

For k=1 the derivative definition below suffices. For k\ge2, the proof is based on repeated application of L'Hôpital's rule. Note that, for each {\textstyle j=0,1,...,k-1}, {\displaystyle f^{(j)}(a)=P_k^{(j)}(a)}. Hence each of the first {\textstyle k-1} derivatives of the numerator in {\displaystyle h_{k}(x)} vanishes at {\displaystyle x=a}, and the same is true of the denominator. Also, since the condition that the function {\textstyle f} be {\textstyle k} times differentiable at a point requires differentiability up to order {\textstyle k-1} in a neighborhood of said point (this is true, because differentiability requires a function to be defined in a whole neighborhood of a point), the numerator and its {\textstyle k-2} derivatives are differentiable in a neighborhood of {\textstyle a}. Clearly, the denominator also satisfies said condition, and additionally, doesn't vanish unless {\textstyle x=a}, therefore all conditions necessary for L'Hôpital's rule are fulfilled, and its use is justified. So

{\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}\lim _{x\to a}{\frac {f(x)-P_k(x)}{(x-a)^{k}}}&=\lim _{x\to a}{\frac {{\frac {d}{dx}}(f(x)-P_k(x))}{{\frac {d}{dx}}(x-a)^{k}}}\\[1ex]&=\cdots \\[1ex]&=\lim _{x\to a}{\frac {{\frac {d^{k-1}}{dx^{k-1}}}(f(x)-P_k(x))}{{\frac {d^{k-1}}{dx^{k-1}}}(x-a)^{k}}}\\[1ex]&={\frac {1}{k!}}\lim _{x\to a}{\frac {f^{(k-1)}(x)-P_k^{(k-1)}(x)}{x-a}}\\[1ex]&={\frac {1}{k!}}(f^{(k)}(a)-P_k^{(k)}(a))=0\end{aligned}}}

where the second-to-last equality follows by the definition of the derivative at {\textstyle x=a}.
