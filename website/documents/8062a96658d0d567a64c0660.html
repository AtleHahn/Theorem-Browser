Statement

Let a<b, \nu\geq0 integer, f\in C^{\nu+1}[a,b], and let L be a linear functional on a function space containing f and all polynomials of degree at most ν, which L annihilates. Set R(x)=\int_a^x(x-\theta)^\nu f^{(\nu+1)}(\theta)\,d\theta. Then Lf=L(R)/\nu!.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Taylor derivatives, integrals and polynomials are standard calculus; linear functionals are scalar-valued linear maps from linear algebra. The annihilation condition explicitly means vanishing on the given polynomials. The remainder identity introduces no further theory.

Proof 1: Apply polynomial annihilation to Taylor’s remainder

Use the Taylor polynomial for f with integral remainder:

{\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}f(x)=f(a)+{}&(x-a)f'(a)+{\frac {(x-a)^{2}}{2}}f''(a)+\cdots \\[6pt]&\cdots +{\frac {(x-a)^{\nu }}{\nu !}}f^{(\nu )}(a)+{\frac {1}{\nu !}}\int _{a}^{x}(x-\theta )^{\nu }f^{(\nu +1)}(\theta )\,d\theta ,\end{aligned}}}

Here the polynomial terms stop at degree \nu (so only f(a) occurs when \nu=0).

Using the linearity of L together with its annihilation of polynomials of degree at most \nu annihilates all but the final term on the right-hand side. Thus Lf=L(R)/\nu!. The remainder R belongs to the domain of L because it is \nu! times the difference of f and the displayed polynomial.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Apply Taylor’s integral-remainder formula and use linearity to remove the polynomial part. A linear functional here is simply a linear map from a vector space of functions to scalars; no continuity, dual topology or functional-analysis theorem is needed. The remainder belongs to the domain because it is a scalar multiple of f minus the Taylor polynomial. Undergraduate calculus and linear algebra fully cover the argument.
