Statement

Every finite division ring is commutative, and hence is a field.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Introductory ring/group theory and linear algebra supply polynomial coefficients, ideals, determinants, bases and matrix products. Elementary transvections are explicitly given elementary row-operation matrices, and division ring means every nonzero element has an inverse. These direct definitions suffice without specialist theory.

Proof 1: Witt’s class equation and cyclotomic estimate

Let A be a finite division ring. Since the center {\displaystyle Z(A)} of {\displaystyle A} is a field, {\displaystyle A} is a vector space over {\displaystyle Z(A)} with finite dimension {\displaystyle n}. Our objective is then to show {\displaystyle n=1}. If {\displaystyle q} is the order of {\displaystyle Z(A)}, then {\displaystyle A} has order {\displaystyle {q}^{n}}. Note that because {\displaystyle Z(A)} contains the distinct elements {\displaystyle 0} and {\displaystyle 1}, {\displaystyle q>1}. For each {\displaystyle b} in {\displaystyle A} that is not in the center, the centralizer {\displaystyle {Z}_{b}} of {\displaystyle b} is a vector space over {\displaystyle Z(A)}, hence it has order {\displaystyle {q}^{d}} where {\displaystyle d} is less than {\displaystyle n}. Viewing {\displaystyle {Z(A)}^{*}}, {\displaystyle A^{*}}, and {\displaystyle {Z}_{b}^{*}} as groups under multiplication, we can write the class equation

{\displaystyle q^{n}-1=q-1+\sum {q^{n}-1 \over q^{d}-1}}

where the sum is taken over the conjugacy classes not contained within {\displaystyle {Z(A)}^{*}}, and the {\displaystyle d} are defined so that for each conjugacy class, the order of {\displaystyle {Z}_{b}^{*}} for any {\displaystyle x} in the class is {\displaystyle {q}^{d}-1}. In particular, the fact that {\displaystyle {Z}_{b}^{*}} is a subgroup of {\displaystyle A^{*}} implies that {\displaystyle q^{d}-1} divides {\displaystyle q^{n}-1}, whence {\displaystyle d} divides {\displaystyle n} by elementary algebra.

{\displaystyle {q}^{n}-1} and {\displaystyle q^{d}-1} both admit polynomial factorization in terms of cyclotomic polynomials {\displaystyle \Phi _{f}(q)}. The cyclotomic polynomials on {\displaystyle \mathbb {Q} } are in {\displaystyle \mathbb {Z} [x]}, and satisfy the identities

{\displaystyle x^{n}-1=\prod _{m\mid n}\Phi _{m}(x)} and {\displaystyle x^{d}-1=\prod _{m\mid d}\Phi _{m}(x)}.

Since each {\displaystyle d} is a proper divisor of {\displaystyle n},

{\displaystyle \Phi _{n}(x)} divides both {\displaystyle {x}^{n}-1} and each {\displaystyle {x^{n}-1 \over x^{d}-1}} in {\displaystyle \mathbb {Z} [x]},

thus by the class equation above, {\displaystyle \Phi _{n}(q)} must divide {\displaystyle q-1}, and therefore by taking the norms,

{\displaystyle |\Phi _{n}(q)|\leq q-1}.

To see that this forces {\displaystyle n} to be {\displaystyle 1}, we will show

{\displaystyle |\Phi _{n}(q)|>q-1}

for {\displaystyle n>1} using factorization over the complex numbers. In the polynomial identity

{\displaystyle \Phi _{n}(x)=\prod (x-\zeta ),}

where {\displaystyle \zeta } runs over the primitive {\displaystyle n}-th roots of unity, set {\displaystyle x} to be {\displaystyle q} and then take absolute values

{\displaystyle |\Phi _{n}(q)|=\prod |q-\zeta |.}

For {\displaystyle n>1}, we see that for each primitive {\displaystyle n}-th root of unity {\displaystyle \zeta },

{\displaystyle |q-\zeta |>|q-1|}

because of the location of {\displaystyle q}, {\displaystyle 1}, and {\displaystyle \zeta } in the complex plane. Since {\displaystyle q\geq 2}, the term {\displaystyle q-1} is at least {\displaystyle 1}. Thus

{\displaystyle |\Phi _{n}(q)|>q-1.}

Thus n=1 and A=Z(A) is a field.

Proof 2: Conjugate maximal subfields (outline)

Let {\displaystyle D} be a finite division algebra with center {\displaystyle k}. Let {\displaystyle [D:k]=n^{2}} and {\displaystyle q} denote the cardinality of {\displaystyle k}. Every maximal subfield of {\displaystyle D} has {\displaystyle q^{n}} elements; so they are isomorphic and thus are conjugate by Skolem–Noether. But a finite group (the multiplicative group of {\displaystyle D} in our case) cannot be a union of conjugates of a proper subgroup; hence, {\displaystyle n=1}.

Here every element is contained in a maximal subfield, and the maximal-subfield dimension theorem and uniqueness of finite fields of a given cardinality are prerequisites.
