Simpler proof assessment

Likely alternative prerequisite level: Undergraduate level; lowest recorded proof level: Beyond undergraduate level. Integration by parts identifies derivative coefficients. Finite orthogonality gives Bessel's inequality for s', and finite Cauchy-Schwarz followed by monotone bounds proves absolute summability of the coefficients of s. The resulting uniformly convergent series has the same coefficients as s; Fejer kernels give uniqueness for continuous periodic functions by an elementary approximate-identity estimate. Rescale the period P and use the uniform absolute tail. No L2 completeness or mean-convergence theory is needed.

Statement

Let P>0 and let s\in C^1(\mathbb R) be complex-valued and P-periodic. Define S[n]=P^{-1}\int_0^P s(x)e^{-2\pi inx/P}\,dx and s_N(x)=\sum_{|n|\le N}S[n]e^{2\pi inx/P}. Then \sum_{n\in\mathbb Z}|S[n]|<\infty and s_N\to s uniformly on \mathbb R.

Proof 1: Square-sum derivative coefficients and bound the absolute tail — outline

If {\displaystyle s} is continuously differentiable, then {\displaystyle (2\pi in/P)S[n]} is the {\displaystyle n}th Fourier coefficient of the first derivative {\displaystyle s'}. Since {\displaystyle s'} is continuous, and therefore bounded, it is square-integrable and its Fourier coefficients are square-summable. Then, by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality,

{\displaystyle {\biggl (}\sum _{n\neq 0}{\bigl |}S[n]{\bigr |}{\biggr )}^{2}\leq \sum _{n\neq 0}{\frac {1}{n^{2}}}\cdot \sum _{n\neq 0}{\bigl |}nS[n]{\bigr |}^{2}.}

This means that the Fourier coefficients of {\displaystyle s} are absolutely summable. The sum of this series is a continuous function, equal to {\displaystyle s}, since the Fourier series converges in {\displaystyle L^{1}} to {\displaystyle s}.

In the absolutely summable case, the inequality:

{\displaystyle \sup _{x}{\bigl |}s(x)-s_{N}(x){\bigr |}\leq \sum _{|n|>N}{\bigl |}S[n]{\bigr |}}

proves uniform convergence.
