Statement

For real \alpha with |\alpha|\ne1, \int_0^\pi\log(1-2\alpha\cos x+\alpha^2)\,dx=\begin{cases}0,&|\alpha|<1,\\2\pi\log|\alpha|,&|\alpha|>1.\end{cases}

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Understanding the statement involves Real logarithms; Parameterized definite integrals. These prerequisites fit undergraduate coursework or a brief extension of it. A bright US college student should be able to understand this statement after approximately 30 minutes of focused background research; this assesses understanding the claim, not its proof.

Proof 1: Differentiate, use the half-angle substitution and invert the parameter

Consider

{\displaystyle \varphi (\alpha )=\int _{0}^{\pi }\ln \left(1-2\alpha \cos(x)+\alpha ^{2}\right)\,dx,\qquad |\alpha |\neq 1.}

Differentiation is valid locally away from \alpha=\pm1, where the logarithm argument is positive and bounded away from zero on the integration interval. For \alpha\ne0,\pm1, its derivative is given by

{\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}{\frac {d}{d\alpha }}\varphi (\alpha )&=\int _{0}^{\pi }{\frac {-2\cos(x)+2\alpha }{1-2\alpha \cos(x)+\alpha ^{2}}}dx\\[6pt]&={\frac {1}{\alpha }}\int _{0}^{\pi }\left(1-{\frac {1-\alpha ^{2}}{1-2\alpha \cos(x)+\alpha ^{2}}}\right)dx\\[6pt]&={\frac {\pi }{\alpha }}-{\frac {2}{\alpha }}\left.\left\{\arctan \left({\frac {1+\alpha }{1-\alpha }}\tan \left({\frac {x}{2}}\right)\right)\right\}\right|_{0}^{\pi }.\end{aligned}}}

Endpoint values of the antiderivative are one-sided limits from within (0,\pi). As {\displaystyle x} varies from {\displaystyle 0} to {\displaystyle \pi }, we have

{\displaystyle {\begin{cases}{\frac {1+\alpha }{1-\alpha }}\tan \left({\frac {x}{2}}\right)\geq 0,&|\alpha |<1,\\{\frac {1+\alpha }{1-\alpha }}\tan \left({\frac {x}{2}}\right)\leq 0,&|\alpha |>1.\end{cases}}}

Hence,

{\displaystyle \left.\arctan \left({\frac {1+\alpha }{1-\alpha }}\tan \left({\frac {x}{2}}\right)\right)\right|_{0}^{\pi }={\begin{cases}{\frac {\pi }{2}},&|\alpha |<1,\\-{\frac {\pi }{2}},&|\alpha |>1.\end{cases}}}

Therefore,

{\displaystyle {\frac {d}{d\alpha }}\varphi (\alpha )={\begin{cases}0,&|\alpha |<1,\\{\frac {2\pi }{\alpha }},&|\alpha |>1.\end{cases}}}

Continuity at \alpha=0 joins the constants on (-1,0) and (0,1). The constant C_2 below may initially be different on the two outer intervals. Integrating both sides with respect to {\displaystyle \alpha }, we get:

{\displaystyle \varphi (\alpha )={\begin{cases}C_{1},&|\alpha |<1,\\2\pi \ln |\alpha |+C_{2},&|\alpha |>1.\end{cases}}}

{\displaystyle C_{1}=0} follows from evaluating {\displaystyle \varphi (0)}:

{\displaystyle \varphi (0)=\int _{0}^{\pi }\ln(1)\,dx=\int _{0}^{\pi }0\,dx=0.}

To determine {\displaystyle C_{2}} in the same manner, we should need to substitute in a value of {\displaystyle \alpha } greater than 1 in {\displaystyle \varphi (\alpha )}. This is somewhat inconvenient. Instead, we substitute {\textstyle \alpha ={\frac {1}{\beta }}}, where {\displaystyle 0<|\beta |<1}. Then,

{\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}\varphi (\alpha )&=\int _{0}^{\pi }\left(\ln \left(1-2\beta \cos(x)+\beta ^{2}\right)-2\ln |\beta |\right)dx\\[6pt]&=\int _{0}^{\pi }\ln \left(1-2\beta \cos(x)+\beta ^{2}\right)\,dx-\int _{0}^{\pi }2\ln |\beta |dx\\[6pt]&=0-2\pi \ln |\beta |\\[6pt]&=2\pi \ln |\alpha |.\end{aligned}}}

Therefore, {\displaystyle C_{2}=0}

The calculation of {\displaystyle \varphi (\alpha )} is now complete:

{\displaystyle \varphi (\alpha )={\begin{cases}0,&|\alpha |<1,\\2\pi \ln |\alpha |,&|\alpha |>1.\end{cases}}}

The foregoing discussion, of course, does not apply when {\displaystyle \alpha =\pm 1}, since the conditions for differentiability are not met.
