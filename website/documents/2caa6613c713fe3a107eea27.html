Simpler proof assessment

Likely alternative prerequisite level: High school only; lowest recorded proof level: Undergraduate level. Set B=(0,0), A=a, C=c noncollinear and r=|X|. Fix the signed integer differences u=|X-a|-r and v=|X-c|-r, of bounded magnitude. Subtracting squared distances gives two linear equations, hence X=P-rQ. Substitution into |X|^2=r^2 gives a quadratic. It cannot vanish identically: that would require P=0, |Q|=1 and equality in both distance-direction bounds, forcing a,c collinear. Each of finitely many (u,v) therefore yields at most two points. Elementary coordinate elimination proves finiteness and hence collinearity without Bezout for curves.

Statement

If an infinite set S of points in the Euclidean plane has integer distance between every pair of points, then all points of S lie on a line.

Level
High school only
Level justification

The claim concerns elementary counting, integer distances in the plane or comparison of nonnegative numbers with their squares. The definitions are explicit and no advanced structural concept is needed to understand it. This rating concerns understanding the statement, independently of the difficulty of its proof.

Proof 1: Use the finite conic-intersection bound — outline

The proof assumes a given set of points with integer distances, not all on a line. It then proves that this set must be finite, using a system of curves for which each point of the given set lies on a crossing of two of the curves. In more detail, it consists of the following steps:

  • Arbitrarily choose a triangle {\displaystyle ABC} formed by three of the given points.

  • Let {\displaystyle d} denote the Euclidean distance function. For any given point {\displaystyle X}, the integer {\displaystyle |d(A,X)-d(B,X)|} is at most {\displaystyle d(A,B)}, by the triangle inequality. Thus, {\displaystyle X} lies on one of {\displaystyle d(A,B)} hyperbolas or degenerate hyperbolas, defined by equations of the form

    {\displaystyle |d(A,X)-d(B,X)|=i}

    with {\displaystyle 0\leq i<d(A,B)}. For i=0 this is the perpendicular bisector of AB. For {\displaystyle i=d(A,B)} this equation instead defines two rays extending in opposite directions on the line through {\displaystyle A} and {\displaystyle B} ; this pair of rays can be treated as a degenerate hyperbola for the purposes of the proof.

  • By a symmetric argument, {\displaystyle X} must also lie on one of {\displaystyle d(B,C)+1} hyperbolas or degenerate hyperbolas defined by equations of the form

    {\displaystyle |d(B,X)-d(C,X)|=j}

    with {\displaystyle 0\leq j\leq d(B,C)}.

  • The loci from the two families containing {\displaystyle X} cannot coincide, because the nondegenerate hyperbolas have different pairs of foci, and the degenerate loci have distinct supporting lines since A,B,C are noncollinear. Thus, {\displaystyle X} must be a point where they intersect. Each pair of loci from the two families has at most four intersection points, by Bézout's theorem.

  • Because each given point must be one of these intersection points, the number of given points is at most the product of the number of pairs of hyperbolas and the number of intersections per pair. This is at most

    {\displaystyle 4{\bigl (}d(A,B)+1{\bigr )}{\bigl (}d(B,C)+1{\bigr )}}

    points, a finite number.
