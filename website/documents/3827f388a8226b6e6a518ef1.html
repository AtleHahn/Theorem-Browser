Statement

Let G be a finite group, p a prime dividing |G|, and write |G|=p^n m with p\nmid m. A p-subgroup is a subgroup of order a power of p (including p^0=1), and a Sylow p-subgroup is one maximal among such subgroups.

  1. There exists a subgroup of order p^k for every 1\le k\le n. The Sylow p-subgroups are exactly those of order p^n.
  2. Every p-subgroup H is conjugate into every Sylow p-subgroup P: some g\in G satisfies g^{-1}Hg\le P. In particular all Sylow p-subgroups are conjugate.
  3. Their number n_p satisfies n_p=[G:N_G(P)], n_p\mid m and n_p\equiv1\pmod p, where N_G(P)=\{g\in G:gPg^{-1}=P\} and [G:N_G(P)] is the subgroup index.

Proof 1: Subset orbits and a binomial valuation

Let |G|=p^km=p^{k+r}u such that p\nmid u, and let \Omega denote the set of subsets of G of size p^k. G acts on \Omega by left multiplication: for g\in G and \omega\in\Omega, g\cdot\omega=\{gx:x\in\omega\}. For a given set \omega\in\Omega, write G_\omega for its stabilizer subgroup \{g\in G:g\cdot\omega=\omega\} and G\omega for its orbit \{g\cdot\omega:g\in G\} in \Omega.

The proof will show the existence of some \omega\in\Omega for which G_\omega has p^k elements, providing the desired subgroup. This is the maximal possible size of a stabilizer subgroup G_\omega, since for any fixed element \alpha\in\omega\subseteq G, the right coset G_\omega\alpha is contained in \omega; therefore,

|G_\omega|=|G_\omega\alpha|\le|\omega|=p^k.

By the orbit–stabilizer theorem we have |G_\omega|\,|G\omega|=|G| for each \omega\in\Omega, and therefore, using the additive p-adic valuation \nu_p, which counts the number of factors p, one has

\nu_p(|G_\omega|)+\nu_p(|G\omega|)=\nu_p(|G|)=k+r.

This means that for those \omega with |G_\omega|=p^k, the ones we are looking for, one has \nu_p(|G\omega|)=r, while for any other \omega one has \nu_p(|G\omega|)>r (as 0<|G_\omega|<p^k implies \nu_p(|G_\omega|)<k). Since |\Omega| is the sum of |G\omega| over all distinct orbits G\omega, one can show the existence of \omega of the former type by showing that \nu_p(|\Omega|)=r (if none existed, that valuation would exceed r).

This is an instance of Kummer's theorem: in base p the number |G| ends with precisely k+r zero digits, and subtracting p^k involves borrowing in r places. It can also be shown by a simple computation:

\begin{aligned} |\Omega|=\binom{p^km}{p^k} &=\prod_{j=0}^{p^k-1}\frac{p^km-j}{p^k-j}\\ &=m\prod_{j=1}^{p^k-1} \frac{p^{k-\nu_p(j)}m-j/p^{\nu_p(j)}} {p^{k-\nu_p(j)}-j/p^{\nu_p(j)}}. \end{aligned}

No power of p remains in any numerator or denominator inside the product on the right. Hence \nu_p(|\Omega|)=\nu_p(m)=r, completing the proof.

Conjugacy and containment

First, let H be a finite p-group, let \Omega be a finite set acted on by H, and let \Omega_0 denote the set of points of \Omega fixed under the action of H. Any element x\in\Omega not fixed by H will lie in an orbit of order |H|/|H_x| (where H_x denotes the stabilizer), which is a multiple of p. Writing |\Omega| as the sum of |Hx| over all distinct orbits Hx and reducing modulo p gives

|\Omega|\equiv|\Omega_0|\pmod p.

Choose P of order p^n, whose existence was established above. Now let \Omega be the set of left cosets of P in G and let H act on \Omega by left multiplication. Applying this fixed-point congruence to H on \Omega, we see that

|\Omega_0|\equiv|\Omega|=[G:P]\pmod p.

Now p\nmid[G:P] since |P|=p^n, so p\nmid|\Omega_0|, hence in particular |\Omega_0|\ne0, so there exists some gP\in\Omega_0. With this gP, we have hgP=gP for all h\in H, so g^{-1}HgP=P and therefore g^{-1}Hg\le P. If H is maximal among p-subgroups, its containment in gPg^{-1} forces equality, so g^{-1}Hg=P.

The containment result also shows that every maximal p-subgroup has the largest prime-power order: it is contained in a conjugate of the subgroup just constructed, and maximality forces equality.

Counting Sylow subgroups

Let \Omega be the set of all Sylow p-subgroups of G and let G act on \Omega by conjugation. Let P\in\Omega be a Sylow p-subgroup. By the Sylow conjugacy theorem, the orbit of P has size n_p, so by the orbit–stabilizer theorem n_p=[G:G_P]. For this group action, the stabilizer G_P is given by

\{g\in G:gPg^{-1}=P\}=N_G(P),

the normalizer of P in G. Thus n_p=[G:N_G(P)], and, since P\le N_G(P), it follows that this number is a divisor of [G:P]=m.

Now let P act on \Omega by conjugation, and let \Omega_0 denote the set of fixed points of this action. Let Q\in\Omega_0 and observe that then Q=xQx^{-1} for all x\in P, so that P\le N_G(Q). By the Sylow conjugacy theorem, P and Q are conjugate in N_G(Q) in particular, and Q is normal in N_G(Q), so then P=Q. It follows that \Omega_0=\{P\}.

For completeness, the fixed-point congruence used here follows by the source's orbit argument: for a finite p-group H acting on a finite set, an element x not fixed by H lies in an orbit of order |H|/|H_x|, a multiple of p. Writing the size of the set as the sum of the sizes of its distinct orbits and reducing modulo p gives its size congruent to the number of fixed points. Applied to the action of P, this gives

|\Omega|\equiv|\Omega_0|=1\pmod p.
