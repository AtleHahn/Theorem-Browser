Statement

Let k be a field and A=k[y_1^\prime,\ldots,y_m^\prime] a nonzero commutative unital finitely generated k-algebra. There are 0\le d\le m algebraically independent elements y_1,\ldots,y_d\in A such that A is a finitely generated module over k[y_1,\ldots,y_d].

Level
Beyond undergraduate level
Level justification

The statement uses polynomial subalgebras and finite ring extensions, beyond calculus and linear algebra.

Proof 1: Nagata’s weighted substitution and induction

We shall induct on m. Case {\displaystyle m=0} is {\displaystyle k=A} and there is nothing to prove. Assume {\displaystyle m=1}. Then {\displaystyle A\cong k[y]/I} as k-algebras, where {\displaystyle I\subset k[y]} is some ideal. Since {\displaystyle k[y]} is a PID (it is a Euclidean domain), {\displaystyle I=(f)}. If {\displaystyle f=0} we are done, so assume {\displaystyle f\neq 0}. Let e be the degree of f. Then A is generated, as a k-vector space, by {\displaystyle 1,y,y^{2},\dots ,y^{e-1}}. Thus A is finite over k. Assume now {\displaystyle m\geq 2}. If the {\displaystyle y_{i}'} are algebraically independent, then by setting {\displaystyle y_{i}=y_{i}'}, we are done. If not, it is enough to prove the claim that there is a k-subalgebra S of A that is generated by {\displaystyle m-1} elements, such that A is finite over S. Indeed, by the inductive hypothesis, we can find, for some integer d, {\displaystyle 0\leq d\leq m-1}, algebraically independent elements {\displaystyle y_{1},...,y_{d}} of S such that S is finite over {\displaystyle k[y_{1},...,y_{d}]}. Since A is finite over S, and S is finite over {\displaystyle k[y_{1},...,y_{d}]}, we obtain the desired conclusion that A is finite over {\displaystyle k[y_{1},...,y_{d}]}.

To prove the claim, we assume by hypothesis that the {\displaystyle y_{i}'} are not algebraically independent, so that there is a nonzero polynomial f in m variables over k such that

{\displaystyle f(y_{1}',\ldots ,y_{m}')=0}.

Given an integer r which is determined later, set

{\displaystyle z_{i}=y_{i}'-(y_{1}')^{r^{i-1}},\quad 2\leq i\leq m,}

and, for simplification of notation, write {\displaystyle {\tilde {y}}=y_{1}'.}

Then the preceding reads:

{\displaystyle f({\tilde {y}},z_{2}+{\tilde {y}}^{r},z_{3}+{\tilde {y}}^{r^{2}},\ldots ,z_{m}+{\tilde {y}}^{r^{m-1}})=0.\ \ \ (*)}

Now, if {\displaystyle a{\tilde {y}}^{\alpha _{1}}\prod _{2}^{m}(z_{i}+{\tilde {y}}^{r^{i-1}})^{\alpha _{i}}} is a monomial appearing in the left-hand side of the displayed dependence equation, with coefficient {\displaystyle a\in k}, the highest term in {\displaystyle {\tilde {y}}} after expanding the product looks like

{\displaystyle a{\tilde {y}}^{\alpha _{1}+\alpha _{2}r+\cdots +\alpha _{m}r^{m-1}}}.

Whenever the above exponent agrees with the highest {\displaystyle {\tilde {y}}} exponent produced by some other monomial, it is possible that the highest term in {\displaystyle {\tilde {y}}} of {\displaystyle f({\tilde {y}},z_{2}+{\tilde {y}}^{r},z_{3}+{\tilde {y}}^{r^{2}},...,z_{m}+{\tilde {y}}^{r^{m-1}})} will not be of the displayed pure-power form, because it may be affected by cancellation. However, if r is large enough (e.g., we can set {\displaystyle r=1+\deg f}), then each {\displaystyle \alpha _{1}+\alpha _{2}r+\cdots +\alpha _{m}r^{m-1}} encodes a unique base r number, so this does not occur. For such an r, let {\displaystyle c\in k} be the coefficient of the unique monomial of f of multidegree {\displaystyle (\alpha _{1},\dots ,\alpha _{m})} for which the quantity {\displaystyle \alpha _{1}+\alpha _{2}r+\cdots +\alpha _{m}r^{m-1}} is maximal. Multiplication of {\displaystyle (*)} by {\displaystyle 1/c} gives an integral dependence equation of {\displaystyle {\tilde {y}}} over {\displaystyle S=k[z_{2},...,z_{m}]}, i.e., {\displaystyle y_{1}'(={\tilde {y}})} is integral over S. Moreover, because {\displaystyle A=S[y_{1}']}, A is in fact finite over S. This proves the finite-extension claim and completes the induction.
