Statement

A finite simple undirected graph consists of finitely many vertices and edges joining distinct unordered pairs, with no repeated edge. Neighbors are vertices joined by an edge; a vertex’s degree is its number of neighbors. Connected means that every two vertices are joined by a path. A proper coloring assigns colors to vertices so that neighbors have different colors. Let G be nonempty and connected with maximum degree \Delta. Unless G is complete (every two distinct vertices adjacent) or a cycle of odd length, it has a proper coloring with at most \Delta colors. For a complete graph or an odd cycle, the minimum number of colors is \Delta+1. A cycle has vertices in a cyclic order and exactly the edges between consecutive vertices.

Proof 1: Lovász tree reduction and greedy coloring — outline

If the graph is not biconnected, its biconnected components may be colored separately and then the colorings combined. Here a biconnected graph is connected and has no vertex whose deletion disconnects it; the standard block decomposition includes bridges as blocks. Colorings with the same palette combine by permuting color names at shared cut vertices.

Greedy coloring with a deficient vertex

If the graph has a vertex v with degree less than Δ, then a greedy coloring algorithm that colors vertices farther from v before closer ones uses at most Δ colors. This is because at the time that each vertex other than v is colored, at least one of its neighbors (the one on a shortest path to v) is uncolored, so it has fewer than Δ colored neighbors and has a free color. When the algorithm reaches v, its small number of neighbors allows it to be colored.

Reduction to a biconnected regular graph

If G has a cut vertex, every block contains a cut vertex with fewer than \Delta neighbors within that block, so the preceding greedy argument applies to each block. Connected graphs of maximum degree at most two are single vertices, paths or cycles; their color counts give the claimed cases. A complete graph requires a different color at each vertex.

Therefore, the most difficult case of the proof concerns biconnected Δ-regular graphs with \Delta\geq3 which are not complete; regular means that every degree equals \Delta.

The Lovász spanning-tree prerequisite and coloring

In this case, Lovász shows that one can find a spanning tree such that two nonadjacent neighbors u and w of the root v are leaves in the tree. A spanning tree contains all vertices and is connected and acyclic; each nonroot vertex has its parent one step nearer the root, and a leaf has no children. Give u and w the same color. A greedy coloring starting from u and w and processing the remaining vertices of the spanning tree in bottom-up order, ending at v, uses at most Δ colors. For, when every vertex other than v is colored, it has an uncolored parent, so its already-colored neighbors cannot use up all the free colors, while at v the two neighbors u and w have equal colors so again a free color remains for v itself.
