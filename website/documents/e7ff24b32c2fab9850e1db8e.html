Statement

For spherical right triangles on spheres of radius R\to\infty, keep the positive geodesic legs a,b fixed and let c=c(R) be the shortest opposite geodesic side. Then c(R)^2=a^2+b^2+O(R^{-2}), so c(R)\to\sqrt{a^2+b^2}.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Spherical triangles and quadrilaterals use great-circle arcs and ordinary area on a sphere. Antipodes and small circles are explicit Euclidean spatial constructions; the limiting formula adds familiar asymptotic notation. No arbitrary Riemannian metric is involved.

Proof 1: Expand the three cosines with a uniform Taylor remainder — outline

For infinitesimal triangles on the sphere (or equivalently, for finite spherical triangles on a sphere of infinite radius), the spherical relation between the sides of a right triangle reduces to the Euclidean form of the Pythagorean theorem. To see how, assume we have a spherical right triangle of fixed geodesic legs a,b and shortest opposite side c=c(R) on a sphere with expanding radius R. The triangle inequality gives c(R)\le a+b, so the following remainder estimates are uniform as R varies. As R approaches infinity the quantities a/R, b/R, and c/R tend to zero and the spherical Pythagorean identity reduces to 1 = 1, so we must look at its asymptotic expansion.

The Maclaurin series for the cosine function can be written as {\textstyle \cos x=1-{\tfrac {1}{2}}x^{2}+O{\left(x^{4}\right)}} with the remainder term in big O notation. Letting {\displaystyle x=c/R} be a side of the triangle, and treating the expression as an asymptotic expansion in terms of R for bounded c=c(R),

{\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}\cos {\frac {c}{R}}=1-{\frac {c^{2}}{2R^{2}}}+O{\left(R^{-4}\right)}\end{aligned}}}

and likewise for a and b. Substituting the asymptotic expansion for each of the cosines into the spherical relation for a right triangle yields

{\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}1-{\frac {c^{2}}{2R^{2}}}+O{\left(R^{-4}\right)}&=\left(1-{\frac {a^{2}}{2R^{2}}}+O{\left(R^{-4}\right)}\right)\left(1-{\frac {b^{2}}{2R^{2}}}+O{\left(R^{-4}\right)}\right)\\&=1-{\frac {a^{2}}{2R^{2}}}-{\frac {b^{2}}{2R^{2}}}+O{\left(R^{-4}\right)}.\end{aligned}}}

Subtracting 1 and then negating each side,

{\displaystyle {\frac {c^{2}}{2R^{2}}}={\frac {a^{2}}{2R^{2}}}+{\frac {b^{2}}{2R^{2}}}+O{\left(R^{-4}\right)}.}

Multiplying through by 2R2, the asymptotic expansion for c in terms of fixed a, b and variable R is

{\displaystyle c^{2}=a^{2}+b^{2}+O{\left(R^{-2}\right)}.}

The Euclidean Pythagorean relationship {\textstyle c^{2}=a^{2}+b^{2}} is recovered in the limit, as the remainder vanishes when the radius R approaches infinity.
