Statement

A Gaussian integer is a+bi with a,b\in\mathbb Z, with norm N(a+bi)=a^2+b^2. Its units are 1,-1,i,-i; call a nonzero nonunit a Gaussian prime if it cannot be factored into two nonunits. A positive rational prime p is Gaussian prime exactly when p\equiv3\pmod4. If p\equiv1\pmod4 or p=2, it is the norm of a Gaussian prime.

Level
High school only
Level justification

Gaussian integers, their norm and the meaning of Gaussian prime are explicitly defined using integer pairs and complex multiplication. The asserted prime classification needs elementary arithmetic to understand. This rating concerns understanding the statement, independently of the difficulty of its proof.

Proof 1: Norm dichotomy and Fermat’s theorem

If the rational prime p is not a Gaussian prime, write p=\alpha\beta with two nonunits. Their positive integer norms exceed one and multiply to p^2, so both equal p. A Gaussian integer of norm p cannot split into two nonunits, again by norm multiplicativity. Thus p is the norm of a Gaussian prime. For odd p, Fermat’s theorem and the obstruction modulo four distinguish the cases: a representation p=a^2+b^2 gives the nonunit factorization (a+bi)(a-bi), and the norm is p. Finally 2=1^2+1^2=N(1+i).
