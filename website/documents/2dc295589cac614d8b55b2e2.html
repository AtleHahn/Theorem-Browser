Statement

Let C be a nonempty closed convex subset of a real or complex Hilbert space H, and let d=\inf_{c\in C}\|c\|. Every sequence (c_n) in C with \|c_n\|\to d converges in norm to the same point m\in C, with \|m\|=d.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Basic topology and real analysis supply compactness, completeness, continuity and sequence convergence; linear algebra supplies inner products and orthogonality. Hilbert completeness and the explicitly specified bounds or maps are direct extensions, with no spectral or weak-topology theory needed.

Proof 1: The parallelogram estimate forces convergence

Let {\displaystyle C} be as described in this theorem and let

{\displaystyle d:=\inf _{c\in C}\|c\|.}

This theorem will follow from the following lemmas.

Lemma 1—If {\displaystyle c_{\bullet }:=\left(c_{n}\right)_{n=1}^{\infty }} is any sequence in {\displaystyle C} such that {\displaystyle \lim _{n\to \infty }\left\|c_{n}\right\|=d} in {\displaystyle \mathbb {R} } then there exists some {\displaystyle c\in C} such that {\displaystyle \lim _{n\to \infty }c_{n}=c} in {\displaystyle H.} Furthermore, {\displaystyle \|c\|=d.}

Proof. Because {\displaystyle C} is convex, if {\displaystyle m,n\in \mathbb {N} } then {\displaystyle {\frac {1}{2}}\left(c_{m}+c_{n}\right)\in C} so that by definition of the infimum, {\displaystyle d\leq \left\|{\frac {1}{2}}\left(c_{m}+c_{n}\right)\right\|,} which implies that {\displaystyle 4d^{2}\leq \left\|c_{m}+c_{n}\right\|^{2}.} By the parallelogram law,

{\displaystyle \left\|c_{m}+c_{n}\right\|^{2}+\left\|c_{m}-c_{n}\right\|^{2}=2\left\|c_{m}\right\|^{2}+2\left\|c_{n}\right\|^{2}}

where {\displaystyle 4d^{2}\leq \left\|c_{m}+c_{n}\right\|^{2}} now implies

{\displaystyle 4d^{2}+\left\|c_{m}-c_{n}\right\|^{2}~\leq ~2\left\|c_{m}\right\|^{2}+2\left\|c_{n}\right\|^{2}}

and so

{\displaystyle {\begin{alignedat}{4}\left\|c_{m}-c_{n}\right\|^{2}~\leq ~2\left\|c_{m}\right\|^{2}+2\left\|c_{n}\right\|^{2}-4d^{2}\end{alignedat}}}

The assumption {\displaystyle \lim _{n\to \infty }\left\|c_{n}\right\|=d} implies that the right hand side (RHS) of the above inequality can be made arbitrary close to {\displaystyle 0} by making {\displaystyle m} and {\displaystyle n} sufficiently large. The same must consequently also be true of the inequality's left hand side {\displaystyle \left\|c_{m}-c_{n}\right\|^{2}} and thus also of {\displaystyle \left\|c_{m}-c_{n}\right\|,} which proves that {\displaystyle \left(c_{n}\right)_{n=1}^{\infty }} is a Cauchy sequence in {\displaystyle H.} Since {\displaystyle H} is complete, there exists some {\displaystyle c\in H} such that {\displaystyle \lim _{n\to \infty }c_{n}=c} in {\displaystyle H.} Because every {\displaystyle c_{n}} belongs to {\displaystyle C,} which is a closed subset of {\displaystyle H,} their limit {\displaystyle c} must also belong to this closed subset, which proves that {\displaystyle c\in C.} Since the norm {\displaystyle \|\,\cdot \,\|:H\to \mathbb {R} } is a continuous function, {\displaystyle \lim _{n\to \infty }c_{n}=c} in {\displaystyle H} implies that {\displaystyle \lim _{n\to \infty }\left\|c_{n}\right\|=\|c\|} in {\displaystyle \mathbb {R} .} But {\displaystyle \lim _{n\to \infty }\left\|c_{n}\right\|=d} also holds (by assumption) so that {\displaystyle \|c\|=d} (because limits in {\displaystyle \mathbb {R} } are unique). {\displaystyle \blacksquare }

Lemma 2—A sequence {\displaystyle \left(c_{n}\right)_{n=1}^{\infty }} satisfying the hypotheses of Lemma 1 exists.

Proof. The existence of the sequence follows from the definition of the infimum, as is now shown. The set {\displaystyle S:=\{\|c\|:c\in C\}} is a non-empty subset of non-negative real numbers and {\displaystyle d:=\inf _{c\in C}\|c\|=\inf S.} Let {\displaystyle n\geq 1} be an integer. Because {\displaystyle \inf S<d+{\frac {1}{n}},} there exists some {\displaystyle s_{n}\in S} such that {\displaystyle s_{n}<d+{\frac {1}{n}}.} Since {\displaystyle s_{n}\in S,} {\displaystyle d=\inf S\leq s_{n}} holds (by definition of the infimum). Thus {\displaystyle d\leq s_{n}<d+{\frac {1}{n}}} and now the squeeze theorem implies that {\displaystyle \lim _{n\to \infty }s_{n}=d} in {\displaystyle \mathbb {R} .} (This first part of the proof works for any non-empty subset of {\displaystyle S\subseteq \mathbb {R} } for which {\displaystyle d:=\inf _{s\in S}s} is finite). For every {\displaystyle n\in \mathbb {N} ,} the fact that {\displaystyle s_{n}\in S=\{\|c\|:c\in C\}} means that there exists some {\displaystyle c_{n}\in C} such that {\displaystyle s_{n}=\left\|c_{n}\right\|.} The convergence {\displaystyle \lim _{n\to \infty }s_{n}=d} in {\displaystyle \mathbb {R} } thus becomes {\displaystyle \lim _{n\to \infty }\left\|c_{n}\right\|=d} in {\displaystyle \mathbb {R} .} {\displaystyle \blacksquare }

Lemma 2 and Lemma 1 together prove that there exists some {\displaystyle c\in C} such that {\displaystyle \|c\|=d.} Lemma 1 can be used to prove uniqueness as follows. Suppose {\displaystyle b\in C} is such that {\displaystyle \|b\|=d} and denote the sequence

{\displaystyle b,c,b,c,b,c,\ldots }

by {\displaystyle \left(c_{n}\right)_{n=1}^{\infty }} so that the subsequence {\displaystyle \left(c_{2n}\right)_{n=1}^{\infty }} of even indices is the constant sequence {\displaystyle c,c,c,\ldots } while the subsequence {\displaystyle \left(c_{2n-1}\right)_{n=1}^{\infty }} of odd indices is the constant sequence {\displaystyle b,b,b,\ldots .} Because {\displaystyle \left\|c_{n}\right\|=d} for every {\displaystyle n\in \mathbb {N} ,} {\displaystyle \lim _{n\to \infty }\left\|c_{n}\right\|=\lim _{n\to \infty }d=d} in {\displaystyle \mathbb {R} ,} which shows that the sequence {\displaystyle \left(c_{n}\right)_{n=1}^{\infty }} satisfies the hypotheses of Lemma 1. Lemma 1 guarantees the existence of some {\displaystyle x\in C} such that {\displaystyle \lim _{n\to \infty }c_{n}=x} in {\displaystyle H.} Because {\displaystyle \left(c_{n}\right)_{n=1}^{\infty }} converges to {\displaystyle x,} so do all of its subsequences. In particular, the subsequence {\displaystyle c,c,c,\ldots } converges to {\displaystyle x,} which implies that {\displaystyle x=c} (because limits in {\displaystyle H} are unique and this constant subsequence also converges to {\displaystyle c}). Similarly, {\displaystyle x=b} because the subsequence {\displaystyle b,b,b,\ldots } converges to both {\displaystyle x} and {\displaystyle b.} Thus {\displaystyle b=c,} which proves the theorem. {\displaystyle \blacksquare }

Level justification

The parallelogram identity makes any minimizing sequence Cauchy; completeness and closedness give its limit, and an alternating sequence gives uniqueness. Undergraduate metric completeness and inner-product algebra suffice; Hilbert space is only their combination, with no duality or weak topology.
