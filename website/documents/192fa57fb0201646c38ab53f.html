Statement

For every integer n\ge1 and bounded Lebesgue-measurable sets A_1,\ldots,A_n\subset\mathbb R^n, there is an affine hyperplane whose two open half-spaces each contain half the Lebesgue measure of each A_i.

Level justification

The statement uses Lebesgue measure on arbitrary bounded measurable subsets, beyond the undergraduate calculus baseline.

Proof 1: Apply Borsuk–Ulam to the remaining half-space volumes — outline

Let A1, A2, ..., An denote the n compact (or more generally bounded and Lebesgue-measurable) subsets of {\displaystyle \mathbb {R} ^{n}} that we wish to simultaneously bisect. Let {\displaystyle S=\{v=(v_{1},\ldots ,v_{n})\in \mathbb {R} ^{n}\colon v_{1}^{2}+\ldots +v_{n}^{2}=1\}} be the unit (n − 1)-sphere in {\displaystyle \mathbb {R} ^{n}}. For each point v on S, we can define a continuum {\displaystyle (E_{v,c})_{c\in \mathbb {R} }} of affine hyperplanes with normal vector v: {\displaystyle E_{v,c}=\{x\in \mathbb {R} ^{n}\colon x_{1}v_{1}+\ldots +x_{n}v_{n}=c\}} for {\displaystyle c\in \mathbb {R} }. For each {\displaystyle c\in \mathbb {R} }, we call the space {\displaystyle E_{v,c}^{+}=\{x\in \mathbb {R} ^{n}\colon x_{1}v_{1}+\ldots +x_{n}v_{n}>c\}} the "positive side" of {\displaystyle E_{v,c}}, which is the side pointed to by the vector v. By the intermediate value theorem, every family of such hyperplanes contains at least one hyperplane that bisects the bounded set An: at one extreme translation, no volume of An is on the positive side, and at the other extreme translation, all of An's volume is on the positive side, so in between there must be a closed interval Iv of possible values of {\displaystyle c\in \mathbb {R} }, for which {\displaystyle E_{v,c}} bisects the volume of An. If An has volume zero, we pick {\displaystyle c=0} for all {\displaystyle v\in S}. Otherwise, the interval Iv is compact and we can canonically pick {\displaystyle c={\frac {1}{2}}(\inf I_{v}+\sup I_{v})} as its midpoint for each {\displaystyle v\in S}. Thus we obtain a continuous function {\displaystyle \alpha \colon S\to \mathbb {R} } such that for each point v on the sphere S the hyperplane {\displaystyle E_{v,\alpha (v)}} bisects An. Note further that we have {\displaystyle I_{-v}=-I_{v}} and thus {\displaystyle \alpha (-v)=-\alpha (v)} for all {\displaystyle v\in S}.

Now we define a function {\displaystyle f\colon S\to \mathbb {R} ^{n-1}} as follows:

{\displaystyle f(v)=(vol(A_{1}\cap E_{v,\alpha (v)}^{+}),\ldots ,vol(A_{n-1}\cap E_{v,\alpha (v)}^{+}))}.

This function f is continuous (which can be proven with the dominated convergence theorem). By the Borsuk–Ulam theorem, there are antipodal points {\displaystyle v} and {\displaystyle -v} on the sphere S such that {\displaystyle f(v)=f(-v)}. Antipodal points correspond to hyperplanes {\displaystyle E_{v,\alpha (v)}} and {\displaystyle E_{-v,\alpha (-v)}=E_{-v,-\alpha (v)}} that are equal except that they have opposite positive sides. Thus, {\displaystyle f(v)=f(-v)} means that the volume of Ai is the same on the positive and negative side of {\displaystyle E_{v,\alpha (v)}}, for {\displaystyle i=1,\ldots ,n-1}. The construction already bisects A_n, and a hyperplane has Lebesgue measure zero. Thus, {\displaystyle E_{v,\alpha (v)}} is the desired ham sandwich cut that simultaneously bisects the volumes of A1, ..., An.

The source leaves the continuity check for the midpoint selection implicit.
