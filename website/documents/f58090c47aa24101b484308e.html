Statement

Let F be a field, n\ge1 an integer, A\in F^{n\times n} with \det A\ne0, and b\in F^n. The system Ax=b has a unique solution, with x_j=\det(A_j)/\det A for 1\le j\le n, where A_j replaces column j of A by b.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Matrices, determinants, kernels, linear independence and bases are standard undergraduate linear algebra. Over an algebraically closed field, the extra condition simply guarantees polynomial roots; infinite dimensions mean cardinalities of bases. The specific matrix or dimension claim adds no specialist construction.

Proof 1: Column determinant functionals and a left inverse

The proof for Cramer's rule uses the following properties of the determinants: linearity with respect to any given column and the fact that the determinant is zero whenever two columns are equal.

Fix the index j of a column, and consider that the entries of the other columns have fixed values. This makes the determinant a function of the entries of the jth column. Linearity with respect to this column means that this function has the form

{\displaystyle D_{j}(a_{1,j},\ldots ,a_{n,j})=C_{1,j}a_{1,j}+\cdots +C_{n,j}a_{n,j},}

where the {\displaystyle C_{i,j}} are coefficients that depend on the entries of A that are not in column j. So, one has

{\displaystyle \det(A)=D_{j}(a_{1,j},\ldots ,a_{n,j})=C_{1,j}a_{1,j}+\cdots +C_{n,j}a_{n,j}}

(Laplace expansion provides a formula for computing the {\displaystyle C_{i,j}} but their expression is not important here.)

If the function {\displaystyle D_{j}} is applied to any other column k of A, then the result is the determinant of the matrix obtained from A by replacing column j by a copy of column k, so the resulting determinant is 0 (the case of two equal columns).

Now consider a system of n linear equations in n unknowns {\displaystyle x_{1},\ldots ,x_{n}}, whose coefficient matrix is A, with det(A) assumed to be nonzero:

{\displaystyle {\begin{matrix}a_{11}x_{1}+a_{12}x_{2}+\cdots +a_{1n}x_{n}&=&b_{1}\\a_{21}x_{1}+a_{22}x_{2}+\cdots +a_{2n}x_{n}&=&b_{2}\\&\vdots &\\a_{n1}x_{1}+a_{n2}x_{2}+\cdots +a_{nn}x_{n}&=&b_{n}.\end{matrix}}}

If one combines these equations by taking C1,j times the first equation, plus C2,j times the second, and so forth until Cn,j times the last, then for every k the resulting coefficient of xk becomes

{\displaystyle D_{j}(a_{1,k},\ldots ,a_{n,k}).}

So, all coefficients become zero, except the coefficient of {\displaystyle x_{j}} that becomes {\displaystyle \det(A).} Similarly, the constant coefficient becomes {\displaystyle D_{j}(b_{1},\ldots ,b_{n}),} and the resulting equation is thus

{\displaystyle \det(A)x_{j}=D_{j}(b_{1},\ldots ,b_{n}),}

which gives the value of {\displaystyle x_{j}} as

{\displaystyle x_{j}={\frac {1}{\det(A)}}D_{j}(b_{1},\ldots ,b_{n}).}

As, by construction, the numerator is the determinant of the matrix obtained from A by replacing column j by b, we get the expression of Cramer's rule as a necessary condition for a solution.

It remains to prove that these values for the unknowns form a solution. Let M be the n × n matrix that has the coefficients of {\displaystyle D_{j}} as jth row, for {\displaystyle j=1,\ldots ,n} (this is the adjugate matrix for A). Expressed in matrix terms, we have thus to prove that

{\displaystyle \mathbf {x} ={\frac {1}{\det(A)}}M\mathbf {b} }

is a solution; that is, that

{\displaystyle A\left({\frac {1}{\det(A)}}M\right)\mathbf {b} =\mathbf {b} .}

For that, it suffices to prove that

{\displaystyle A\,\left({\frac {1}{\det(A)}}M\right)=I_{n},}

where {\displaystyle I_{n}} is the identity matrix.

The above properties of the functions {\displaystyle D_{j}} show that one has MA = det(A)In, and therefore,

{\displaystyle \left({\frac {1}{\det(A)}}M\right)\,A=I_{n}.}

This completes the proof, since a left inverse of a square matrix is also a right-inverse (see Invertible matrix theorem).

Proof 2: Determinant of an identity matrix with one replaced column

Since \det A\ne0, the square-matrix inverse theorem gives the unique solution x=A^{-1}b.

A short proof of Cramer's rule can be given by noticing that {\displaystyle x_{1}} is the determinant of the matrix

{\displaystyle X_{1}={\begin{bmatrix}x_{1}&0&0&\cdots &0\\x_{2}&1&0&\cdots &0\\x_{3}&0&1&\cdots &0\\\vdots &\vdots &\vdots &\ddots &\vdots \\x_{n}&0&0&\cdots &1\end{bmatrix}}}

On the other hand, assuming that our original matrix A is invertible, this matrix {\displaystyle X_{1}} has columns {\displaystyle A^{-1}\mathbf {b} ,A^{-1}\mathbf {v} _{2},\ldots ,A^{-1}\mathbf {v} _{n}}, where {\displaystyle \mathbf {v} _{n}} is the n-th column of the matrix A. Recall that the matrix {\displaystyle A_{1}} has columns {\displaystyle \mathbf {b} ,\mathbf {v} _{2},\ldots ,\mathbf {v} _{n}}, and therefore {\displaystyle X_{1}=A^{-1}A_{1}}. Hence, by using that the determinant of the product of two matrices is the product of the determinants, we have

{\displaystyle x_{1}=\det(X_{1})=\det(A^{-1})\det(A_{1})={\frac {\det(A_{1})}{\det(A)}}.}

The proof for other {\displaystyle x_{j}} is similar.
