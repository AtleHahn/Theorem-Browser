Statement

Let A be a real nonnegative square matrix with A^m>0 entrywise for some m\geq1 (a primitive matrix). Then r=\rho(A)>0 is an algebraically simple eigenvalue, all other eigenvalues have modulus less than r, and there are strictly positive right and left eigenvectors for r.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Understanding the statement involves finite-dimensional matrices; eigenvalues and spectral radius. These prerequisites fit undergraduate coursework or a brief extension of it. A bright US college student should be able to understand this statement after approximately 30 minutes of focused background research; this assesses understanding the claim, not its proof.

Proof 1: Source spectral argument outline

Given a nonnegative A, assume there exists m such that A^m is positive. Then A^{m+1},A^{m+2},A^{m+3},\ldots are all positive. Indeed, A^{m+1}=AA^m, so it can have a zero element only if some row of A is entirely zero, but in this case the same row of A^m will be zero.

Assume that the spectral radius \rho(A)=1 (otherwise consider A/\rho(A); the radius is nonzero since a positive power has positive trace). Hence there exists an eigenvalue \lambda on the unit circle, and all the other eigenvalues are less than or equal to 1 in absolute value. Suppose that \lambda\ne1. Then there exists a positive integer m such that A^m is a positive matrix and the real part of \lambda^m is negative. Let \epsilon be half the smallest diagonal entry of A^m and set T=A^m-\epsilon I, which is yet another positive matrix. Moreover, if Ax=\lambda x, then A^mx=\lambda^m x, thus \lambda^m-\epsilon is an eigenvalue of T. Because of the choice of m, this point lies outside the unit disk; consequently \rho(T)>1. On the other hand, all the entries in T are positive and less than or equal to those in A^m, so by entrywise comparison of powers and Gelfand's formula,

\rho(T)\leq\rho(A^m)=\rho(A)^m=1.

This contradiction means that \lambda=1. In particular, there can be no other eigenvalues on the unit circle.

The existence of a nonnegative eigenvector can be established using the power method, which states here that the sequence of vectors

b_{k+1}=\frac{Ab_k}{|Ab_k|}

converges to an eigenvector with the maximum eigenvalue for an initial vector b_0 outside an exceptional set of measure zero. Starting with a nonnegative vector b_0 outside that set produces a sequence of nonnegative vectors b_k. Hence the limiting vector is also nonnegative. By the power method this limiting vector is a dominant eigenvector for A. The corresponding eigenvalue is nonnegative.

The power-method convergence theorem used here requires that there are not several eigenvalues of the same absolute value as the maximal one. The preceding argument guarantees this for the primitive matrix under consideration. Strict positivity follows from the following argument (a primitive matrix is irreducible).

One of the definitions of irreducibility for nonnegative matrices is that for all indices i,j there exists m\geq1 such that (A^m)_{ij} is strictly positive. Given a nonzero nonnegative eigenvector v, at least one of its components, say the ith, is strictly positive. Its eigenvalue r is nonnegative because Av=rv\geq0. Given n\geq1 such that (A^n)_{ii}>0, we have

r^n v_i=(A^n v)_i\geq(A^n)_{ii}v_i>0.

Hence r is strictly positive. Then, given m such that (A^m)_{ji}>0,

r^m v_j=(A^m v)_j\geq(A^m)_{ji}v_i>0,

hence v_j is strictly positive, i.e., the eigenvector is strictly positive.

Applying the same argument to A^T gives a strictly positive left eigenvector.

Given a strictly positive eigenvector v corresponding to r, suppose there is another eigenvector w with the same eigenvalue, linearly independent of v. The vectors v and w can be chosen to be real, because A and r are both real, so the null space of A-rI has a basis consisting of real vectors. Assume at least one of the components of w is positive (otherwise multiply w by -1). Given maximal possible \alpha such that u=v-\alpha w is nonnegative, one of the components of u is zero, otherwise \alpha is not maximum. Vector u is a nonzero eigenvector. It is nonnegative, hence by the positivity argument above it is strictly positive. On the other hand, at least one component of u is zero. The contradiction implies that such a w does not exist.

There are no nontrivial Jordan blocks corresponding to the Perron–Frobenius eigenvalue r or any other eigenvalue with the same absolute value. If there is such a Jordan block, then the infinity norm \|(A/r)^k\|_\infty tends to infinity as k\to\infty, but that contradicts the existence of the positive eigenvector.

Normalize r=1 by replacing A by A/r. Letting v be a Perron–Frobenius strictly positive eigenvector, so Av=v, we have

\|v\|_\infty=\|A^kv\|_\infty \geq\|A^k\|_\infty\min_i v_i, \qquad \|A^k\|_\infty\leq\frac{\|v\|_\infty}{\min_i v_i}.

So \|A^k\|_\infty is bounded for all k. This contradicts the existence of a nontrivial Jordan block for any eigenvalue of absolute value 1 (in particular for the Perron–Frobenius one). For a two by two block,

J^k=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda&1\\0&\lambda\end{pmatrix}^k =\begin{pmatrix}\lambda^k&k\lambda^{k-1}\\0&\lambda^k\end{pmatrix},

hence \|J^k\|_\infty=1+k for |\lambda|=1, so it tends to infinity when k does so. Larger nontrivial Jordan blocks have the same unbounded entries. If J=C^{-1}AC is the Jordan form, then

\|A^k\|_\infty\geq \frac{\|J^k\|_\infty}{\|C^{-1}\|_\infty\|C\|_\infty},

so it also tends to infinity. The resulting contradiction implies that there are no nontrivial Jordan blocks for the corresponding eigenvalues.

Combining the two claims above reveals that the Perron–Frobenius eigenvalue r is a simple root of the characteristic polynomial.
