Statement

Let X_1,\ldots,X_n be independent zero-one variables with \Pr(X_i=1)=p, where n\ge1 and 0<p<1. Put \bar X=n^{-1}\sum_iX_i and, for 0<q<1,

D(q\Vert p)=q\log(q/p)+(1-q)\log((1-q)/(1-p)).

For p<q<1, \Pr(\bar X\ge q)\le e^{-nD(q\Vert p)}; for 0<q<p, \Pr(\bar X\le q)\le e^{-nD(q\Vert p)}.

Level
High school only
Level justification

The variables are finitely many independent success/failure trials, their average is a fraction of successes, and D is explicitly given by logarithms. Thus both assertions are numerical tail bounds for finite coin trials using supplied formulas. Elementary finite probability, averages, logarithms and exponentials suffice; the relative-entropy name adds no prerequisite.

Proof 1: Optimize the Bernoulli exponential bound

Let q = p + ε. For p<q<1, taking a=nq in the exponential Markov bound for a sum of independent variables, we obtain:

{\displaystyle \Pr \left({\frac {1}{n}}\sum X_{i}\geq q\right)\leq \inf _{t>0}{\frac {E\left[\prod e^{tX_{i}}\right]}{e^{tnq}}}=\inf _{t>0}\left({\frac {E\left[e^{tX_{i}}\right]}{e^{tq}}}\right)^{n}.}

Now, knowing that Pr(Xi = 1) = p, Pr(Xi = 0) = 1 − p, we have

{\displaystyle \left({\frac {\operatorname {E} \left[e^{tX_{i}}\right]}{e^{tq}}}\right)^{n}=\left({\frac {pe^{t}+(1-p)}{e^{tq}}}\right)^{n}=\left(pe^{(1-q)t}+(1-p)e^{-qt}\right)^{n}.}

Therefore, we can easily compute the infimum, using calculus:

{\displaystyle {\frac {d}{dt}}\left(pe^{(1-q)t}+(1-p)e^{-qt}\right)=(1-q)pe^{(1-q)t}-q(1-p)e^{-qt}}

Setting the equation to zero and solving, we have

{\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}(1-q)pe^{(1-q)t}&=q(1-p)e^{-qt}\\(1-q)pe^{t}&=q(1-p)\end{aligned}}}

so that

{\displaystyle e^{t}={\frac {(1-p)q}{(1-q)p}}.}

Thus,

{\displaystyle t=\log \left({\frac {(1-p)q}{(1-q)p}}\right).}

As q = p + ε > p, we see that t > 0, so our bound is satisfied on t. Having solved for t, we can plug back into the equations above to find that

{\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}\log \left(pe^{(1-q)t}+(1-p)e^{-qt}\right)&=\log \left(e^{-qt}(1-p+pe^{t})\right)\\&=\log \left(e^{-q\log \left({\frac {(1-p)q}{(1-q)p}}\right)}\right)+\log \left(1-p+pe^{\log \left({\frac {1-p}{1-q}}\right)}e^{\log {\frac {q}{p}}}\right)\\&=-q\log {\frac {1-p}{1-q}}-q\log {\frac {q}{p}}+\log \left(1-p+p\left({\frac {1-p}{1-q}}\right){\frac {q}{p}}\right)\\&=-q\log {\frac {1-p}{1-q}}-q\log {\frac {q}{p}}+\log \left({\frac {(1-p)(1-q)}{1-q}}+{\frac {(1-p)q}{1-q}}\right)\\&=-q\log {\frac {q}{p}}+\left(-q\log {\frac {1-p}{1-q}}+\log {\frac {1-p}{1-q}}\right)\\&=-q\log {\frac {q}{p}}+(1-q)\log {\frac {1-p}{1-q}}\\&=-D(q\parallel p).\end{aligned}}}

We now have our desired result, that

{\displaystyle \Pr \left({\tfrac {1}{n}}\sum X_{i}\geq p+\varepsilon \right)\leq e^{-D(p+\varepsilon \parallel p)n}.}

To complete the proof for the symmetric case, we simply define the random variable Yi = 1 − Xi, apply the same proof, and plug it into our bound.

Level justification

Factor the finite independent exponential moment, compute the Bernoulli expectation and minimize the displayed exponential expression by differentiation. The optimizer has the required sign; complementing the bits gives the other tail. This is undergraduate calculus and elementary probability.

Proof 2: Compare the two finite product probabilities

The following proof is from an article by Wolfgang Mulzer. Let 0<p<q<1. The proof analyzes two distributions {\displaystyle D_{p}} and {\displaystyle D_{q}}, both over {\displaystyle n}-tuples of bits {\displaystyle X=(X_{1},\dots ,X_{n})}. In the distribution {\displaystyle D_{p}} each {\displaystyle X_{i}} is an independent Bernoulli random variable with expectation {\displaystyle p}, and {\displaystyle D_{q}} is defined analogously. When {\displaystyle \sum X_{i}=k}, the ratio {\displaystyle D_{q}(X)/D_{p}(X)} is

{\displaystyle \left({\frac {q}{p}}\right)^{k}\left({\frac {1-q}{1-p}}\right)^{n-k}.}

Note that this is monotone in k, and so whenever {\displaystyle \sum X_{i}\geq qn}, the ratio {\displaystyle D_{q}(X)/D_{p}(X)} is at least

{\displaystyle \left({\frac {q}{p}}\right)^{qn}\left({\frac {1-q}{1-p}}\right)^{n-qn}=e^{D(q\parallel p)n}.}

This shows us that {\displaystyle \sum X_{i}\geq qn} is unlikely in {\displaystyle D_{p}} since

{\displaystyle \Pr _{X\sim D_{p}}\left(\sum X_{i}\geq qn\right)\leq e^{-D(q\parallel p)n}\Pr _{X\sim D_{q}}\left(\sum X_{i}\geq qn\right)\leq e^{-D(q\parallel p)n}.}

For the symmetric case we simply define the random variable Yi = 1 − Xi, apply the same proof, and plug it into our bound.

Level
High school only
Level justification

On the finite set of bit strings, compare the explicit product weights at p and q. Their ratio increases with the number of ones, so summing over the tail gives the bound; logarithms merely rewrite the displayed product. No differentiation or infinite probability framework is used.
