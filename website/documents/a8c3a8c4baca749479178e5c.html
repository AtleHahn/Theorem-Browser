Statement

Let p be prime and f∈Z[x] have at least one coefficient not divisible by p. Then p divides f(x) for at most deg(f) distinct integers x in {1,2,…,p}.

Level

Proof 1: Reduce modulo p and induct after removing a linear factor

Let f\in\mathbb Z[x] be an integer polynomial, and write g\in(\mathbb Z/p\mathbb Z)[x] for the polynomial obtained by taking its coefficients modulo p. Then, for all integers x,

f(x)\equiv0\pmod p\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad g(x\bmod p)=0.

Furthermore, by the basic rules of modular arithmetic,

f(x)\equiv0\pmod p \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad f(x\bmod p)\equiv0\pmod p \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad g(x\bmod p)=0.

The integers 1,\ldots,p represent every residue once. Since not every coefficient of f is divisible by p, g is nonzero, and \deg g\leq\deg f. We prove the bound on the number of roots of g by induction on its degree.

If \deg g=0 then g has no roots and the statement is true.

If \deg g\geq1 without roots then the statement is also trivially true.

Otherwise, \deg g\geq1 and g has a root k\in\mathbb Z/p\mathbb Z. The fact that \mathbb Z/p\mathbb Z is a field allows us to apply the division algorithm to g and the polynomial x-k (of degree 1), which yields the existence of a polynomial h\in(\mathbb Z/p\mathbb Z)[x] (of degree lower than that of g) and of a constant r\in\mathbb Z/p\mathbb Z such that

g(x)=h(x)(x-k)+r.

Evaluating at x=k provides r=0. The other roots of g are then roots of h as well, which by the induction hypothesis are at most \deg h=\deg g-1 in number. Including k gives at most \deg g, hence at most \deg f, roots. This proves the result.
