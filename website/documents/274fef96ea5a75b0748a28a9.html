Statement

Let n\ge1 be an integer. Partition a rectangle into columns of widths 0<x_1<\cdots<x_n and rows of heights 0<y_1<\cdots<y_n. Among selections of one cell from each row and column, the largest total area uses equally ordered indices and the smallest uses oppositely ordered indices.

Level
High school only
Level justification

The finite grid, rectangle areas and one-per-row/column choices are elementary Euclidean geometry and counting.

Proof 1: Encode a cell selection by a permutation

Assume that {\displaystyle 0<x_{1}<\cdots <x_{n}} and {\displaystyle 0<y_{1}<\cdots <y_{n}.} Consider a rectangle of width {\displaystyle x_{1}+\cdots +x_{n}} and height {\displaystyle y_{1}+\cdots +y_{n},} subdivided into {\displaystyle n} columns of widths {\displaystyle x_{1},\ldots ,x_{n}} and the same number of rows of heights {\displaystyle y_{1},\ldots ,y_{n},} so there are {\displaystyle \textstyle n^{2}} small rectangles. You are supposed to take {\displaystyle n} of these, one from each column and one from each row. The rearrangement inequality says that you optimize the total area of your selection by taking the rectangles on the diagonal or the antidiagonal.

Each selection has area \sum_i x_i y_{\sigma(i)} for a permutation \sigma; the matching order maximizes this sum and the reverse order minimizes it.

Level
High school only
Level justification

Encode a one-per-row-and-column choice by a permutation and apply finite rearrangement to the area sum. School finite counting and inequalities suffice.
