Statement

Let R be a commutative ring with identity, n\ge1 an integer and A\in M_n(R). Write p(t)=\det(tI_n-A)=\sum_{i=0}^n c_it^i with c_n=1. Then \operatorname{adj}(-A)=\sum_{i=1}^n c_i A^{i-1}.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Undergraduate linear algebra supplies determinants, cofactors, matrices and permutation signs; introductory algebra supplies commutative rings and polynomial coefficients. The displayed formula uses these directly, so moving the entries from a field to a commutative ring is a small extension, with no additional structural theory required.

Proof 1: The adjugate is a characteristic-coefficient polynomial — outline

The matrix {\displaystyle tI_{n}-A} whose determinant is the characteristic polynomial of A is a matrix with polynomials as entries, and since polynomials form a commutative ring, it has an adjugate

{\displaystyle B=\operatorname {adj} (tI_{n}-A).}

Then, according to the right-hand fundamental relation of the adjugate, one has

{\displaystyle (tI_{n}-A)B=\det(tI_{n}-A)I_{n}=p(t)I_{n}.}

Since B is also a matrix with polynomials in t as entries, one can, for each i, collect the coefficients of {\displaystyle t^{i}} in each entry to form a matrix Bi with entries in R, such that one has

{\displaystyle B=\sum _{i=0}^{n-1}t^{i}B_{i}.}

(The way the entries of B are defined makes clear that no powers higher than tn−1 occur). While this looks like a polynomial with matrices as coefficients, we shall not consider such a notion; it is just a way to write a matrix with polynomial entries as a linear combination of n constant matrices, and the coefficient {\displaystyle t^{i}} has been written to the left of the matrix to stress this point of view.

Now, one can expand the matrix product in our equation:

{\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}p(t)I_{n}&=(tI_{n}-A)B\\&=(tI_{n}-A)\sum _{i=0}^{n-1}t^{i}B_{i}\\&=\sum _{i=0}^{n-1}tI_{n}\cdot t^{i}B_{i}-\sum _{i=0}^{n-1}A\cdot t^{i}B_{i}\\&=\sum _{i=0}^{n-1}t^{i+1}B_{i}-\sum _{i=0}^{n-1}t^{i}AB_{i}\\&=t^{n}B_{n-1}+\sum _{i=1}^{n-1}t^{i}(B_{i-1}-AB_{i})-AB_{0}.\end{aligned}}}

Writing

{\displaystyle p(t)I_{n}=t^{n}I_{n}+t^{n-1}c_{n-1}I_{n}+\cdots +tc_{1}I_{n}+c_{0}I_{n},}

one obtains an equality of two matrices with polynomial entries, written as linear combinations of constant matrices with powers of t as coefficients.

Such an equality can hold only if in any matrix position the entry that is multiplied by a given power {\displaystyle t^{i}} is the same on both sides; it follows that the constant matrices with coefficient {\displaystyle t^{i}} in both expressions must be equal. Writing these equations then for i from n down to 0, one finds

{\displaystyle B_{n-1}=I_{n},\qquad B_{i-1}-AB_{i}=c_{i}I_{n}\quad {\text{for }}1\leq i\leq n-1,\qquad -AB_{0}=c_{0}I_{n}.}

In fact, these equations allow successively expressing {\displaystyle B_{n-1},\ldots ,B_{1},B_{0}} as polynomials in A, which leads to the identity

{\displaystyle \operatorname {adj} (-A)=\sum _{i=1}^{n}c_{i}A^{i-1},}

valid for all n × n matrices, where

{\displaystyle p(t)=t^{n}+c_{n-1}t^{n-1}+\cdots +c_{1}t+c_{0}}

is the characteristic polynomial of A.

Level justification

The argument expands the adjugate matrix entrywise as a polynomial in t, applies the adjugate identity, and compares coefficients. Starting with B_(n-1)=I, the recurrence B_(i-1)=A B_i+c_i I gives the displayed polynomial for B_0=adj(-A). Matrix multiplication, polynomial coefficients and finite backward substitution are undergraduate algebra. The ring generality requires no division or advanced matrix-polynomial theory.
