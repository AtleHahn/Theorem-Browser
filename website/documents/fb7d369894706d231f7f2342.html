Statement

For real t, put H(t)=0 for t\le0 and H(t)=1 for t>0. Then

y(t)=\int_0^tH(s)\,ds=\begin{cases}0&t\le0,\\t&t>0\end{cases}

is absolutely continuous on each bounded interval, satisfies y(0)=0 and y'(t)=H(t) almost everywhere, but is not differentiable at zero. Thus it is an extended solution (an absolutely continuous function satisfying the equation almost everywhere and the initial condition), not an everywhere classical solution.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Calculus supplies the explicit ramp function and its derivative. Absolute continuity is a direct interval-length condition, and almost everywhere here excludes only the single point zero. These limited definitions suffice for this concrete example.

Proof 1: The ramp solves the Heaviside equation in the extended sense — outline

Integrating the two constant pieces of H gives the displayed ramp. Its derivative is zero for t<0 and one for t>0. At zero the left and right slopes differ, so the derivative does not exist there. The initial value is zero. The ramp is absolutely continuous, and its one exceptional point does not affect almost-everywhere satisfaction of the equation.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Integrate two constant pieces and compare the one-sided slopes. Absolute continuity here follows directly from the Lipschitz bound, and almost everywhere excludes just a singleton; these are small additions to undergraduate real analysis.
