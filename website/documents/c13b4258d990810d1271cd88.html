Statement

For a,b\geq0 and real p,q>1 with 1/p+1/q=1, one has ab\leq a^p/p+b^q/q. Equality holds if and only if a^p=b^q.

High school only

Proof 1: Partition a rectangle under inverse power curves

Since {\displaystyle {\tfrac {1}{p}}+{\tfrac {1}{q}}=1,}, then {\displaystyle p-1={\tfrac {1}{q-1}}.} A graph {\displaystyle y=x^{p-1}} on the {\displaystyle xy}-plane is thus also a graph {\displaystyle x=y^{q-1}.} From sketching a visual representation of the integrals of the area between this curve and the axes, and the area in the rectangle bounded by the lines {\displaystyle x=0,x=a,y=0,y=b,} and the fact that {\displaystyle y} is always increasing for increasing {\displaystyle x} and vice versa, we can see that {\displaystyle \textstyle \int _{0}^{a}x^{p-1}\mathrm {d} x} upper bounds the area of the rectangle below the curve (with equality when {\displaystyle b\geq a^{p-1}}) and {\displaystyle \textstyle \int _{0}^{b}y^{q-1}\mathrm {d} y} upper bounds the area of the rectangle above the curve (with equality when {\displaystyle b\leq a^{p-1}}). Thus, {\displaystyle \textstyle \int _{0}^{a}x^{p-1}\mathrm {d} x+\int _{0}^{b}y^{q-1}\mathrm {d} y\geq ab,} with equality when {\displaystyle b=a^{p-1}} (or equivalently, {\displaystyle a^{p}=b^{q}}). Young's inequality follows from evaluating the integrals.

Proof 2: Concavity of the logarithm

The claim is certainly true if {\displaystyle a=0} or {\displaystyle b=0} so henceforth assume that {\displaystyle a>0} and {\displaystyle b>0.} Set {\displaystyle t=1/p} and {\displaystyle (1-t)=1/q.} Because the logarithm function is concave,

{\displaystyle \ln \left(ta^{p}+(1-t)b^{q}\right)~\geq ~t\ln \left(a^{p}\right)+(1-t)\ln \left(b^{q}\right)=\ln(ab)}

with equality if and only if {\displaystyle a^{p}=b^{q}.} Young's inequality follows by exponentiating.

Proof 3: Scalar minimization and rescaling — outline

Let {\displaystyle 0<\alpha <1} and {\displaystyle \alpha +\beta =1}. The inequality

{\displaystyle x~\leq ~\alpha x^{p}+\beta }

holds for all {\displaystyle x\geq 0} if and only if {\displaystyle \alpha =1/p} (and hence {\displaystyle \beta =1/q}). This can be shown by convexity arguments or by simply minimizing the single-variable function.

To prove the full Young's inequality, clearly assume that {\displaystyle a>0} and {\displaystyle b>0}. Apply the inequality above to {\displaystyle x=a/b^{s}} to obtain:

{\displaystyle {\frac {a}{b^{s}}}\leq {\frac {1}{p}}{\frac {a^{p}}{b^{sp}}}+{\frac {1}{q}}.}

It is easy to see that choosing {\displaystyle s=q-1} and multiplying both sides by {\displaystyle b^{q}} yields Young's inequality.
