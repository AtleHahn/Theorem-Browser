Statement

If \alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_n are complex algebraic numbers linearly independent over \mathbb Q, then e^{\alpha_1},\ldots,e^{\alpha_n} are algebraically independent over \mathbb Q: no nonzero polynomial in \mathbb Q[X_1,\ldots,X_n] vanishes at that tuple.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Polynomial roots, rational linear independence and complex exponentials belong to undergraduate algebra and calculus. Algebraic numbers are polynomial roots over Q; algebraic independence is the explicitly given absence of a polynomial relation. These definitions suffice without number-field or Galois theory.

Proof 1: Expand monomials into distinct exponentials

Use Baker's linear formulation of the Lindemann–Weierstrass theorem: exponentials of distinct algebraic numbers are linearly independent over the algebraic numbers. This implies the stated algebraic independence. Indeed, if {\displaystyle \alpha (1),\ldots ,\alpha (n)} are algebraic numbers that are linearly independent over {\displaystyle \mathbb {Q} }, and

{\displaystyle P(x_{1},\ldots ,x_{n})=\sum b_{i_{1},\ldots ,i_{n}}x_{1}^{i_{1}}\cdots x_{n}^{i_{n}}}

is a polynomial with rational coefficients that vanishes at the tuple of exponentials, then we have

{\displaystyle P\left(e^{\alpha (1)},\dots ,e^{\alpha (n)}\right)=\sum b_{i_{1},\dots ,i_{n}}e^{i_{1}\alpha (1)+\cdots +i_{n}\alpha (n)},}

and since {\displaystyle \alpha (1),\ldots ,\alpha (n)} are algebraic numbers which are linearly independent over the rationals, the numbers {\displaystyle i_{1}\alpha (1)+\cdots +i_{n}\alpha (n)} are algebraic and they are distinct for distinct n-tuples {\displaystyle (i_{1},\dots ,i_{n})}. So from Baker's formulation of the theorem we get {\displaystyle b_{i_{1},\ldots ,i_{n}}=0} for all n-tuples {\displaystyle (i_{1},\dots ,i_{n})}.
