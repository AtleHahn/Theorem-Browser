Statement

Let G be a finite abelian group, and let the prime p divide |G|. Then G has an element of order p.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Introductory abstract algebra supplies groups, ring operations, quotients and homomorphisms. The additional operations or central-element conditions are explicitly given; for the circle group, complex exponentials and continuity are familiar from calculus and topology. No further structural theory is needed to understand the claim.

Proof 1: Induction through a cyclic subgroup quotient

The proof is by induction on n = |G|, and has as starting case n = p which is trivial because any non-identity element now has order p. Suppose first that G is abelian. Take any non-identity element a, and let H be the cyclic group it generates. If p divides |H|, then a|H|/p is an element of order p. If p does not divide |H|, then it divides the order [G:H] of the quotient group G/H, which therefore contains an element of order p by the inductive hypothesis. That element is a class xH for some x in G, and if m is the order of x in G, then xm = e in G gives (xH)m = eH in G/H, so p divides m; as before xm/p is now an element of order p in G, completing the proof for the abelian case.

Level justification

Induction uses a cyclic subgroup and its abelian quotient, then lifts a quotient element whose order divides that of its representative. Lagrange and the order of a power finish the argument. This is standard undergraduate group theory.
