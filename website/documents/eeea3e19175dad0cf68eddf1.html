Statement

Let X_1,\ldots,X_n be independent normal random variables with common mean \mu and variance q>0, where n\ge1 is an integer. With known \mu and parameter q, the sample Fisher information is I_n(q)=n/(2q^2), where Fisher information is the expected square of the parameter derivative of the joint log density.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Fisher information is explicitly the expected squared log-density derivative. Normal density, expectation and independence are linked finite-dimensional probability definitions extending calculus; no estimator optimality or unspecified comparison class occurs.

Proof 1: Differentiate the normal log density twice

Recall that the score V for one observation is defined as

V=\frac{\partial}{\partial q}\log L(q,X),

where L is the likelihood function. Thus in this case,

\log L(q,X) =\log\left[\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi q}}e^{-(X-\mu)^2/(2q)}\right] =-\log\sqrt{2\pi q}-\frac{(X-\mu)^2}{2q},V=\frac{\partial}{\partial q}\log L(q,X) =\frac{\partial}{\partial q}\left[-\log\sqrt{2\pi q}-\frac{(X-\mu)^2}{2q}\right] =-\frac1{2q}+\frac{(X-\mu)^2}{2q^2},

where the second equality is from elementary calculus. By the Fisher information identity, the information in a single observation is just minus the expectation of the derivative of V, or

I_1(q)=-E\left(\frac{\partial V}{\partial q}\right) =-E\left(-\frac{(X-\mu)^2}{q^3}+\frac1{2q^2}\right) =\frac q{q^3}-\frac1{2q^2} =\frac1{2q^2}.

Thus the information in a sample of n independent observations is just n times this, or

I_n(q)=\frac n{2q^2}.
