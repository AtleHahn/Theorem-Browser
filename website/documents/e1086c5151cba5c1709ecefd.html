Statement

Let u,v be real C^1 functions on an interval I, and suppose W(x)=u(x)v'(x)-u'(x)v(x) is nowhere zero. If x_0<x_1 are successive zeros of u, then v has exactly one zero in (x_0,x_1).

Proof 1: Endpoint signs and the intermediate value theorem (outline)

Since the Wronskian W(x) is continuous and nonzero on I, it has constant sign. Without loss of generality, suppose that W(x)<0 for all x\in I. Then

u(x)v'(x)-u'(x)v(x)\ne0.

So at x=x_0,

W(x_0)=-u'(x_0)v(x_0),

and either u'(x_0) and v(x_0) are both positive or both negative. Without loss of generality, suppose that they are both positive. Now, at x=x_1,

W(x_1)=-u'(x_1)v(x_1).

Since x_0 and x_1 are successive zeros of u, the function is positive between them, so u'(x_1)\leq0. The nonvanishing of W(x_1) excludes equality, giving u'(x_1)<0. Thus, to keep W(x_1)<0, we must have v(x_1)<0.

So somewhere in the interval (x_0,x_1) the sign of v(x) changed. By the intermediate value theorem there exists x^*\in(x_0,x_1) such that v(x^*)=0.

On the other hand, there can be only one zero in (x_0,x_1), because otherwise v would have two successive zeros and there would be no zeros of u in between, and, with u and v exchanged, it was just proved that this is impossible.
