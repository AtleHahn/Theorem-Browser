Statement

A single-vertex crease pattern consists of distinct rays from an interior point of a flat sheet. Flat-foldable means that all prescribed creases are folded, each as a mountain or valley, with no other creases, to a flat configuration with compatible layer order and no paper crossing. For 2n creases, n\geq1, let \alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_{2n}>0 be the successive sector angles in degrees, so their sum is 360^\circ. The pattern is flat-foldable if and only if \alpha_1-\alpha_2+\cdots+\alpha_{2n-1}-\alpha_{2n}=0. Equivalently each alternating set of angles sums to 180^\circ.

Proof 1: Orientation reversal and minimal-partial-sum accordion folding — outline

To show that Kawasaki's condition necessarily holds for any flat-folded figure, it suffices to observe that, at each fold, the orientation of the paper is reversed. Thus, if the first crease in the flat-folded figure is placed in the plane parallel to the x-axis, the next crease must be rotated from it by an angle of α1, the crease after that by an angle of α1 − α2 (because the second angle has the reverse orientation from the first), etc. In order for the paper to meet back up with itself at the final angle, Kawasaki's condition must be met. More precisely, closure gives the alternating sum zero modulo 360^\circ; positivity and the total 360^\circ put it strictly between -360^\circ and 360^\circ, so it is zero. The two alternating sums therefore both equal 180^\circ.

Showing that the condition is also a sufficient condition is a matter of describing how to fold a given crease pattern so that it folds flat. That is, one must show how to choose whether to make mountain or valley folds, and in what order the flaps of paper should be arranged on top of each other. One way to do this is to choose an integer i\in\{0,\ldots,n-1\} such that the partial alternating sum

α1 − α2 + α3 − ⋯ + α2i − 1 − α2i

is as small as possible, choosing i=0 if the minimum is zero. Either i = 0 and the partial sum is an empty sum that is also zero, or for some nonzero choice of i the partial sum is negative. Then, accordion fold the pattern, starting with angle α2i + 1 and alternating between mountain and valley folds, placing each angular wedge of the paper below the previous folds. At each step until the final fold, an accordion fold of this type will never self-intersect. The choice of i ensures that the first wedge sticks out to the left of all the other folded pieces of paper, allowing the final wedge to connect back up to it.

Proof 2: Orientation reversal and smallest-sector flap induction — outline

To show that Kawasaki's condition necessarily holds for any flat-folded figure, it suffices to observe that, at each fold, the orientation of the paper is reversed. Thus, if the first crease in the flat-folded figure is placed in the plane parallel to the x-axis, the next crease must be rotated from it by an angle of α1, the crease after that by an angle of α1 − α2 (because the second angle has the reverse orientation from the first), etc. In order for the paper to meet back up with itself at the final angle, Kawasaki's condition must be met. More precisely, closure gives the alternating sum zero modulo 360^\circ; positivity and the total 360^\circ put it strictly between -360^\circ and 360^\circ, so it is zero. The two alternating sums therefore both equal 180^\circ.

Consider the smallest angle αi and the two creases on either side of it. Mountain-fold one of these two creases and valley-fold the other, choosing arbitrarily which fold to use for which crease. Then, glue the resulting flap of paper onto the remaining part of the crease pattern. The result of this gluing will be a crease pattern with two fewer creases, on a conical sheet of paper, that still satisfies Kawasaki's condition. Therefore, by mathematical induction, repeating this process will eventually lead to a flat folding. The base case of the induction is a cone with only two creases and two equal-angle wedges, which can obviously be flat-folded by using a mountain fold for both creases. Undoing the flap identifications gives a flat folding of the original pattern.
