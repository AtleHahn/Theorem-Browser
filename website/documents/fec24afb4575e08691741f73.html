Statement

Let G=(V,E) be a finite simple bipartite graph with vertex partition V=A\sqcup B, every edge joining A to B. A matching is a set of pairwise vertex-disjoint edges; a vertex cover is a set of vertices containing an endpoint of every edge. The largest matching size equals the smallest vertex-cover size.

The statement defines matching, covering and bipartition in a finite graph and compares finite cardinalities.

Proof 1: Construct a cover by alternating paths — outline

Let M be a maximum matching, and call vertices in A left vertices and those in B right vertices.

No vertex in a vertex cover can cover more than one edge of {\displaystyle M} (because the edge half-overlap would prevent {\displaystyle M} from being a matching in the first place), so if a vertex cover with {\displaystyle |M|} vertices can be constructed, it must be a minimum cover.

To construct such a cover, let {\displaystyle U} be the set of unmatched vertices in {\displaystyle A} (possibly empty), and let {\displaystyle Z} be the set of vertices that are either in {\displaystyle U} or are connected to {\displaystyle U} by alternating paths (paths that alternate between edges that are in the matching and edges that are not in the matching). Let

{\displaystyle K=(A\setminus Z)\cup (B\cap Z).}

Every edge e in E has a right endpoint in K or a left endpoint in K. If e belongs to an alternating path from U, its right endpoint lies in B\cap Z. If e is matched but not in such a path, then its left endpoint cannot be in such a path (because its unique matching edge would be part of the path), and thus belongs to A\setminus Z. Alternatively, if e is unmatched and its left endpoint lies on such an alternating path, that path can be extended by adding e unless its right endpoint is already on the path; in either case the right endpoint lies in B\cap Z. If the left endpoint is not reachable, it belongs to A\setminus Z. Thus, {\displaystyle K} forms a vertex cover.

Additionally, every vertex in {\displaystyle K} is an endpoint of a matched edge. For, every vertex in {\displaystyle A\setminus Z} is matched because {\displaystyle Z} is a superset of {\displaystyle U}, the set of unmatched left vertices. And every vertex in {\displaystyle B\cap Z} must also be matched, for if there existed an alternating path to an unmatched vertex then changing the matching by removing the matched edges from this path and adding the unmatched edges in their place would increase the size of the matching. However, no matched edge can have both of its endpoints in {\displaystyle K}. Thus, {\displaystyle K} is a vertex cover of cardinality equal to |M|, and must be a minimum vertex cover.

Proof 2: Translate a minimum cut into a vertex cover

The following proof provides a way of constructing a minimum vertex cover from a maximum matching. Let {\displaystyle G=(V,E)} be a bipartite graph and let {\displaystyle A,B} be the two parts of the vertex set {\displaystyle V}. Suppose that {\displaystyle M} is a maximum matching for {\displaystyle G}.

Construct the flow network {\displaystyle G'_{\infty }} derived from {\displaystyle G} in such way that there are edges of capacity {\displaystyle 1} from the source {\displaystyle s} to every vertex {\displaystyle a\in A} and from every vertex {\displaystyle b\in B} to the sink {\displaystyle t}, and of capacity {\displaystyle +\infty } from {\displaystyle a} to {\displaystyle b} for any {\displaystyle (a,b)\in E}.

By the matching–flow correspondence, taken as a prerequisite, the size {\displaystyle |M|} of the maximum matching in {\displaystyle G} is the size of a maximum flow in {\displaystyle G'_{\infty }}, which, in turn, is the size of a minimum cut in the network {\displaystyle G'_{\infty }}, as follows from the max-flow min-cut theorem.

Let {\displaystyle (S,T)} be a minimum cut, with s\in S and t\in T. Let {\displaystyle A=A_{S}\cup A_{T}} and {\displaystyle B=B_{S}\cup B_{T}}, such that {\displaystyle A_{S},B_{S}\subset S} and {\displaystyle A_{T},B_{T}\subset T}. Then the minimum cut is composed only of edges going from {\displaystyle s} to {\displaystyle A_{T}} or from {\displaystyle B_{S}} to {\displaystyle t}, as any edge from {\displaystyle A_{S}} to {\displaystyle B_{T}} would make the size of the cut infinite.

Therefore, the size of the minimum cut is equal to {\displaystyle |A_{T}|+|B_{S}|}. On the other hand, {\displaystyle A_{T}\cup B_{S}} is a vertex cover, as any edge that is not incident to vertices from {\displaystyle A_{T}} and {\displaystyle B_{S}} must be incident to a pair of vertices from {\displaystyle A_{S}} and {\displaystyle B_{T}}, which would contradict the fact that there are no edges between {\displaystyle A_{S}} and {\displaystyle B_{T}}.

Thus, {\displaystyle A_{T}\cup B_{S}} is a minimum vertex cover of {\displaystyle G}.

No vertex cover can be smaller, since it must meet every edge of the matching M, whose edges have disjoint endpoints.

Proof 3: Use fractional duality and bipartite integrality

To explain this proof, we first have to extend the notion of a matching to that of a fractional matching - an assignment of a weight in [0,1] to each edge, such that the sum of weights near each vertex is at most 1 (an integral matching is a special case of a fractional matching in which the weights are in {0,1}). Similarly we define a fractional vertex-cover - an assignment of a non-negative weight to each vertex, such that the sum of weights in each edge is at least 1 (an integral vertex-cover is a special case of a fractional vertex-cover in which the weights are in {0,1}).

The maximum fractional matching size in a graph {\displaystyle G=(V,E)} is the solution of the following linear program:

Maximize 1E · x

Subject to: x ≥ 0E

AG · x ≤ 1V.

where x is a vector of size |E| in which each element represents the weight of an edge in the fractional matching. 1E is a vector of |E| ones, so the first line indicates the size of the matching. 0E is a vector of |E| zeros, so the second line indicates the constraint that the weights are non-negative. 1V is a vector of |V| ones and AG is the vertex-edge incidence matrix of G (entry 1 precisely at an incident vertex-edge pair), so the third line indicates the constraint that the sum of weights near each vertex is at most 1. Similarly, the minimum fractional vertex-cover size in {\displaystyle G=(V,E)} is the solution of the following LP:

Minimize 1V · y

Subject to: y ≥ 0V

AGT · y ≥ 1E.

where y is a vector of size |V| in which each element represents the weight of a vertex in the fractional cover. Here, the first line is the size of the cover, the second line represents the non-negativity of the weights, and the third line represents the requirement that the sum of weights near each edge must be at least 1. Now, the minimum fractional cover LP is exactly the dual linear program of the maximum fractional matching LP. Therefore, by the LP duality theorem, both programs have the same optimal value. This fact is true not only in bipartite graphs but in arbitrary graphs:

In any graph, the largest size of a fractional matching equals the smallest size of a fractional vertex cover.

What makes bipartite graphs special is that, in bipartite graphs, both these linear programs have optimal solutions in which all variable values are integers. This follows from the fact that in the fractional matching polytope of a bipartite graph, all extreme points have only integer coordinates, and the same is true for the fractional vertex-cover polytope. These polytope integrality results are taken as prerequisites. Integral matching weights are 0 or 1; integral cover weights can be reduced to 0 or 1 without losing feasibility. Therefore, the fractional equality implies:

In any bipartite graph, the largest size of a matching equals the smallest size of a vertex cover.
