Statement

Let f be a nonzero entire function of finite order \rho, and set p=\lfloor\rho\rfloor. Let m be its zero multiplicity at the origin and (a_n) its nonzero zeros repeated with multiplicity. Then f(z)=z^m e^{Q(z)}\prod_n E_p(z/a_n), where Q is a polynomial of degree at most p, E_p(w)=(1-w)\exp(\sum_{j=1}^p w^j/j), with the sum empty for p=0, and the product converges locally uniformly (a finite or empty zero list is allowed). Here the finite order \rho\ge0 means \rho=\limsup_{r\to\infty}\log\log\max(e,M_f(r))/\log r, where M_f(r)=\max_{|z|=r}|f(z)|.

Proof 1: Hadamard factorization with the floor of the order (outline)

Let {\displaystyle f} be an entire function of finite order {\displaystyle \rho }. If {\displaystyle f} has a zero of order {\displaystyle m} at the origin, write

{\displaystyle f(z)=z^{m}f_{0}(z),}

where {\displaystyle f_{0}} is entire and {\displaystyle f_{0}(0)\neq 0}. Multiplying by a nonzero constant does not affect the order, so it is enough to prove the theorem under the normalization {\displaystyle f(0)=1}. The factor {\displaystyle z^{m}} can then be restored at the end.

Let {\displaystyle a_{1},a_{2},\ldots } be the nonzero zeros of {\displaystyle f}, repeated according to multiplicity and ordered so that

{\displaystyle |a_{1}|\leq |a_{2}|\leq \cdots .}

Let {\displaystyle p=\lfloor \rho \rfloor }. For a finite zero list, the summability below is immediate. For an infinite zero list, the first step is to show that the zero sequence has exponent of convergence at most {\displaystyle \rho }, and in particular that

{\displaystyle \sum _{n=1}^{\infty }|a_{n}|^{-p-1}<\infty .}

Indeed, let {\displaystyle n(r)} denote the number of zeros of {\displaystyle f} in {\displaystyle |z|<r}, counted with multiplicity. By Jensen's formula, one obtains an estimate of the form

{\displaystyle n(r)\log 2\leq \log M(2r),}

where

{\displaystyle M(r)=\max _{|z|=r}|f(z)|.}

Since {\displaystyle f} has order {\displaystyle \rho }, for every {\displaystyle \varepsilon >0} and all sufficiently large {\displaystyle r},

{\displaystyle \log M(r)\leq r^{\rho +\varepsilon }.}

Choosing {\displaystyle \varepsilon >0} so small that {\displaystyle \rho +\varepsilon <p+1}, it follows that

{\displaystyle n(r)=O(r^{\rho +\varepsilon }).}

Since {\displaystyle k\leq n(2|a_{k}|)}, this gives

{\displaystyle |a_{k}|\geq Ck^{1/(\rho +\varepsilon )}}

for all sufficiently large {\displaystyle k} and some constant {\displaystyle C>0}. Therefore

{\displaystyle |a_{k}|^{-p-1}\leq C'k^{-(p+1)/(\rho +\varepsilon )},}

and the exponent on the right is greater than {\displaystyle 1}. Hence

{\displaystyle \sum _{n=1}^{\infty }|a_{n}|^{-p-1}<\infty .}

Consequently the canonical product

{\displaystyle P(z)=\prod _{n=1}^{\infty }E_{p}(z/a_{n})}

converges locally uniformly and defines an entire function whose zeros are precisely the zeros {\displaystyle a_{n}}, with the same multiplicities. Thus

{\displaystyle F(z)={\frac {f(z)}{P(z)}}}

is a zero-free entire function. Since the complex plane is simply connected, there is an entire function {\displaystyle g} such that

{\displaystyle F(z)=e^{g(z)}.}

Hence

{\displaystyle f(z)=e^{g(z)}P(z).}

It remains to prove that {\displaystyle g} is a polynomial of degree at most {\displaystyle p}. For this Conway uses the following logarithmic-derivative lemma.

Conway’s logarithmic-derivative lemma (used without proof). If {\displaystyle f} is an entire function of finite order {\displaystyle \rho }, {\displaystyle f(0)\neq 0}, and {\displaystyle p} is a nonnegative integer with {\displaystyle p>\rho -1}, then, away from the zeros of {\displaystyle f},

{\displaystyle {\frac {d^{p}}{dz^{p}}}\left({\frac {f'(z)}{f(z)}}\right)=-p!\sum _{n=1}^{\infty }{\frac {1}{(a_{n}-z)^{p+1}}}.}

In the present case {\displaystyle p=\lfloor \rho \rfloor }, so {\displaystyle p>\rho -1}. Applying the lemma to {\displaystyle f} gives

{\displaystyle {\frac {d^{p}}{dz^{p}}}\left({\frac {f'(z)}{f(z)}}\right)=-p!\sum _{n=1}^{\infty }{\frac {1}{(a_{n}-z)^{p+1}}}.}

On the other hand, since {\displaystyle f=e^{g}P},

{\displaystyle {\frac {f'}{f}}=g'+{\frac {P'}{P}}.}

For a single elementary factor,

{\displaystyle {\frac {d}{dz}}\log E_{p}(z/a)=-{\frac {1}{a-z}}+\sum _{j=1}^{p}{\frac {z^{j-1}}{a^{j}}}.}

After differentiating {\displaystyle p} times, the polynomial part vanishes, and hence

{\displaystyle {\frac {d^{p}}{dz^{p}}}\left({\frac {P'}{P}}\right)=-p!\sum _{n=1}^{\infty }{\frac {1}{(a_{n}-z)^{p+1}}}.}

Comparing the two formulas gives, away from the zeros and hence everywhere by analyticity,

{\displaystyle g^{(p+1)}(z)=0.}

Therefore {\displaystyle g} is a polynomial of degree at most {\displaystyle p}.

Restoring the constant factor and the zero at the origin, one obtains

{\displaystyle f(z)=z^{m}e^{Q(z)}\prod _{n=1}^{\infty }E_{p}(z/a_{n}),}

where {\displaystyle Q} is a polynomial of degree at most {\displaystyle p=\lfloor \rho \rfloor }.
