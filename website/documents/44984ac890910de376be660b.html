Statement

Let G and H be real Hilbert spaces, without a separability assumption, and let T:G\to H be a bounded linear operator. Write T^*:H\to G for its adjoint, characterized by \langle Tg,h\rangle_H=\langle g,T^*h\rangle_G.

The canonical Gaussian cylindrical probability on G specifies that every finite collection of linear coordinates g\mapsto\langle g,v_j\rangle_G has a centered joint Gaussian distribution with covariance matrix (\langle v_j,v_k\rangle_G)_{j,k}. Its image under T therefore specifies, for h_1,\ldots,h_m\in H, the centered Gaussian law with covariance matrix (\langle T^*h_j,T^*h_k\rangle_G)_{j,k}. A centered Gaussian vector with covariance matrix Q means a random vector whose every real linear combination with coefficient vector a is normal with mean zero and variance a^{\mathsf T}Qa; variance zero means a point mass at zero.

Call T Gaussian radonifying if these finite-dimensional laws are realized by a Radon probability measure \mu on H: for every finite list h_1,\ldots,h_m, the image of \mu under x\mapsto(\langle x,h_1\rangle_H,\ldots,\langle x,h_m\rangle_H) is the specified law. Here a Radon probability is a countably additive measure on the Borel sets of the norm topology, of total mass one, inner regular by compact sets.

Then T is Gaussian radonifying if and only if it is Hilbert–Schmidt: for an orthonormal basis (e_i)_{i\in I} of G,

\sum_{i\in I}\|Te_i\|_H^2<\infty.

For a possibly uncountable index set, this nonnegative sum is the supremum of the sums over finite subsets of I; it suffices that the condition hold for one orthonormal basis.
