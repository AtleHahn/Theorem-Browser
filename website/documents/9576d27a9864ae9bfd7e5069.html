Statement

There exist absolute positive constants C,K such that for every finite set P of n\ge1 distinct points in the Euclidean plane, either some line contains at least n/C points of P, or at least n^2/K distinct lines each contain at least two points of P.

Proof 1: Dyadic counting using the rich-line bound (outline)

Rich-line estimate (external prerequisite)

The Szemerédi–Trotter rich-line bound gives O(n^2/2^{3j}+n/2^j) lines containing between 2^j and 2^{j+1}-1 points of P. The implicit constant is absolute.

Dyadic pair count

Consider a set P of n points in the plane. Let j be a positive integer. Let us say that a pair of points A, B in the set P is j-connected if the line connecting A and B contains between {\displaystyle 2^{j}} and {\displaystyle 2^{j+1}-1} points of P (including A and B).

By the rich-line estimate, the number of such lines is O(n^2/2^{3j}+n/2^j). Since each such line connects together at most \binom{2^{j+1}-1}{2}=O(2^{2j}) pairs of points, we thus see that at most O(n^2/2^j+2^jn) pairs of points can be j-connected.

Now, let C be a large constant. By summing the geometric series, we see that the number of pairs of points which are j-connected for some j satisfying {\displaystyle C\leq 2^{j}\leq n/C} is at most {\displaystyle O(n^{2}/C)}.

On the other hand, the total number of pairs is {\displaystyle {\frac {n(n-1)}{2}}}. Thus if we choose C to be large enough, for sufficiently large n we can find at least {\displaystyle n^{2}/4} pairs (for instance) which are not j-connected for any {\displaystyle C\leq 2^{j}\leq n/C}. The lines that connect these pairs either pass through fewer than 2C points, or pass through more than n/C points. If the latter case holds for even one of these pairs, then we have the first conclusion of Beck's theorem. Thus we may assume that all of the {\displaystyle n^{2}/4} pairs are connected by lines which pass through fewer than 2C points. But each such line can connect at most {\displaystyle C(2C-1)} pairs of points. Thus there must be at least {\displaystyle n^{2}/[4C(2C-1)]} lines connecting at least two points, and the claim follows by taking {\displaystyle K=4C(2C-1)}.

Enlarging the absolute constants covers the finitely many smaller sizes.

Proof 2: Specialization of the Erdős–Beck bound (outline)

Use the Erdős–Beck theorem as a prerequisite: if a set of n planar points has at most n-k points on each line, with 0\le k<n-2, it determines \Omega(nk) lines, with an absolute positive implicit constant.

Beck's theorem can be derived by letting k=n(1-1/C) and applying the Erdős–Beck theorem. If no line contains at least n/C points, its collinearity hypothesis holds. The substitution is within the stated range for sufficiently large n; the finitely many smaller sizes are accommodated by increasing the absolute constants.
