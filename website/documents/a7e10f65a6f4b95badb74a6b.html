Statement

Let I be an open real interval, a,b\in C^1(I) with a(t)\le b(t), and let the real-valued function f(x,t) and its partial derivative f_t(x,t) be continuous on an open region containing all (x,t) with t\in I and a(t)\le x\le b(t). Then \frac d{dt}\int_{a(t)}^{b(t)}f(x,t)\,dx=\int_{a(t)}^{b(t)}f_t(x,t)\,dx+f(b(t),t)b'(t)-f(a(t),t)a'(t).

Proof 1: Split the increment into interior and boundary terms

Work near a fixed t on a compact rectangle in the open region containing the nearby integration segments. All integrals below are oriented integrals.

Now, suppose

{\displaystyle \int _{a}^{b}f(x,t )\,dx=\varphi (t ),}

where a and b are functions of t which take increments Δa and Δb, respectively, when t is increased by Δt. Then,

{\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}\Delta \varphi &=\varphi (t +\Delta t )-\varphi (t )\\[6pt]&=\int _{a+\Delta a}^{b+\Delta b}f(x,t +\Delta t )\,dx-\int _{a}^{b}f(x,t )\,dx\\[6pt]&=\int _{a+\Delta a}^{a}f(x,t +\Delta t )\,dx+\int _{a}^{b}f(x,t +\Delta t )\,dx+\int _{b}^{b+\Delta b}f(x,t +\Delta t )\,dx-\int _{a}^{b}f(x,t )\,dx\\[6pt]&=-\int _{a}^{a+\Delta a}f(x,t +\Delta t )\,dx+\int _{a}^{b}[f(x,t +\Delta t )-f(x,t )]\,dx+\int _{b}^{b+\Delta b}f(x,t +\Delta t )\,dx.\end{aligned}}}

A form of the mean value theorem, {\textstyle \int _{a}^{b}f(x)\,dx=(b-a)f(\xi ),} where a < ξ < b, can be applied to the first and last integrals of the formula for Δφ above, resulting in

{\displaystyle \Delta \varphi =-\Delta a\,f(\xi _{1},t +\Delta t )+\int _{a}^{b}[f(x,t +\Delta t )-f(x,t )]\,dx+\Delta b\,f(\xi _{2},t +\Delta t ).}

When an endpoint increment is negative, the intermediate point lies between the two endpoints; when it is zero, its term is zero.

Dividing by Δt, letting Δt → 0, noticing ξ1 → a and ξ2 → b and using the fixed-limit differentiation rule (the mean value theorem and uniform continuity of f_t give uniform convergence of the difference quotients on the fixed interval) in the form

\frac{d}{dt}\int_A^B f(x,t)\,dx=\int_A^B f_t(x,t)\,dx\qquad(A,B\text{ fixed})

yields

{\displaystyle {\frac {d\varphi }{dt }}=\int _{a}^{b}{\frac {\partial }{\partial t }}f(x,t )\,dx+f(b,t ){\frac {\partial b}{\partial t }}-f(a,t ){\frac {\partial a}{\partial t }}.}

This is the general form of the Leibniz integral rule.

Proof 2: Use a local integral primitive and the multivariable chain rule

In this proof use the source’s parameter-first notation g(x,t)=f(t,x). Work locally near a fixed parameter value, on a rectangle in the open region containing the nearby integration segments.

The general form of Leibniz's Integral Rule with variable limits can be derived as a consequence of the basic form of Leibniz's Integral Rule, the multivariable chain rule, and the first fundamental theorem of calculus. Suppose {\displaystyle g} is defined in a rectangle in the {\displaystyle x}-{\displaystyle t} plane, for {\displaystyle x\in [x_{1},x_{2}]} and {\displaystyle t\in [t_{1},t_{2}]}. Also, assume {\displaystyle g} and the partial derivative {\textstyle {\frac {\partial g}{\partial x}}} are both continuous functions on this rectangle. Suppose {\displaystyle a,b} are differentiable real valued functions defined on {\displaystyle [x_{1},x_{2}]}, with values in {\displaystyle [t_{1},t_{2}]} (i.e. for every {\displaystyle x\in [x_{1},x_{2}],a(x),b(x)\in [t_{1},t_{2}]}). Now, set

{\displaystyle F(x,y)=\int _{t_{1}}^{y}g(x,t)\,dt,\qquad {\text{for}}~x\in [x_{1},x_{2}]~{\text{and}}~y\in [t_{1},t_{2}]}

and

{\displaystyle G(x)=\int _{a(x)}^{b(x)}g(x,t)\,dt,\quad {\text{for}}~x\in [x_{1},x_{2}]}

Then, by properties of definite Integrals, we can write

{\displaystyle G(x)=\int _{t_{1}}^{b(x)}g(x,t)\,dt-\int _{t_{1}}^{a(x)}g(x,t)\,dt=F(x,b(x))-F(x,a(x))}

Since the functions {\displaystyle F,a,b} are all differentiable (see the remark at the end of the proof), by the multivariable chain rule, it follows that {\displaystyle G} is differentiable, and its derivative is given by the formula:

{\displaystyle G'(x)=\left({\frac {\partial F}{\partial x}}(x,b(x))+{\frac {\partial F}{\partial y}}(x,b(x))b'(x)\right)-\left({\frac {\partial F}{\partial x}}(x,a(x))+{\frac {\partial F}{\partial y}}(x,a(x))a'(x)\right)}

Now, note that for every {\displaystyle x\in [x_{1},x_{2}]}, and for every {\displaystyle y\in [t_{1},t_{2}]}, we have that {\textstyle {\frac {\partial F}{\partial x}}(x,y)=\int _{t_{1}}^{y}{\frac {\partial g}{\partial x}}(x,t)\,dt}, because when taking the partial derivative with respect to {\displaystyle x} of {\displaystyle F}, we are keeping {\displaystyle y} fixed in the expression {\textstyle \int _{t_{1}}^{y}g(x,t)\,dt}; thus the basic form of Leibniz's Integral Rule with constant limits of integration applies. Next, by the first fundamental theorem of calculus, we have that {\textstyle {\frac {\partial F}{\partial y}}(x,y)=g(x,y)}; because when taking the partial derivative with respect to {\displaystyle y} of {\displaystyle F}, the first variable {\displaystyle x} is fixed, so the fundamental theorem can indeed be applied.

Substituting these results into the equation for {\displaystyle G'(x)} above gives:

{\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}G'(x)&=\left(\int _{t_{1}}^{b(x)}{\frac {\partial g}{\partial x}}(x,t)\,dt+g(x,b(x))b'(x)\right)-\left(\int _{t_{1}}^{a(x)}{\dfrac {\partial g}{\partial x}}(x,t)\,dt+g(x,a(x))a'(x)\right)\\[2pt]&=g(x,b(x))b'(x)-g(x,a(x))a'(x)+\int _{a(x)}^{b(x)}{\frac {\partial g}{\partial x}}(x,t)\,dt,\end{aligned}}}

which, after returning to the notation f(t,x)=g(x,t), is the desired formula.

Applying the chain rule requires a justification: applying the Chain Rule to {\displaystyle G} requires that {\displaystyle F} already be differentiable. This is where we use our assumptions about {\displaystyle g}. As mentioned above, the partial derivatives of {\displaystyle F} are given by the formulas {\textstyle {\frac {\partial F}{\partial x}}(x,y)=\int _{t_{1}}^{y}{\frac {\partial g}{\partial x}}(x,t)\,dt} and {\textstyle {\frac {\partial F}{\partial y}}(x,y)=g(x,y)}. Since {\textstyle {\dfrac {\partial g}{\partial x}}} is continuous, its integral is also a continuous function, and since {\displaystyle g} is also continuous, these two results show that both the partial derivatives of {\displaystyle F} are continuous. Since continuity of partial derivatives implies differentiability of the function, {\displaystyle F} is indeed differentiable.
