Simpler proof assessment

Likely alternative prerequisite level: Undergraduate level; lowest recorded proof level: Beyond undergraduate level. The ascending chain condition implies every ideal is a finite intersection of irreducible ideals: otherwise a maximal counterexample splits into two strictly larger ones. In the quotient by an irreducible ideal, if ab=0 with a!=0, stabilize ann(b^n). For large n, (a) intersect (b^n)=0: multiply an equality ar=b^n s by b and use stabilization. Irreducibility forces b^n=0, proving primaryness. This covers arbitrary commutative Noetherian rings using ideals, quotients and induction, without associated-prime module theory.

Statement

Every proper ideal I of a commutative Noetherian ring R is a finite intersection of primary ideals. An ideal Q is primary if ab\in Q and a\notin Q imply b^m\in Q for some m\ge1.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Introductory ring theory supplies ideals, prime/maximal ideals, products and inclusion. Noetherian means every ideal is finitely generated. The further conditions are short ideal-based definitions: height counts chains of primes, local means a unique maximal ideal, and primary uses powers in the defining product condition. The relevant definitions are a small extension of familiar ideals, without localization or scheme theory.

Proof 1: Specialize the module decomposition to the ring

Use the primary-decomposition theorem for modules: every submodule N of a finitely generated module M over a commutative Noetherian ring is a finite intersection of primary submodules. Here a submodule Q\subset M is primary when M/Q has exactly one associated prime, where an associated prime is a prime ideal that is the annihilator of a nonzero element.

A submodule of the R-module R is primary as a submodule if and only if it is a primary ideal. Thus, when M=R and N=I, the module decomposition is precisely a primary decomposition of the ideal I. Both the module theorem and this equivalence are taken as prerequisites.

Level
Beyond undergraduate level
Level justification

Specialize the finite-module decomposition theorem to the regular module and identify primary submodules with primary ideals. Associated primes and primary module decompositions require graduate commutative algebra.
