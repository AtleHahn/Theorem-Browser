Statement

Let A,B be events, write A^c for the complement of A, and assume 0<P(A)<1 and P(B)>0. For events E,F with P(F)>0, conditional probability means P(E\mid F)=P(E\cap F)/P(F). Then

P(A\mid B)=\frac{P(B\mid A)P(A)}{P(B\mid A)P(A)+P(B\mid A^c)P(A^c)}.

High school only

Proof 1: Partition by the event and its complement

Bayes' theorem may be derived from the relation between joint and conditional probabilities. The joint probability of the events {\displaystyle A} and {\displaystyle B} both happening, written {\displaystyle P(A\cap B)}, is equal to the conditional probability of {\displaystyle A} given {\displaystyle B} times the probability of {\displaystyle B}:

{\displaystyle P(A\cap B)=P(A\vert B)P(B).}

Likewise,

{\displaystyle P(A\cap B)=P(B\vert A)P(A).}

The two products must therefore be equal to each other:

{\displaystyle P(A\vert B)P(B)=P(B\vert A)P(A),}

and dividing both sides by {\displaystyle P(B)} gives Bayes' theorem:

{\displaystyle P(A\vert B)={\frac {P(B\vert A)P(A)}{P(B)}},{\text{ if }}P(B)\neq 0.}

The events {\displaystyle A} and not-{\displaystyle A}, written {\displaystyle A^c}, are a mutually exclusive and exhaustive pair. Therefore the law of total probability can be applied in the denominator of Bayes’ formula, and the sum in the denominator will have only two terms:

{\displaystyle P(A|B)={\frac {P(B|A)P(A)}{P(B|A)P(A)+P(B|A^c)P(A^c)}}.}

High school only
Level justification

Use the ratio definition twice for the same intersection, equate the products, and split B into the two disjoint events B intersect A and B intersect A complement. All steps are elementary probability and algebra, and the positive-probability assumptions justify division.
