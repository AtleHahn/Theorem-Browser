Statement

Let (\lambda_n) be finite complex measures on a measurable space (S,\mathcal B) such that \lambda_n(B) has a finite complex limit for every B\in\mathcal B. The setwise limit \lambda is a finite complex measure, and \sup_n\|\lambda_n\|_{TV}<\infty. For every finite positive measure m with \lambda_n\ll m for all n, the family is uniformly absolutely continuous with respect to m and \lambda\ll m. Writing f_n=d\lambda_n/dm and f=d\lambda/dm, the family (f_n) is uniformly integrable and f_n\to f weakly in L^1(m), that is, \int gf_n\,dm\to\int gf\,dm for every bounded measurable g. Such a finite dominating measure always exists.

Beyond undergraduate level
Level justification

The full conclusion concerns complex measures, uniform integrability of densities and weak measure-theoretic convergence.

Proof 1: Complete-measure-algebra and Baire argument outline

Write |\nu| for the variation of a complex measure and \|\nu\|_{TV}=|\nu|(S). Use the standard estimate

|\nu(B)|\leq|\nu|(B)\leq4\sup_{A\subseteq B}|\nu(A)|,

where the supremum is over measurable subsets. Absolute continuity with respect to the finite measure m is equivalent to: for every \varepsilon>0 there is \delta>0 such that m(B)<\delta implies |\nu(B)|<\varepsilon.

Let {\displaystyle m} be a finite measure on {\displaystyle (S,{\mathcal {B}})} satisfying {\displaystyle \lambda _{n}\ll m} for all {\displaystyle n\geq 1}. If none is given, take for instance

{\displaystyle m=\sum _{n\geq 1}{\frac {|\lambda _{n}|}{2^{n}(1+\lVert \lambda _{n}\rVert _{TV})}}\,,}

This is a finite positive measure dominating every \lambda_n.

Complete metric of measurable sets

Let {\displaystyle (S,{\mathcal {B}},m)} be a measure space and write {\displaystyle {\mathcal {B}}_{0}} to denote the set of measurable sets {\displaystyle B\in {\mathcal {B}}} with {\displaystyle m(B)<\infty }:

{\displaystyle {\mathcal {B}}_{0}=\{B\in {\mathcal {B}}:m(B)<\infty \}.}

Write {\displaystyle B_{1}\mathbin {\vartriangle } B_{2}=(B_{1}\setminus B_{2})\cup (B_{2}\setminus B_{1})} to denote the symmetric difference of the sets {\displaystyle B_{1},B_{2}}. Define the equivalence relation {\displaystyle \approx } on {\displaystyle {\mathcal {B}}_{0}} by {\displaystyle B_{1}\approx B_{2}} if {\displaystyle m(B_{1}\mathbin {\vartriangle } B_{2})=0}. Write {\displaystyle {\bar {\mathcal {B}}}_{0}={\mathcal {B}}_{0}/{\mathord {\approx }}} to denote the quotient set of {\displaystyle {\mathcal {B}}_{0}} with respect to the equivalence relation {\displaystyle \approx }:

{\displaystyle {\bar {\mathcal {B}}}_{0}=\{{\bar {B}}:B\in {\mathcal {B}}_{0}\},}

where {\displaystyle {\bar {B}}=\{A\in {\mathcal {B}}_{0}:A\approx B\}} is the equivalence class of {\displaystyle B}. Then, {\displaystyle m} induces a well-defined quotient map on {\displaystyle {\bar {\mathcal {B}}}_{0}} which is denoted by {\displaystyle {\bar {m}}}: {\displaystyle {\bar {m}}({\bar {B}})=m(B)}.

Proposition: Define {\displaystyle d_{m}\colon {\bar {\mathcal {B}}}_{0}\times {\bar {\mathcal {B}}}_{0}\to \mathbb {R} } by

{\displaystyle d_{m}({\bar {B}}_{1},{\bar {B}}_{2})=m(B_{1}\mathbin {\vartriangle } B_{2}).}

Then, {\displaystyle d_{m}} is well-defined and {\displaystyle ({\bar {\mathcal {B}}}_{0},d_{m})} is a complete metric space.

Using indicator functions, we have

{\displaystyle d_{m}({\bar {B}}_{1},{\bar {B}}_{2})=m(B_{1}\mathbin {\vartriangle } B_{2})=\int |\mathbf {1} _{B_{1}}-\mathbf {1} _{B_{2}}|\,dm=\lVert \mathbf {1} _{B_{1}}-\mathbf {1} _{B_{2}}\rVert _{1}.}

This means that the metric space {\displaystyle ({\bar {\mathcal {B}}}_{0},d_{m})} can be identified with a subset of the space {\displaystyle L^{1}(S,{\mathcal {B}},m)} which is a Banach space. Hence, {\displaystyle d_{m}} is a well-defined metric. Consider a Cauchy sequence {\displaystyle ({\bar {B}}_{n})_{n\geq 1}}. Then,

{\displaystyle \lim _{k,n\to \infty }d_{m}({\bar {B}}_{k},{\bar {B}}_{n})=\lim _{k,n\to \infty }\lVert \mathbf {1} _{B_{k}}-\mathbf {1} _{B_{n}}\rVert _{1}=0.}

Hence, we can choose a subsequence {\displaystyle ({\bar {B}}_{n_{k}})_{k\geq 1}} such that {\displaystyle (\mathbf {1} _{B_{n_{k}}})_{k\geq 1}} converges almost everywhere. Define {\displaystyle g\colon S\to \mathbb {R} } by

{\displaystyle g(x)=\liminf _{k\to \infty }\mathbf {1} _{B_{n_{k}}}(x).}

Then, {\displaystyle g} is measurable, has values in {\displaystyle \{0,1\}} almost everywhere and

{\displaystyle \lim _{k\to \infty }\lVert \mathbf {1} _{B_{n_{k}}}-g\rVert _{1}=0.}

Therefore, {\displaystyle g=\mathbf {1} _{B_{\infty }}} almost everywhere for some {\displaystyle B_{\infty }\in {\mathcal {B}}_{0}} and

{\displaystyle \lim _{k\to \infty }d_{m}({\bar {B}}_{n_{k}},{\bar {B}}_{\infty })=\lim _{k\to \infty }\lVert \mathbf {1} _{B_{n_{k}}}-\mathbf {1} _{B_{\infty }}\rVert _{1}=0,}

that is, the Cauchy sequence {\displaystyle ({\bar {B}}_{n})_{n\geq 1}} contains a convergent subsequence and is convergent itself. Therefore, {\displaystyle {\bar {\mathcal {B}}}_{0}} is complete. We remark that {\displaystyle B_{\infty }} is explicitly given by the limit inferior of the sequence {\displaystyle (B_{n_{k}})_{k\geq 1}}:

{\displaystyle B_{\infty }=\liminf _{k\to \infty }B_{n_{k}}=\bigcup _{k=1}^{\infty }\bigcap _{j=k}^{\infty }B_{n_{j}}.}

Baire argument

Since {\displaystyle m} is finite, we have {\displaystyle {\mathcal {B}}_{0}={\mathcal {B}}}. Furthermore, we write {\displaystyle {\bar {\mathcal {B}}}} for the quotient set {\displaystyle {\bar {\mathcal {B}}}_{0}} above. Each {\displaystyle \lambda _{n}} defines a quotient map {\displaystyle {\bar {\lambda }}_{n}} on {\displaystyle {\bar {\mathcal {B}}}} by {\displaystyle {\bar {\lambda }}_{n}({\bar {B}})=\lambda _{n}(B)}. This quotient map {\displaystyle {\bar {\lambda }}_{n}} is well-defined: If {\displaystyle A\in {\bar {B}}}, then {\displaystyle m(A\mathbin {\vartriangle } B)=0} and thus {\displaystyle |\lambda _{n}|(A\mathbin {\vartriangle } B)=0}, since {\displaystyle \lambda _{n}\ll m}. Therefore, {\displaystyle \lambda _{n}(A)=\lambda _{n}(B)}. Moreover, {\displaystyle {\bar {\lambda }}_{n}} is continuous as a function from the metric space {\displaystyle ({\bar {\mathcal {B}}},d_{m})} to {\displaystyle \mathbb {C} }.

Fix {\displaystyle \varepsilon >0}. For every {\displaystyle n,k\geq 1} the function {\displaystyle |{\bar {\lambda }}_{n}-{\bar {\lambda }}_{n+k}|} is again continuous, so that

{\displaystyle F_{n,k,\varepsilon }=\left\{{\bar {B}}\in {\bar {\mathcal {B}}}\,:\,|{\bar {\lambda }}_{n}({\bar {B}})-{\bar {\lambda }}_{n+k}({\bar {B}})|\leq \varepsilon \right\}}

is a closed subset of {\displaystyle {\bar {\mathcal {B}}}}. Consequently,

{\displaystyle F_{n,\varepsilon }=\bigcap _{k\geq 1}F_{n,k,\varepsilon }=\left\{{\bar {B}}\in {\bar {\mathcal {B}}}\,:\,\sup _{k\geq 1}|{\bar {\lambda }}_{n}({\bar {B}})-{\bar {\lambda }}_{n+k}({\bar {B}})|\leq \varepsilon \right\}}

is also a closed subset of {\displaystyle {\bar {\mathcal {B}}}}. By the hypothesis the sequence {\displaystyle (\lambda _{n}(B))_{n\geq 1}} converges for every {\displaystyle B\in {\mathcal {B}}}, so that

{\displaystyle \sup _{k\geq 1}|{\bar {\lambda }}_{n}({\bar {B}})-{\bar {\lambda }}_{n+k}({\bar {B}})|=\sup _{k\geq 1}|\lambda _{n}(B)-\lambda _{n+k}(B)|\to 0.}

Therefore, we obtain

{\displaystyle {\bar {\mathcal {B}}}=\bigcup _{n=1}^{\infty }F_{n,\varepsilon }.}

By Baire category theorem at least one of the sets {\displaystyle (F_{n,\varepsilon })_{n\geq 1}} must contain a non-empty open set of {\displaystyle {\bar {\mathcal {B}}}}. This means that there is {\displaystyle n_{0}\geq 1} and {\displaystyle B_{0}\in {\mathcal {B}}} and an {\displaystyle r_{0}>0} such that {\displaystyle F_{n_{0},\varepsilon }} contains the open ball in {\displaystyle {\bar {\mathcal {B}}}} with center {\displaystyle {\bar {B}}_{0}} and radius {\displaystyle r_{0}}:

{\displaystyle \{{\bar {B}}\in {\bar {\mathcal {B}}}:d_{m}({\bar {B}},{\bar {B}}_{0})<r_{0}\}\subseteq F_{n_{0},\varepsilon }.}

In other words:

{\displaystyle d_{m}({\bar {B}},{\bar {B}}_{0})<r_{0}\quad \Longrightarrow \quad \sup _{k\geq 1}|{\bar {\lambda }}_{n_{0}}({\bar {B}})-{\bar {\lambda }}_{n_{0}+k}({\bar {B}})|\leq \varepsilon .}

Since {\displaystyle \lambda _{n}\ll m} for all {\displaystyle n\leq n_{0}}, there is a {\displaystyle \delta >0}, so that {\displaystyle m(B)<\delta } implies {\displaystyle |\lambda _{n}(B)|<\varepsilon } for all {\displaystyle n\leq n_{0}}. We may choose {\displaystyle \delta >0} sufficiently small, so that {\displaystyle \delta \leq r_{0}}.

Now, consider a measurable set {\displaystyle B\in {\mathcal {B}}} with {\displaystyle m(B)<\delta }. Define {\displaystyle B_{1}=B\cup B_{0}} and {\displaystyle B_{2}=B_{0}\setminus B}. Then, we have

{\displaystyle B=(B\cup B_{0})\setminus (B_{0}\setminus B)=B_{1}\setminus B_{2}}

and

{\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}d_{m}({\bar {B}}_{1},{\bar {B}}_{0})&=m(B_{1}\mathbin {\vartriangle } B_{0})=m((B\cup B_{0})\mathbin {\vartriangle } B_{0})\leq m(B)<\delta ,\\d_{m}({\bar {B}}_{2},{\bar {B}}_{0})&=m(B_{2}\mathbin {\vartriangle } B_{0})=m((B_{0}\setminus B)\mathbin {\vartriangle } B_{0})\leq m(B)<\delta .\end{aligned}}}

Thus, if {\displaystyle n\leq n_{0}}, then {\displaystyle |\lambda _{n}(B)|<\varepsilon } and, if {\displaystyle n>n_{0}}, then

{\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}|\lambda _{n}(B)|&\leq |\lambda _{n_{0}}(B)|+|\lambda _{n_{0}}(B)-\lambda _{n}(B)|\\&\leq |\lambda _{n_{0}}(B)|+|\lambda _{n_{0}}(B_{1})-\lambda _{n}(B_{1})|+|\lambda _{n_{0}}(B_{2})-\lambda _{n}(B_{2})|\\&<3\epsilon \end{aligned}}}

Therefore, {\displaystyle (\lambda _{n})_{n\geq 1}} is uniformly absolutely continuous with respect to {\displaystyle m}.

Limit measure and bounded variations

Let {\displaystyle m} be a measure on {\displaystyle (S,{\mathcal {B}})}. Fix some {\displaystyle \delta >0}. A set {\displaystyle B\in {\mathcal {B}}} is called {\displaystyle \delta }-indivisible, if it cannot be partitioned into two disjoint measurable subsets {\displaystyle B_{1},B_{2}\in {\mathcal {B}}} satisfying {\displaystyle m(B_{1})\geq \delta } and {\displaystyle m(B_{2})\geq \delta }.

Proposition: Let {\displaystyle m} be a finite measure on {\displaystyle (S,{\mathcal {B}})} and fix some {\displaystyle \delta >0}. Then, there exists a partition {\displaystyle \pi } of {\displaystyle S} into at most {\displaystyle \lfloor m(S)/\delta \rfloor +1} disjoint {\displaystyle \delta }-indivisible, measurable sets.

Start with the partition {\displaystyle \pi _{1}=\{S\}}. Assume that {\displaystyle \pi _{n}} is already defined. If all sets in {\displaystyle \pi _{n}} are already {\displaystyle \delta }-indivisible, then set {\displaystyle \pi _{n+1}=\pi _{n}}. If not, pick one set {\displaystyle B\in \pi _{n}} that is not {\displaystyle \delta }-indivisible. Then, {\displaystyle B} can be partitioned into two disjoint measurable subsets {\displaystyle B_{1},B_{2}\in {\mathcal {B}}} satisfying {\displaystyle m(B_{1})\geq \delta } and {\displaystyle m(B_{2})\geq \delta }. In this case, set {\displaystyle \pi _{n+1}=(\pi _{n}\setminus \{B\})\cup \{B_{1},B_{2}\}}. This process must stabilize after at most {\displaystyle \lfloor m(S)/\delta \rfloor } splittings, since each splitting creates a new set of measure at least {\displaystyle \delta }.

By the linearity of the limit {\displaystyle \lambda } is finitely additive. And by uniformly absolute continuity of {\displaystyle (\lambda _{n})_{n\geq 1}} the limit {\displaystyle \lambda } is actually countably additive. Here one uses continuity at the empty set for a finitely additive function and the finiteness of m.

Next, we prove boundedness of the total variations: Fix {\displaystyle \varepsilon =1}. By uniformly absolute continuity of {\displaystyle (\lambda _{n})_{n\geq 1}} there is {\displaystyle \delta >0} such that {\displaystyle m(B)<\delta } implies {\displaystyle |\lambda _{n}(B)|<1}. By the finite-partition construction just given, there is a finite partition {\displaystyle \pi } of {\displaystyle S} into {\displaystyle \delta }-indivisible sets with respect to the finite measure {\displaystyle m}. For any {\displaystyle B\in \pi } and any measurable {\displaystyle A\subseteq B}, we cannot have both, {\displaystyle m(A)\geq \delta } and {\displaystyle m(B\setminus A)\geq \delta }. If {\displaystyle m(A)<\delta }, then, by uniformly absolute continuity,

{\displaystyle |\lambda _{n}(A)|<1\leq |\lambda _{n}(B)|+1}

for all {\displaystyle n\geq 1}. If, on the other hand {\displaystyle m(B\setminus A)<\delta }, then, again by uniform absolute continuity, {\displaystyle |\lambda _{n}(B\setminus A)|<1} for all {\displaystyle n\geq 1}, which implies

{\displaystyle |\lambda _{n}(A)|=|\lambda _{n}(B)-\lambda _{n}(B\setminus A)|\leq |\lambda _{n}(B)|+|\lambda _{n}(B\setminus A)|<|\lambda _{n}(B)|+1}

for all {\displaystyle n\geq 1}. In both cases, for every measurable subset {\displaystyle A\subseteq B} and every {\displaystyle n\geq 1}:

{\displaystyle |\lambda _{n}(A)|<|\lambda _{n}(B)|+1}

Thus,

{\displaystyle |\lambda _{n}|(B)\leq 4\sup _{A\subseteq B}|\lambda _{n}(A)|\leq 4|\lambda _{n}(B)|+4}

Since {\displaystyle (\lambda _{n}(B))_{n\geq 1}} converges for every {\displaystyle B\in \pi } and {\displaystyle \pi } is finite, we have

{\displaystyle C=\max _{B\in \pi }\sup _{n\geq 1}|\lambda _{n}(B)|<\infty }

and therefore

{\displaystyle \lVert \lambda _{n}\rVert _{TV}=\sum _{B\in \pi }|\lambda _{n}|(B)\leq \sum _{B\in \pi }(4|\lambda _{n}(B)|+4)\leq (4C+4)|\pi |<\infty .}

Densities and weak convergence

For convergence of bounded integrals, we first note that, by linearity of limits, the sequence {\displaystyle \textstyle (\int s\,d\lambda _{n})_{n\geq 1}} converges for all measurable simple functions {\displaystyle s\colon S\to \mathbb {C} }. Write {\displaystyle K} for the bound of total variations:

{\displaystyle K=\sup _{n\geq 1}\lVert \lambda _{n}\rVert _{TV}.}

Given a bounded measurable function {\displaystyle g\colon S\to \mathbb {C} } and an {\displaystyle \varepsilon >0} there is a measurable simple function {\displaystyle s\colon S\to \mathbb {C} } with {\displaystyle \lVert g-s\rVert _{\infty }<\varepsilon }. Furthermore, {\displaystyle \textstyle (\int s\,d\lambda _{n})_{n\geq 1}} converges, thus there exists {\displaystyle N} such that

{\displaystyle \left|\int s\,d\lambda _{k}-\int s\,d\lambda _{n}\right|<\varepsilon }

for all {\displaystyle k,n>N}. Then,

{\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}\left|\int g\,d\lambda _{k}-\int g\,d\lambda _{n}\right|&\leq \left|\int g\,d\lambda _{k}-\int s\,d\lambda _{k}\right|+\left|\int s\,d\lambda _{k}-\int s\,d\lambda _{n}\right|+\left|\int s\,d\lambda _{n}-\int g\,d\lambda _{n}\right|\\&\leq \int |g-s|\,d|\lambda _{k}|+\left|\int s\,d\lambda _{k}-\int s\,d\lambda _{n}\right|+\int |g-s|\,d|\lambda _{n}|\\&<\varepsilon (2K+1)\end{aligned}}}

for all {\displaystyle k,n>N}. Consequently, the sequence {\displaystyle \textstyle (\int g\,d\lambda _{n})_{n\geq 1}} converges.

The same simple-function approximation identifies the limit as \int g\,d\lambda. By Radon–Nikodym, the densities f_n=d\lambda_n/dm and f=d\lambda/dm exist. The remaining assertions follow from this bounded-integral argument and boundedness of all total variations. Here one uses the finite-measure uniform-integrability criterion: bounded L^1 norms and uniform absolute continuity of integrals imply uniform integrability. The source leaves these final consequences compressed.
