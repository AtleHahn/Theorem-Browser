Statement

For a fixed circular word with p>q\ge0 letters A and B, respectively, exactly p-q of its p+q starting positions give a linear word whose every nonempty prefix has more A’s than B’s. Starting positions are counted separately even when resulting words coincide.

High school only
Level justification

Starting positions and prefixes are explicitly finite combinatorial objects.

Proof 1: Cancel adjacent AB pairs — outline

Call a word dominating when every nonempty prefix contains more A’s than B’s.

The cycle lemma asserts that any sequence of {\displaystyle p} A's and {\displaystyle q} B's, where {\displaystyle p>q}, has precisely {\displaystyle p-q} dominating cyclic permutations. To see this, just arrange the given sequence of {\displaystyle p+q} A's and B's in a circle and repeatedly remove adjacent pairs AB until only {\displaystyle p-q} A's remain. Each of these A's was the start of a dominating cyclic permutation before anything was removed.

Level
High school only
Level justification

The cancellation argument uses finite circular words and prefix counts. Removing an adjacent AB pair preserves the good starting positions among surviving letters: inserting AB changes the running surplus temporarily by one and then restores it. Neither removed position is good, since starting at B immediately fails and starting at A gives surplus zero after AB. Thus the remaining p-q letters account for all good positions. These details require only elementary counting; positions remain distinct even for periodic words.
