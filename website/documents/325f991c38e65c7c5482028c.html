Statement

Graphs are finite and simple. A clique is a set of pairwise adjacent vertices; its maximum size is the clique number. A proper coloring assigns different colors to adjacent vertices; the least number of colors is the chromatic number. An induced subgraph keeps a vertex subset and all edges between its vertices. A graph is perfect if these two numbers agree for every induced subgraph. A graph is bipartite if its vertices split into two sets with no edge inside either set. The line graph has the original edges as vertices, with two adjacent exactly when they share an endpoint. The line graph of every bipartite graph is perfect.

Level
High school only
Level justification

All finite graph notions, including the line graph and perfection over induced subgraphs, are explicitly defined by adjacency, subsets and coloring counts.

Proof 1: Translate edge colorings and common-endpoint cliques

Kőnig’s line coloring theorem applies to the line graphs of bipartite graphs. If G is a graph, the line graph L(G) has a vertex for each edge of G, and an edge for each pair of adjacent edges in G. Thus, the chromatic number of L(G) equals the chromatic index of G. If G is bipartite, the cliques in L(G) are exactly the sets of edges in G sharing a common endpoint. Now Kőnig's line coloring theorem, stating that the chromatic index equals the maximum vertex degree in any bipartite graph, can be interpreted as stating that the line graph of a bipartite graph is perfect.

Every induced subgraph of L(G) is the line graph of the bipartite subgraph obtained by restricting to the corresponding edges, so the same equality holds for every induced subgraph.
