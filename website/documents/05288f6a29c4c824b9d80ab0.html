Statement

Let K be an algebraically closed field, n\ge1 an integer, and A\in K^{n\times n}. For an eigenvalue \lambda, the dimension of \ker(A-\lambda I) is at most the multiplicity of \lambda as a root of \det(A-tI).

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Matrices, determinants, kernels, linear independence and bases are standard undergraduate linear algebra. Over an algebraically closed field, the extra condition simply guarantees polynomial roots; infinite dimensions mean cardinalities of bases. The specific matrix or dimension claim adds no specialist construction.

Proof 1: Include the eigenspace in the generalized eigenspace

Recall that the geometric multiplicity of an eigenvalue can be described as the dimension of the associated eigenspace, the nullspace of A-\lambda I. By the generalized-eigenspace dimension theorem, the algebraic multiplicity can also be thought of as a dimension: it is the dimension of the associated generalized eigenspace, which is the nullspace of the matrix (A-\lambda I)^k for any sufficiently large k. That is, it is the space of generalized eigenvectors, where a generalized eigenvector is any vector which eventually becomes 0 if A-\lambda I is applied to it enough times successively. Any eigenvector is a generalized eigenvector, and so each eigenspace is contained in the associated generalized eigenspace. This provides a proof that the geometric multiplicity is always less than or equal to the algebraic multiplicity.
