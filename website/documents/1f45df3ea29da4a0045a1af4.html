Statement

Let a transversal meet two distinct lines in a Euclidean or hyperbolic plane at distinct points. At each intersection, call the angles opening toward the other intersection interior; the other two are exterior. Alternate interior angles are interior angles on opposite sides of the transversal. Corresponding angles lie on the same side of the transversal, one interior and one exterior. Consecutive interior angles are interior angles on the same side. Parallel here means nonintersecting. If one pair of alternate interior angles is equal, the two lines are parallel.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Angle positions and parallel as nonintersection are locally defined. The additional hyperbolic-plane incidence and angle setting is a short geometric extension of undergraduate knowledge; the statement does not require a model construction or curvature theory.

Proof 1: Contradict the exterior-angle theorem

Euclid proves this by contradiction: If the lines are not parallel then they must intersect and a triangle is formed. Then one of the alternate angles is an exterior angle equal to the other angle which is an opposite interior angle in the triangle. This contradicts Euclid’s exterior-angle theorem (Proposition 16) which states that an exterior angle of a triangle is always greater than the opposite interior angles.

Level justification
