Statement

Let G be a finite simple undirected graph. Then G is planar if and only if it contains no subgraph that is a subdivision of K_5 or K_{3,3}.

Planar means that the graph can be drawn in the plane with edge interiors disjoint from one another and from all vertices. The graph K_5 has five vertices with an edge between every pair; K_{3,3} has two sets of three vertices and all nine edges between the two sets. A subdivision replaces each edge by a path of one or more edges, with new internal vertices distinct for different edges. A subgraph may be obtained by deleting vertices and edges.

Proof 1: Wagner’s characterization and the stated minor–subdivision conversion — outline

A minor is obtained by deleting vertices or edges and contracting edges. Use Wagner’s theorem, stated in the source: a finite graph is planar exactly when it has neither K_5 nor K_{3,3} as a minor. Also use the source-stated conversion fact: from a K_5 or K_{3,3} minor one can find a subdivision of one of those two graphs as a subgraph. The type of the subdivision need not be the same as the type of the minor. The source does not prove this conversion fact.

If G is nonplanar, Wagner’s theorem supplies a forbidden minor; the conversion fact supplies a forbidden subdivision.

Conversely, the source uses the nonplanarity of K_5 and K_{3,3}. Subdivision cannot make either graph planar: a planar drawing of its subdivision would give a planar drawing of the original graph by treating each subdivided path as one edge. Thus a graph containing either forbidden subdivision cannot be planar.

These two implications give the characterization. This is a deduction from the stated external premises, not a proof of Wagner’s theorem or of the minor–subdivision conversion.
