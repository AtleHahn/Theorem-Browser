Statement

Let p\in\mathbb Z[x] be a nonzero square-free polynomial (one with no repeated complex root) and let 0<a<b. For an interval (u,v)\subseteq(a,b) or the initial interval itself, count variations of (1+x)^{\deg p}p((u+vx)/(1+x)). Discard it when the count is zero, retain it as isolating when the count is one, and otherwise bisect at m=(u+v)/2, recording the singleton \{m\} if p(m)=0 and recursing on the two open halves. This procedure terminates and partitions the roots in (a,b) into single-root intervals and recorded singleton roots.

Proof 1: Root-accounting recursion and uniform short-interval termination outline

Use Vincent's bisection theorem: for a real polynomial p that has only simple roots, there is a positive quantity \delta so that for every pair of positive real numbers u<v with v-u<\delta, the transformed polynomial

(1+x)^{\deg(p)}p\left(\frac{u+vx}{1+x}\right)

has exactly 0 or 1 sign variations.

The Alesina–Galuzzi roots test, by Descartes' rule of signs, gives an upper bound on the number of roots in the open interval: if the number of sign variations is 0, there are no roots, and if it is 1, there is one root.

Here is how VAG(p,(a,b)) works on the given positive interval:

  • Repeat the following steps while there are intervals (a,b) to be processed; in this case the polynomial p(x) stays the same.
  • Use the Alesina–Galuzzi roots test on p(x) to count the sign variations. If the count is zero return the empty set, \varnothing, and if the count is one return the interval (a,b).
  • If there are two or more sign variations, there may be zero, one, two or more real roots inside the interval (a,b). In this case cut it in half and consider separately the roots of p(x) inside the interval (a,\tfrac12(a+b)) from those inside the interval (\tfrac12(a+b),b); that is, process, respectively, these two intervals. It may well turn out that \tfrac12(a+b) is a root of p(x), in which case it is recorded as a singleton in addition to processing the two open halves.

The bisection theorem guarantees termination as the interval lengths decrease under repeated halving. The source leaves this termination deduction implicit in its algorithm; it does not give a separate finite-depth calculation.
