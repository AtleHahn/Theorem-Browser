Likely alternative prerequisite level: High school only; lowest recorded proof level: Beyond undergraduate level. For nonnegative integer s,t the identity counts n-subsets of two disjoint sets. For fixed n both sides are polynomials in s,t. Fixing each nonnegative integer t extends equality to every complex s by the elementary finite-degree root bound; then fixing s extends it to all complex t. The falling-factorial version follows by multiplying by n!, including n=0. No analytic logarithms or infinite binomial series occur.

Statement

For s,t\in\mathbb C and an integer n\ge0, define (z)_j=z(z-1)\cdots(z-j+1), (z)_0=1, and \binom zj=(z)_j/j!. Then \binom{s+t}{n}=\sum_{k=0}^n\binom sk\binom t{n-k}. Equivalently, (s+t)_n=\sum_{k=0}^n\binom nk(s)_k(t)_{n-k}.

High school only

Proof 1: Multiplication of binomial series

Use the generalized binomial theorem (1+x)^z=\sum_{j\ge0}\binom zjx^j for |x|<1, with a common analytic logarithm defining the powers.

It can be proved by multiplying the binomial series for {\displaystyle (1+x)^{s}} and {\displaystyle (1+x)^{t}} and comparing terms with the binomial series for {\displaystyle (1+x)^{s+t}}.

This identity may be rewritten in terms of the falling Pochhammer symbols as

{\displaystyle (s+t)_{n}=\sum _{k=0}^{n}{n \choose k}(s)_{k}(t)_{n-k}}

Level justification
