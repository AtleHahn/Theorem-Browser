Statement

Let p be an odd prime. Suppose there is a prime q such that no two nonzero pth-power residues modulo q differ by 1, and p is not a pth-power residue modulo q. Explicitly, there are no integers u,v with q\nmid uv and u^p-v^p\equiv1\pmod q, and no integer w with w^p\equiv p\pmod q.

If positive integers x,y,z satisfy x^p+y^p=z^p, then at least one of x,y,z is divisible by p^2. In particular, no such solution has p\nmid xyz (the first case of Fermat's Last Theorem).

Proof 1: Coprime factorization and congruences modulo the prime square (external)

A link to the proof can be found here (Section3, strong original theorem and proof, pp.9–10; Claim4, pp.7–8).

The source assumes q=2np+1. This follows here: the second hypothesis excludes q=p and q=2, and if p\nmid q-1, Bézout's identity and Fermat's little theorem make the pth-power map modulo q bijective. Divide a solution by its common gcd; any prime dividing two coordinates then divides the third, so the resulting coordinates are pairwise coprime. Permutations and sign changes are legitimate because p is odd. The coprime-factor step uses prime exponents, not the source's incidental assertion that a pth power cannot also be another power. The final binomial calculation establishes p^2 divisibility for the primitive solution and hence for the original one.
