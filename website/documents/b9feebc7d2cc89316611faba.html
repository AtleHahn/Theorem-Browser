Statement

Let n\ge1 be an integer and let permutations act on \{1,\ldots,n\}. If x_1<\cdots<x_n and y_1\le\cdots\le y_n are real, the maximum of \sum_i x_i y_{\sigma(i)} is attained exactly by permutations whose y-values remain nondecreasing.

Level
High school only
Level justification

Maximizing a finite real sum over finite reorderings requires only elementary ordering and permutations.

Proof 1: Contradict maximality with a strict exchange

Since there are only finitely many permutations of 1,\ldots,n, there exists at least one \sigma for which

x_1y_{\sigma(1)}+\cdots+x_ny_{\sigma(n)}

is maximal.

We will now prove by contradiction that \sigma has to keep the order of y_1,\ldots,y_n. Assume that there exists a j\in\{1,\ldots,n-1\} such that y_i=y_{\sigma(i)} for all i\in\{1,\ldots,j-1\} and y_j\ne y_{\sigma(j)}. Hence y_j<y_{\sigma(j)} and there has to exist a k\in\{j+1,\ldots,n\} with y_j=y_{\sigma(k)} to fill the gap. Therefore,

x_j<x_k\qquad\text{and}\qquad y_{\sigma(k)}<y_{\sigma(j)},

which implies that

0<(x_k-x_j)(y_{\sigma(j)}-y_{\sigma(k)}).

Expanding this product and rearranging gives

x_jy_{\sigma(j)}+x_ky_{\sigma(k)} <x_jy_{\sigma(k)}+x_ky_{\sigma(j)}.

Hence the permutation

\tau(i)= \begin{cases} \sigma(i)&\text{for }i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}\setminus\{j,k\},\\ \sigma(k)&\text{for }i=j,\\ \sigma(j)&\text{for }i=k, \end{cases}

which arises from \sigma by exchanging the values \sigma(j) and \sigma(k), gives a strictly larger sum, contradicting maximality. Thus the maximum can only be attained by permutations keeping the order of y_1\le\cdots\le y_n, and every other permutation cannot be optimal. All permutations keeping that order give the same sum.
