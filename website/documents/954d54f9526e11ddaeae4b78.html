Statement

For a nonzero real polynomial f, let V(f) count sign changes between successive nonzero coefficients in descending exponent order, and let Z(f) count positive real roots with multiplicity (a root has multiplicity k when (x-r)^k is its exact power factor). Then V(f)-Z(f) is a nonnegative even integer.

Level
High school only
Level justification

The claim compares two explicitly defined finite counts: coefficient-sign variations and positive roots with their algebraic multiplicities.

Proof 1: Induct using Rolle and root-count parity — outline

The following is a rough outline of a proof. First, some preliminary definitions:

  • Write the polynomial {\displaystyle f(x)} as {\displaystyle \sum _{i=0}^{n}a_{i}x^{b_{i}}} where we have integer powers {\displaystyle 0\leq b_{0}<b_{1}<\cdots <b_{n}}, and nonzero coefficients {\displaystyle a_{i}\neq 0}.

  • Let {\displaystyle V(f)} be the number of sign changes of the coefficients of {\displaystyle f}, meaning the number of {\displaystyle k} such that {\displaystyle a_{k}a_{k+1}<0}.

  • Let {\displaystyle Z(f)} be the number of strictly positive roots (counting multiplicity).

If {\displaystyle b_{0}>0}, then we can divide the polynomial by {\displaystyle x^{b_{0}}}, which would not change its number of strictly positive roots. Thus without loss of generality, let {\displaystyle b_{0}=0}.

Local parity lemma

Lemma—If {\displaystyle a_{n}a_{0}>0}, then {\displaystyle Z(f)} is even. If {\displaystyle a_{0}a_{n}<0}, then {\displaystyle Z(f)} is odd.

Proof of Lemma

The constant case is immediate. In the first case for a nonconstant polynomial, multiply f by -1 if necessary so that a_0,a_n>0.

{\displaystyle f(x)} starts at {\displaystyle f(0)=a_{0}>0} and ends at {\displaystyle f(+\infty )=+\infty >0}, so it must cross the positive x-axis an even number of times (each of which contributes an odd number of roots), and each instance where it glances off (without crossing) the positive x-axis contributes an even number of roots.

The other case is similar.

Induction on the number of nonzero terms

From the lemma, it follows that {\displaystyle Z(f)} and {\displaystyle V(f)} always have the same parity. It remains to show {\displaystyle Z(f)\leq V(f)}.

We induct on {\displaystyle n}. If {\displaystyle n=0,1}, then it is obvious. Now assume {\displaystyle n\geq 2}.

The derivative has one fewer nonzero term. By induction hypothesis, {\displaystyle Z(f')=V(f')-2s} for some integer {\displaystyle s\geq 0}.

By Rolle's theorem, there exists at least one positive root of {\displaystyle f'} between any two different positive roots of {\displaystyle f}. Also, any {\displaystyle k} -multiple positive root of {\displaystyle f} is a {\displaystyle k-1} -multiple root of {\displaystyle f'}. Thus {\displaystyle Z(f')\geq Z(f)-1}.

If {\displaystyle a_{0}a_{1}>0}, then {\displaystyle V(f')=V(f)}, else {\displaystyle V(f')=V(f)-1}. In both cases, {\displaystyle V(f')\leq V(f)}

Together, we have

{\displaystyle Z(f)\leq Z(f')+1=V(f')-2s+1\leq V(f)-2s+1\leq V(f)+1}

Further, since {\displaystyle Z(f)} and {\displaystyle V(f)} have the same parity, we have {\displaystyle Z(f)\leq V(f)}.
