Statement

On a measure space (S,\Sigma,\mu), write \|h\|_t=(\int_S|h|^t\,d\mu)^{1/t} for 0<t<\infty, and \|h\|_\infty=\operatorname{ess\,sup}|h|. Norms may be infinite; products with a zero factor are defined to be zero, including 0\cdot\infty. For conjugate exponents p,q\in[1,\infty], 1/p+1/q=1 (with 1/\infty=0), all measurable real or complex f,g satisfy \|fg\|_1\le\|f\|_p\|g\|_q.

If additionally 1<p,q<\infty, \|f\|_p<\infty and \|g\|_q<\infty, equality holds if and only if there are real numbers \alpha,\beta\ge0, not both zero, with

\alpha|f|^p=\beta|g|^q\quad\mu\text{-almost everywhere}.

Almost everywhere means outside a set of measure zero, and the essential supremum is the least extended nonnegative bound that holds almost everywhere.

Proof 1: Normalize and integrate Young’s inequality

If ‖f ‖p = 0, then f is zero μ-almost everywhere, and the product fg is zero μ-almost everywhere, hence the left-hand side of Hölder's inequality is zero. The same is true if ‖g‖q = 0. Therefore, we may assume ‖f ‖p > 0 and ‖g‖q > 0 in the following.

If ‖f ‖p = ∞ or ‖g‖q = ∞, then the right-hand side of Hölder's inequality is infinite. Therefore, we may assume that ‖f ‖p and ‖g‖q are in (0, ∞).

If p = ∞ and q = 1, then |fg| ≤ ‖f ‖∞ |g| almost everywhere and Hölder's inequality follows from the monotonicity of the Lebesgue integral. Similarly for p = 1 and q = ∞. Therefore, we may assume p, q ∈ (1,∞).

We now use Young's inequality for products, which states that whenever {\displaystyle p,q} are in (1,∞) with {\displaystyle {\frac {1}{p}}+{\frac {1}{q}}=1}

{\displaystyle ab\leq {\frac {a^{p}}{p}}+{\frac {b^{q}}{q}}}

for all nonnegative a and b, where equality is achieved if and only if ap = bq. Hence

{\displaystyle {\frac {|f(s)|}{\|f\|_{p}}}{\frac {|g(s)|}{\|g\|_{q}}}\leq {\frac {|f(s)|^{p}}{p\|f\|_{p}^{p}}}+{\frac {|g(s)|^{q}}{q\|g\|_{q}^{q}}},\qquad s\in S.}

Integrating both sides gives

{\displaystyle {\frac {\|fg\|_{1}}{||f||_{p}||g||_{q}}}\leq {\frac {\|f\|_{p}^{p}}{p\|f\|_{p}^{p}}}+{\frac {\|g\|_{q}^{q}}{q\|g\|_{q}^{q}}}={\frac {1}{p}}+{\frac {1}{q}}=1,}

which proves the claim.

For the equality clause, use the source Young equality analysis:

For positive norms normalize a=|f|/\|f\|_p and b=|g|/\|g\|_q. Young’s nonnegative difference a^p/p+b^q/q-ab integrates to zero exactly when it vanishes almost everywhere. Young’s equality condition is a^p=b^q, equivalently

\|g\|_q^q|f|^p=\|f\|_p^p|g|^q.

Conversely a nonzero nonnegative proportionality of the two integrable powers gives this normalized equality after integration, hence equality in Hölder. If f=0 almost everywhere, take \alpha=1,\beta=0; if g=0, take \alpha=0,\beta=1. These are exactly the zero-norm cases.

Proof 2: Use a probability density and Jensen’s inequality

If either norm is zero, the corresponding function and product vanish almost everywhere. If neither is zero but one is infinite, the bound is immediate. At the endpoint p=\infty,q=1, use |fg|\le\|f\|_\infty|g| almost everywhere and integrate; the opposite endpoint is symmetric. Thus assume 1<p,q<\infty and positive finite norms, and replace f,g by their moduli.

Work first on T=\{g>0\} with the restricted measure \mu; all integrals in the following calculation are over T. The product fg vanishes outside T, and the integral of f^p over T is at most its integral over S. This reduction avoids undefined negative powers of g.

The function {\displaystyle x\mapsto x^{p}} on [0,∞) is convex because {\displaystyle p\geq 1}, so by Jensen's inequality,

{\displaystyle \int h\mathrm {d} \nu \leq \left(\int h^{p}\mathrm {d} \nu \right)^{\frac {1}{p}}}

where ν is any probability distribution and h any nonnegative ν-measurable function. Let μ be any measure, and ν the distribution whose density w.r.t. μ is proportional to {\displaystyle g^{q}}, i.e.

{\displaystyle \mathrm {d} \nu ={\frac {g^{q}}{\int g^{q}\,\mathrm {d} \mu }}\mathrm {d} \mu }

Hence we have, using {\displaystyle {\frac {1}{p}}+{\frac {1}{q}}=1}, hence {\displaystyle p(1-q)+q=0}, and letting {\displaystyle h=fg^{1-q}},

{\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}\int fg\,\mathrm {d} \mu =&\left(\int g^{q}\,\mathrm {d} \mu \right)\int \underbrace {fg^{1-q}} _{h}\underbrace {{\frac {g^{q}}{\int g^{q}\,\mathrm {d} \mu }}\mathrm {d} \mu } _{\mathrm {d} \nu }\\\leq &\left(\int g^{q}\mathrm {d} \mu \right)\left(\int \underbrace {f^{p}g^{p(1-q)}} _{h^{p}}\underbrace {{\frac {g^{q}}{\int g^{q}\,\mathrm {d} \mu }}\,\mathrm {d} \mu } _{\mathrm {d} \nu }\right)^{\frac {1}{p}}\\=&\left(\int g^{q}\,\mathrm {d} \mu \right)\left(\int {\frac {f^{p}}{\int g^{q}\,\mathrm {d} \mu }}\,\mathrm {d} \mu \right)^{\frac {1}{p}}.\end{aligned}}}

Finally, we get

{\displaystyle \int fg\,\mathrm {d} \mu \leq \left(\int f^{p}\,\mathrm {d} \mu \right)^{\frac {1}{p}}\left(\int g^{q}\,\mathrm {d} \mu \right)^{\frac {1}{q}}}

The comparison of the integrals over T and S gives the required inequality on S.

The source also supplies an alternative scalar route to the probability-measure estimate used in this proof. If \nu(S)=1 and A=\|h\|_p>0 is finite, use

z\le \frac{z^p}{p}+1-\frac1p\quad(z>0).

Putting z=|h|/A gives |h|\le A^{1-p}|h|^p/p+(1-1/p)A (also at h=0). Integration gives \int|h|\,d\nu\le A. The source identifies these constants as the unique choice with sum one and asserts the scalar inequality; its elementary one-variable verification is left implicit. This can replace the Jensen step above.

For the equality clause of the complete statement, supplement the probability-density argument with the source Young equality analysis:

For positive norms normalize a=|f|/\|f\|_p and b=|g|/\|g\|_q. Young’s nonnegative difference a^p/p+b^q/q-ab integrates to zero exactly when it vanishes almost everywhere. Young’s equality condition is a^p=b^q, equivalently

Conversely a nonzero nonnegative proportionality of the two integrable powers gives this normalized equality after integration, hence equality in Hölder. If f=0 almost everywhere, take \alpha=1,\beta=0; if g=0, take \alpha=0,\beta=1. These are exactly the zero-norm cases.
