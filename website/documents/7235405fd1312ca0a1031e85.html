Statement

A dependency graph for events A_1,\ldots,A_n means that each A_i is independent of the sigma-algebra generated by all A_j with j\ne i that are not its neighbours. Write \Gamma(i) for its neighbours. If 0\le x_i<1 and \Pr(A_i)\le x_i\prod_{j\in\Gamma(i)}(1-x_j) for every i, then \Pr(\bigcap_i A_i^c)\ge\prod_i(1-x_i)>0.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

The events form a finite family. The generated sigma-algebra here is only the finite collection of their Boolean combinations, and independence means the usual product rule on those combinations. The dependency graph and explicit finite inequalities add a small extension of elementary finite probability.

Proof 1: Induct on the number of conditioned complementary events

Write \mathcal A for the indexed family A_1,\ldots,A_n, put x(A_i)=x_i, and identify \Gamma(A_i) with the corresponding family of neighbouring events. Complements are denoted by bars.

All conditioning intersections below have positive probability. Indeed, once the conditional bounds for sets of size less than r hold, the probability chain rule bounds any intersection of r complementary events below by the product of its positive factors 1-x(A). This starts with the empty intersection, of probability one.

By using the principle of mathematical induction we prove that for all {\displaystyle A} in {\displaystyle {\mathcal {A}}} and all subsets {\displaystyle S} of {\displaystyle {\mathcal {A}}} that do not include {\displaystyle A}, {\displaystyle \Pr \left(A\mid \bigwedge _{B\in S}{\overline {B}}\right)\leq x(A)}. The induction here is applied on the size (cardinality) of the set {\displaystyle S}. For base case {\displaystyle S=\emptyset } the statement obviously holds since {\displaystyle \Pr(A_{i})\leq x\left(A_{i}\right)}. We need to show that the inequality holds for any subset of {\displaystyle {\mathcal {A}}} of a certain cardinality given that it holds for all subsets of a lower cardinality.

Let {\displaystyle S_{1}=S\cap \Gamma (A),S_{2}=S\setminus S_{1}}. If S_1 is empty, joint independence gives \Pr(A\mid\bigwedge_{B\in S}\overline B)=\Pr(A)\le x(A). Otherwise, we have from Bayes' theorem

{\displaystyle \Pr \left(A\mid \bigwedge _{B\in S}{\overline {B}}\right)={\frac {\Pr \left(A\wedge \bigwedge _{B\in S_{1}}{\overline {B}}\mid \bigwedge _{B\in S_{2}}{\overline {B}}\right)}{\Pr \left(\bigwedge _{B\in S_{1}}{\overline {B}}\mid \bigwedge _{B\in S_{2}}{\overline {B}}\right)}}.}

We bound the numerator and denominator of the above expression separately. For this, let {\displaystyle S_{1}=\{B_{j1},B_{j2},\ldots ,B_{jl}\}}. First, exploiting the fact that {\displaystyle A} is independent of the sigma-algebra generated by the events in {\displaystyle S_{2}}.

{\displaystyle {\text{Numerator}}\leq \Pr \left(A\mid \bigwedge _{B\in S_{2}}{\overline {B}}\right)=\Pr(A)\leq x(A)\prod _{B\in \Gamma (A)}(1-x(B)).\qquad (1)}

Expanding the denominator by using Bayes' theorem and then using the inductive assumption, we get

{\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}&{\text{Denominator}}\\={}&\Pr \left({\overline {B}}_{j1}\mid \bigwedge _{t=2}^{l}{\overline {B}}_{jt}\wedge \bigwedge _{B\in S_{2}}{\overline {B}}\right)\cdot \Pr \left({\overline {B}}_{j2}\mid \bigwedge _{t=3}^{l}{\overline {B}}_{jt}\wedge \bigwedge _{B\in S_{2}}{\overline {B}}\right)\cdots \Pr \left({\overline {B}}_{jl}\mid \bigwedge _{B\in S_{2}}{\overline {B}}\right)\geq \prod _{B\in S_{1}}(1-x(B))\qquad (2)\end{aligned}}}

The inductive assumption can be applied here since each event is conditioned on lesser number of other events, i.e. on a subset of cardinality less than {\displaystyle |S|}. From (1) and (2), we get

{\displaystyle \Pr \left(A\mid \bigwedge _{B\in S}{\overline {B}}\right)\leq x(A)\prod _{B\in \Gamma (A)-S_{1}}(1-x(B))\leq x(A)}

Since the value of x is always in {\displaystyle [0,1)}. Note that we have essentially proved {\displaystyle \Pr \left({\overline {A}}\mid \bigwedge _{B\in S}{\overline {B}}\right)\geq 1-x(A)}. To get the desired probability, we write it in terms of conditional probabilities applying Bayes' theorem repeatedly. Hence,

{\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}&\Pr \left({\overline {A_{1}}}\wedge \cdots \wedge {\overline {A_{n}}}\right)\\={}&\Pr \left({\overline {A_{1}}}\mid {\overline {A_{2}}}\wedge \cdots {\overline {A_{n}}}\right)\cdot \Pr \left({\overline {A_{2}}}\mid {\overline {A_{3}}}\wedge \cdots {\overline {A_{n}}}\right)\cdots \Pr \left({\overline {A_{n}}}\right)\\\geq {}&\prod _{A\in {\mathcal {A}}}(1-x(A)),\end{aligned}}}

which is what we had intended to prove.
