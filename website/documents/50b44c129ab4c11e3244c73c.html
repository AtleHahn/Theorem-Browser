Statement

For n≥1 and variables x_1,\ldots,x_n, let p_k=\sum_jx_j^k for k≥1, let e_0=1, and let e_k=\sum_{j_1<\cdots<j_k}x_{j_1}\cdots x_{j_k}, with e_k=0 for k>n. For k≥1 let M be k×k with first column M_{i1}=i e_i, entries M_{ij}=e_{i-j+1} for 2≤j≤i, M_{i,i+1}=1, and all remaining entries zero. Then p_k=det M.

Proof 1: Symmetric-polynomial determinant: p from e

One can obtain explicit formulas for p_k in the form of determinants, by considering the first k of Newton's identities as linear equations in which the elementary symmetric functions are known and the power sums are unknowns, and apply Cramer's rule to find the solution for the final unknown. For instance taking Newton's identities in the form

{\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}e_{1}&=1p_{1},\\2e_{2}&=e_{1}p_{1}-1p_{2},\\3e_{3}&=e_{2}p_{1}-e_{1}p_{2}+1p_{3},\\&\,\,\,\vdots \\ke_{k}&=e_{k-1}p_{1}-e_{k-2}p_{2}+\cdots +(-1)^{k}e_{1}p_{k-1}+(-1)^{k-1}p_{k}\end{aligned}}}

we consider p_1,-p_2,p_3,\ldots,(-1)^{k-1}p_k as unknowns, and solve for the final one, giving

{\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}(-1)^{k-1}p_{k}={}&{\begin{vmatrix}1&0&\cdots &&e_{1}\\e_{1}&1&0&\cdots &2e_{2}\\e_{2}&e_{1}&1&&3e_{3}\\\vdots &&\ddots &\ddots &\vdots \\e_{k-1}&\cdots &e_{2}&e_{1}&ke_{k}\end{vmatrix}}{\begin{vmatrix}1&0&\cdots &\\e_{1}&1&0&\cdots \\e_{2}&e_{1}&1&\\\vdots &&\ddots &\ddots \\e_{k-1}&\cdots &e_{2}&e_{1}&1\end{vmatrix}}^{-1}\\[7pt]p_{k}={(-1)^{k-1}}&{\begin{vmatrix}1&0&\cdots &&e_{1}\\e_{1}&1&0&\cdots &2e_{2}\\e_{2}&e_{1}&1&&3e_{3}\\\vdots &&\ddots &\ddots &\vdots \\e_{k-1}&\cdots &e_{2}&e_{1}&ke_{k}\end{vmatrix}}\\[7pt]={}&{\begin{vmatrix}e_{1}&1&0&\cdots \\2e_{2}&e_{1}&1&0&\cdots \\3e_{3}&e_{2}&e_{1}&1\\\vdots &&&\ddots &\ddots \\ke_{k}&e_{k-1}&\cdots &&e_{1}\end{vmatrix}}.\end{aligned}}}

The denominator determinant is 1, since its matrix is lower triangular with diagonal entries e_0=1. Moving the last column to the first position takes k-1 column interchanges, giving the final determinant. For k=1 this is simply p_1=e_1.
