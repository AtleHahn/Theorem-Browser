Statement

Let A,B,C be noncollinear points in the Euclidean plane. Choose D\in BC\setminus\{B,C\}, E\in CA\setminus\{C,A\} and F\in AB\setminus\{A,B\} on the full lines. On each line choose an orientation and use directed segment lengths; thus \overline{AF}/\overline{FB} is positive when F is between A,B and negative otherwise. Then D,E,F are collinear if and only if \frac{\overline{AF}}{\overline{FB}}\frac{\overline{BD}}{\overline{DC}}\frac{\overline{CE}}{\overline{EA}}=-1.

Level
High school only
Level justification

All objects and directed-length conventions are elementary plane geometry, explicitly within the high-school rubric.

Proof 1: Perpendicular distances and directed-ratio uniqueness — outline

Suppose first that D,E,F are collinear. The sign of the left-hand side will be negative since either all three of the ratios are negative, the case where the line DEF misses the triangle, or one is negative and the other two are positive, the case where DEF crosses two sides of the triangle.

To check the magnitude, construct perpendiculars from A, B, C to the line DEF and let their lengths be a, b, c respectively. Then by similar triangles it follows that

{\left|{\frac {\overline {AF}}{\overline {FB}}}\right|=\left|{\frac {a}{b}}\right|,\quad \left|{\frac {\overline {BD}}{\overline {DC}}}\right|=\left|{\frac {b}{c}}\right|,\quad \left|{\frac {\overline {CE}}{\overline {EA}}}\right|=\left|{\frac {c}{a}}\right|.}

Therefore,

{\left|{\frac {\overline {AF}}{\overline {FB}}}\right|\times \left|{\frac {\overline {BD}}{\overline {DC}}}\right|\times \left|{\frac {\overline {CE}}{\overline {EA}}}\right|=\left|{\frac {a}{b}}\times {\frac {b}{c}}\times {\frac {c}{a}}\right|=1.}

The converse follows as a corollary. Let D, E, F be given on the lines BC, AC, AB so that the equation holds. The line DE cannot be parallel to AB: in that case similarity gives (\overline{BD}/\overline{DC})(\overline{CE}/\overline{EA})=1, forcing \overline{AF}/\overline{FB}=-1, impossible since \overline{AF}+\overline{FB}=\overline{AB}\ne0. Let F' be the point where DE crosses AB; neither A nor B lies on DE under the hypotheses. Then by the theorem, the equation also holds for D, E, F'. Comparing the two,

{{\frac {\overline {AF}}{\overline {FB}}}={\frac {\overline {AF'}}{\overline {F'B}}}\ .}

But at most one point can cut a segment in a given ratio so F=F'.

Proof 2: An auxiliary parallel and directed-ratio uniqueness — outline

Proof 3: Compose three homotheties around the triangle — outline

A homothety with center P and nonzero ratio r is the map x\mapsto P+r(x-P).

Whether or not D, E, F are collinear, there are three homotheties with centers D, E, F that respectively send B to C, C to A, and A to B. The composition of the three then is an element of the group of homothety-translations that fixes B, so it is a homothety with center B, possibly with ratio 1 (in which case it is the identity). This composition fixes the line DE if and only if F is collinear with D, E (since the first two homotheties certainly fix DE, and the third does so only if F lies on DE). Since B does not lie on DE, a homothety centered at B preserves that line only if it is the identity. Therefore D, E, F are collinear if and only if this composition is the identity, which means that the product of the three signed ratios is 1:

{{\frac {\overline {DC}}{\overline {DB}}}\times {\frac {\overline {EA}}{\overline {EC}}}\times {\frac {\overline {FB}}{\overline {FA}}}=1,}

which is equivalent to the given equation.
