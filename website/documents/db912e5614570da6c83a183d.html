Statement

Let R>0, let f=u+iv be holomorphic in a neighborhood of |z|\le R, and let n\ge1 be an integer. If u(z)\le M on |z|=R, then

|f^{(n)}(0)|\le\frac{2n!}{R^n}(M-u(0)).

If instead v(z)\le M on the boundary, the analogous bound is |f^{(n)}(0)|\le 2n!(M-v(0))/R^n.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Real analysis supplies derivatives, maxima, uniform convergence and completeness; complex arithmetic supplies the plane. Holomorphic means complex differentiable, and entire means so throughout the plane. With that short extension, the displayed bound or convergence assertion uses familiar analytic concepts without contour integrals or specialist function-space theory.

Proof 1: A nonnegative trigonometric weight in Cauchy integrals

Local coefficient estimate

It suffices to prove the u case, since the v case is found by -if=v-iu.

Subtract a constant away: put h=f-f(0), u_h=\operatorname{Re}h and B=M-u(0). Then h(0)=0, u_h\leq B on the boundary, and h^{(n)}=f^{(n)} for n\geq1.

Do three contour integrals around the circle |z|=R using Cauchy integral formula:

{\displaystyle h^{(n)}(0)/n!={\frac {1}{2\pi i}}\oint {\frac {h(z)}{z^{n+1}}}dz=\int _{0}^{1}{\frac {h(Re^{2\pi it})}{R^{n}e^{2\pi int}}}dt}{\displaystyle \int _{0}^{1}h(Re^{2\pi it})e^{2\pi int}dt={\frac {1}{2\pi iR^{n}}}\oint h(z)z^{n-1}dz=0}{\displaystyle \int _{0}^{1}h(Re^{2\pi it})dt={\frac {1}{2\pi i}}\oint h(z)z^{-1}dz=h(0)=0}

Pick angle {\displaystyle \theta }, so that {\displaystyle e^{-i\theta }h^{(n)}(0)=|h^{(n)}(0)|}. Then by linearly combining the three integrals, we get

{\displaystyle \int _{0}^{1}h(Re^{2\pi it})(1+\cos(2\pi nt+\theta ))\,dt={\frac {1}{2}}R^{n}|h^{(n)}(0)|/n!}

The imaginary part vanishes, and the real part gives

{\displaystyle \int _{0}^{1}u_h(Re^{2\pi it})(1+\cos(2\pi nt+\theta ))\,dt={\frac {1}{2}}R^{n}|h^{(n)}(0)|/n!}

The integral is bounded above by {\displaystyle B\int _{0}^{1}(1+\cos(2\pi nt+\theta ))\,dt=B}, so we have the result.

Level
Beyond undergraduate level
Level justification

The proof combines Cauchy’s derivative formula and vanishing contour integrals with a complex phase and a nonnegative trigonometric weight. Understanding the holomorphic contour framework and all three integral manipulations requires a substantial complex-analysis background beyond ordinary calculus.
