Statement

If a is irrational and \{x\}=x-\lfloor x\rfloor, then \{\{na\}:n\in\mathbb Z\} is dense in [0,1]: every p\in[0,1] is within any prescribed positive tolerance of such a fractional part.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Density is quantified approximation at every real point and every positive tolerance, an undergraduate real-analysis concept rather than a fixed numerical inequality. A bright US college student should be able to understand the statement after about 30 minutes of focused background research; this rating concerns understanding the claim, not proving it.

Proof 1: A small fractional part and its successive multiples

Write \{x\}=x-\lfloor x\rfloor for the fractional part. The pigeonhole principle says that more objects than classes force two objects into one class. Irrationality ensures that no multiple in the construction is an endpoint of the subdivisions.

Fix \varepsilon>0. If one takes a positive integer {\displaystyle M} such that {\displaystyle 1/M<\varepsilon }, by the pigeonhole principle there must be distinct {\displaystyle n_{1},n_{2}\in \{1,2,\ldots ,M+1\}} such that {\displaystyle n_{1}a} and {\displaystyle n_{2}a} are in the same integer subdivision of size {\displaystyle 1/M} (there are only {\displaystyle M} such subdivisions between consecutive integers). In particular, one can find {\displaystyle n_{1}},{\displaystyle n_{2}} such that

{\displaystyle n_{1}a\in \left(u+{\frac {k}{M}},\ u+{\frac {k+1}{M}}\right),\quad n_{2}a\in \left(v+{\frac {k}{M}},\ v+{\frac {k+1}{M}}\right),}

for some integers {\displaystyle u}, {\displaystyle v} and {\displaystyle k\in \{0,1,\cdots ,M-1\}}. One can then easily verify that

{\displaystyle (n_{2}-n_{1})a\in \left(v-u-{\frac {1}{M}},v-u+{\frac {1}{M}}\right).}

This implies that {\displaystyle 0<\{na\}<1/M<\varepsilon }, where {\displaystyle n=n_{2}-n_{1}} or {\displaystyle n=n_{1}-n_{2}}. This shows that 0 is a limit point of {\displaystyle \{\{na\}\}}. One can then use this fact to prove the case for {\displaystyle p} in {\displaystyle (0,1]}: find {\displaystyle n} such that {\displaystyle 0<\{na\}<1/M<\varepsilon }; then if {\displaystyle p\in (0,1/M]}, the proof is complete. Otherwise

{\displaystyle p\in \left({\frac {j}{M}},{\frac {j+1}{M}}\right],}

where j\in\{1,\ldots,M-1\}, and by setting

{\displaystyle k=\sup \left\{r\in\mathbb N_{>0}:r\{na\}<{\frac {j}{M}}\right\},}

one obtains

{\displaystyle {\Bigl |}\{(k+1)na\}-p{\Bigr |}<{\frac {1}{M}}<\varepsilon .}
