Statement

For an integer n\ge1, use deterministic Turing machines with n operational states, a separate Halt state, one starting state, a two-way unbounded tape over \{0,1\}, and a transition for each operational state and scanned symbol. Each transition writes a bit, moves one cell left or right, and enters a state (possibly Halt). The machine starts on an all-zero tape. Among machines that halt, \Sigma(n) is the largest number of ones left on the tape, and S(n) the largest number of transitions, including the halting transition. Let C(m) be the least number of states of a machine halting on blank tape with a single block of m ones. Suppose an arithmetic theory T has a number k\ge1 such that no particular integer m can be proved in T to satisfy C(m)>k, as supplied by the relevant Chaitin incompleteness theorem. Assume the ordinary elementary formalization of machine scores and unused-state padding in T. Then T proves no particular upper bound for \Sigma(k).

Proof 1: A forbidden complexity lower bound

If a particular upper bound for \Sigma(k) were proved, choose a particular integer m larger than that bound. The inequality m>\Sigma(k) proves that no machine with at most k states can output a unary block of m ones, since unused states can be added. Thus C(m)>k would be proved, contrary to the assumed Chaitin complexity bound.

Level justification

The numerical comparison and state padding are elementary, but the claim is about what a formal arithmetic theory proves, and requires transporting machine-complexity assertions through an internal formalization. Chaitin incompleteness and this metamathematical setting exceed a small extension of the ordinary undergraduate baseline.
