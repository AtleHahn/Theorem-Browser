Statement

Let (S,d) and (S',d') be metric spaces, and let X_n (n\geq1) and X be S-valued random elements. Let g:S\to S' have discontinuity set

D_g=\{x\in S:g\text{ is not continuous at }x\},

and suppose \Pr(X\in D_g)=0. Assume that g(X_n) and g(X) are random elements. Then

\begin{aligned} X_n\xrightarrow{\mathrm d}X&\ \Longrightarrow\ g(X_n)\xrightarrow{\mathrm d}g(X),\\ X_n\xrightarrow{\mathrm p}X&\ \Longrightarrow\ g(X_n)\xrightarrow{\mathrm p}g(X),\\ X_n\xrightarrow{\mathrm{a.s.}}X&\ \Longrightarrow\ g(X_n)\xrightarrow{\mathrm{a.s.}}g(X). \end{aligned}

Here \mathrm d, \mathrm p, and \mathrm{a.s.} mean convergence in distribution, in probability, and almost surely, respectively. For the latter two assertions the random elements are defined on a common probability space, and the distances d(X_n,X) and d'(g(X_n),g(X)) are assumed measurable. Convergence in distribution means convergence of expectations of every bounded continuous real-valued test function; convergence in probability means that the probability of a distance exceeding any fixed \varepsilon>0 tends to zero; almost sure convergence means pointwise metric convergence on an event of probability one.

Level
Beyond undergraduate level
Level justification

The full metric-space formulation combines measurable random elements, probability-one exceptional sets, weak convergence tested by bounded continuous functions, and two distinct probabilistic convergence modes. Their cumulative measure-theoretic prerequisites are not a confidently small 30-minute extension of the baseline undergraduate curriculum; conservatively beyond undergraduate.
