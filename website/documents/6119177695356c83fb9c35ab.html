Statement

Let A=(a_{ij})\in\mathbb C^{n\times n}, n\ge1, and put R_i=\sum_{j\ne i}|a_{ij}| and D_i=\{z\in\mathbb C:|z-a_{ii}|\le R_i\}. If the union U of a selection of k disks is disjoint from the union V of the remaining n-k disks, then U contains exactly k eigenvalues and V exactly n-k, counting algebraic multiplicities.

Proof 1: Follow continuously labeled roots along a diagonal homotopy

Assume 0<k<n; the cases k=0,n follow from the Gershgorin row theorem. Let D be the diagonal matrix with entries equal to the diagonal entries of A and let

B(t)=(1-t)D+tA,\qquad 0\le t\le1.

We will use the fact that the eigenvalues can be continuously labeled on the real interval [0,1], counting algebraic multiplicities, and show that if any eigenvalue moves from one of the unions to the other, then it must be outside all the disks for some t, which is a contradiction.

The statement is true for D=B(0). The diagonal entries of B(t) are equal to those of A, thus the centers of the Gershgorin circles are the same; however, their radii are t times those of A. Therefore, the union of the corresponding k disks of B(t) is disjoint from the union of the remaining n-k for all t\in[0,1]. The disks are compact, so the distance of the two unions U,V for A is d>0. The distance between the two groups for B(t) is a decreasing function of t, so it is always at least d.

Since the eigenvalues of B(t) admit continuous labels, for any labeled eigenvalue \lambda(t) starting in the union of the k disks, its distance d(t) from the fixed union V of the other n-k disks of A is also continuous. Obviously d(0)\ge d, and assume \lambda(1) lies in V. Then d(1)=0, so there exists 0<t_0<1 such that 0<d(t_0)<d. But this means \lambda(t_0) lies outside U\cup V, and hence outside the Gershgorin disks of B(t_0), which is impossible by the row theorem. Therefore \lambda(1) lies in U. The same reasoning applies with the two groups interchanged. Thus the k roots initially in the first group remain there, and the n-k remaining roots stay in the other group, counting algebraic multiplicities.
