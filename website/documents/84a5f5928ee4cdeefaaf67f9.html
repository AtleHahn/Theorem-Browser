Statement

For real numbers u,v, |u+v|\le |u|+|v|.

Level
High school only
Level justification

Only real absolute values and an inequality of sums occur.

Proof 1: Split according to signs

  • If u and v have the same sign or either of them is zero, then

    {\displaystyle |u+v|=|u|+|v|.}

  • If u and v have opposite signs and unequal absolute values, then without loss of generality assume {\displaystyle |u|>|v|.} Then

    {\displaystyle |u+v|=|u|-|v|<|u|+|v|.}

If u and v have opposite signs and equal absolute values, then u+v=0 and the inequality also holds.

Combining these cases:

{\displaystyle |u+v|\leq |u|+|v|.}
Level
High school only
Level justification

The proof checks same-sign, opposite-sign and zero cases directly from the definition of absolute value. All calculations are ordinary real-number arithmetic and comparisons taught in high school.

Proof 2: Add the two order bounds

Any number is always less than or equal to its own absolute value, so {\displaystyle -|u|\leq u\leq |u|} and {\displaystyle -|v|\leq v\leq |v|.} Adding these inequalities together, {\displaystyle -{\bigl (}|u|+|v|{\bigr )}\leq u+v\leq |u|+|v|,} the sum has the form {\displaystyle -a\leq b\leq a} for {\displaystyle a=|u|+|v|} and {\displaystyle b=u+v.} But this always implies {\displaystyle |b|\leq a}, or, expanded, {\displaystyle |u+v|\leq |u|+|v|.}
