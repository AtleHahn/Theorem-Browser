Statement

For integers g\ge0, k\ge1, define h_{g;k} as 1/k! times the number of tuples (\tau_1,\ldots,\tau_{k+2g-1},\sigma) of permutations of \{1,\ldots,k\}, where each \tau_i swaps two elements and fixes the others, \sigma is a single k-cycle, \tau_1\cdots\tau_{k+2g-1}\sigma=1, and the swaps generate an action transitive on the k elements. For g=1 and every integer k\ge1, h_{1;k}=(k^2-1)k^k/24.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

The Hurwitz count combines permutation products, cycles, transitive generated actions and a normalization; these are undergraduate algebra/combinatorics prerequisites even for the small values. A bright US college student should be able to understand the statement after about 30 minutes of focused background research; this rating concerns understanding the claim, not proving it.

Proof 1: Insert the calibrated Hodge integrals into ELSV — outline

On \overline{\mathcal M}_{1,1}, let \lambda_1=c_1(E) for the Hodge line bundle and \psi_1 be the cotangent-line Chern class.

Apply the genus-one, one-pole ELSV formula. Since the moduli stack has complex dimension one, expanding its denominator and taking degree one gives:

{\displaystyle h_{1;k}=(k+1)!{\frac {k^{k}}{k!}}\int _{{\overline {\mathcal {M}}}_{1,1}}{\frac {1-\lambda _{1}}{1-k\psi _{1}}}=(k+1)k^{k}\left\{\left[\int _{{\overline {\mathcal {M}}}_{1,1}}\psi _{1}\right]k-\left[\int _{{\overline {\mathcal {M}}}_{1,1}}\lambda _{1}\right]\right\}.}

On the other hand, by the transposition-count definition, the Hurwitz number h1, k equals 1/k times the number of ways to decompose a fixed k-cycle in the symmetric group Sk into a product of k + 1 transpositions. In particular, h1, 1 = 0 (since there are no transpositions in S1), while h1, 2 = 1/2 (since there is a unique factorization of the transposition (1 2) in S2 into a product of three transpositions).

Plugging these two values into the ELSV formula we find

{\displaystyle \int _{{\overline {\mathcal {M}}}_{1,1}}\psi _{1}=\int _{{\overline {\mathcal {M}}}_{1,1}}\lambda _{1}={\frac {1}{24}}.}

From which we deduce

{\displaystyle h_{1;k}={\frac {(k^{2}-1)k^{k}}{24}}.}
