Statement

Let D=\{z\in\mathbb C:|z|<1\}, and let f_n:D\to\mathbb C be injective holomorphic functions with f_n(0)=0 and positive real f_n'(0). Put U_n=f_n(D). For any sequence of open sets containing zero, define its kernel as the union of the components containing zero of the interiors of its tail intersections; a component is empty if zero is not in that interior, and an empty union is replaced by \{0\}. Convergence to a kernel means that every subsequence has that same kernel. Suppose (U_n) converges to a kernel U\ne\mathbb C. Then (f_n) converges uniformly on compact subsets to a holomorphic function f. If U=\{0\} then f=0; otherwise f is injective and f(D)=U.

Proof 1: Kernel convergence implies compact-uniform convergence — outline

Suppose Un converges to a kernel not equal to C, then by the Koebe quarter theorem Un contains the disk of radius f 'n(0) / 4 with centre 0. The assumption that U ≠ C implies that these radii are uniformly bounded. By the Koebe distortion theorem

{\displaystyle |f_{n}(z)|\leq f_{n}^{\prime }(0){|z| \over (1-|z|)^{2}}.}

Hence the sequence fn is uniformly bounded on compact sets. Montel's theorem supplies convergent subsequences. If two subsequences converge to holomorphic limits f and g, then f(0) = g(0) and with f'(0), g'(0) ≥ 0. By the forward image/kernel implication (a compact-uniform limit of normalized univalent maps has image equal to the kernel of every subsequence of their images), taken as a prerequisite, and the assumptions it follows that f(D) = g(D). By Hurwitz's theorem each limit is injective or constant. If U=\{0\}, both limits are zero. Otherwise both are injective with positive derivatives at zero, and uniqueness in the Riemann mapping theorem forces f = g, so the original sequence fn is uniformly convergent on compact sets.
