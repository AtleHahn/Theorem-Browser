Statement

Let P\in\mathbb C[z_1,\ldots,z_n] be a nonzero polynomial. The constant-coefficient differential operator P(\partial_{x_1},\ldots,\partial_{x_n}) on \mathbb R^n has a distributional fundamental solution: a distribution E satisfying P(\partial)E=\delta_0.

Proof 1: Laurent constant of polynomial powers via Bernstein–Sato — outline

By taking Fourier transforms, it suffices to show that every nonzero polynomial has a tempered-distributional inverse. Here the convention is \mathcal F(\partial_j u)(\xi)=i\xi_j\mathcal F(u)(\xi) and \mathcal F\delta_0=1, so the polynomial to invert is Q(\xi)=P(i\xi).

By replacing Q by the product with its complex conjugate, one can assume that the polynomial is nonnegative: if H=Q\overline Q and HT=1, then Q(\overline Q\,T)=1.

For nonnegative polynomials H, the existence of a distributional inverse follows from the existence of the Bernstein–Sato polynomial, which implies that H^s can be analytically continued as a meromorphic distribution-valued function of the complex variable s; the constant term of the Laurent expansion of H^s at s=-1 is then a distributional inverse of H.

The continuation theorem is used in its tempered-distribution form, regular at s=0 with value 1 and satisfying HH^s=H^{s+1}. These are the analytic prerequisites for the source's Fourier and Laurent-term argument, not proved there. If T_0 is that constant term, the fundamental solution is E=\mathcal F^{-1}(\overline Q\,T_0).
