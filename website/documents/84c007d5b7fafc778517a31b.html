Statement

A correspondence C:\Theta\rightrightarrows X assigns subsets of X to points of \Theta. It is upper hemicontinuous if for every \theta and open G\supseteq C(\theta), some neighborhood U of \theta satisfies C(\theta')\subseteq G for all \theta'\in U. It is lower hemicontinuous if for every open G meeting C(\theta), some such U has C(\theta')\cap G\ne\varnothing throughout U. Let X,\Theta be topological spaces, f:X\times\Theta\to\mathbb R continuous, and C:\Theta\rightrightarrows X upper and lower hemicontinuous with nonempty compact values. Define v(\theta)=\sup_{x\in C(\theta)}f(x,\theta) and M(\theta)=\{x\in C(\theta):f(x,\theta)=v(\theta)\}. Then v is continuous, and M is upper hemicontinuous with nonempty compact values.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Basic topology and real analysis supply neighborhoods, compactness and extrema. The two kinds of hemicontinuity are explicitly defined set-valued analogues of continuity; the maximizer or minimizer set is then given directly. A short interval-valued example clarifies the additional conditions without further theory.

Proof 1: Combine value semicontinuity with the closed level-set graph

Write f^*=v and C^*=M. For a correspondence A and a set U\subseteq\Theta, write A(U)=\bigcup_{\theta\in U}A(\theta). In the value-function lemmas below, assume the constraint values are nonempty and the supremum is finite.

Throughout this proof we will use the term neighborhood to refer to an open set containing a particular point. We preface with a preliminary lemma, which is a general fact in the calculus of correspondences. Recall that a correspondence is closed if its graph is closed.

Lemma. If {\displaystyle A,B:\Theta \rightrightarrows X} are correspondences, {\displaystyle A} is upper hemicontinuous and compact-valued, and {\displaystyle B} is closed, then {\displaystyle A\cap B:\Theta \rightrightarrows X} defined by {\displaystyle (A\cap B)(\theta )=A(\theta )\cap B(\theta )} is upper hemicontinuous.

Let {\displaystyle \theta \in \Theta }, and suppose {\displaystyle G} is an open set containing {\displaystyle (A\cap B)(\theta )}. If {\displaystyle A(\theta )\subseteq G}, then the result follows immediately. Otherwise, observe that for each {\displaystyle x\in A(\theta )\setminus G} we have {\displaystyle x\notin B(\theta )}, and since {\displaystyle B} is closed there is a neighborhood {\displaystyle U_{x}\times V_{x}} of {\displaystyle (\theta ,x)} in which {\displaystyle x'\notin B(\theta ')} whenever {\displaystyle (\theta ',x')\in U_{x}\times V_{x}}. The collection of sets {\displaystyle \{G\}\cup \{V_{x}:x\in A(\theta )\setminus G\}} forms an open cover of the compact set {\displaystyle A(\theta )}, which allows us to extract a finite subcover {\displaystyle G,V_{x_{1}},\dots ,V_{x_{n}}}. By upper hemicontinuity, there is a neighborhood {\displaystyle U_{\theta }} of {\displaystyle \theta } such that {\displaystyle A(U_{\theta })\subseteq G\cup V_{x_{1}}\cup \dots \cup V_{x_{n}}}. Then whenever {\displaystyle \theta '\in U_{\theta }\cap U_{x_{1}}\cap \dots \cap U_{x_{n}}}, we have {\displaystyle A(\theta ')\subseteq G\cup V_{x_{1}}\cup \dots \cup V_{x_{n}}}, and so {\displaystyle (A\cap B)(\theta ')\subseteq G}. This completes the proof. {\displaystyle \square }

The continuity of {\displaystyle f^{*}} in the maximum theorem is the result of combining two independent theorems together.

Theorem 1. If {\displaystyle f} is upper semicontinuous and {\displaystyle C} is upper hemicontinuous, nonempty and compact-valued, then {\displaystyle f^{*}} is upper semicontinuous.

Fix {\displaystyle \theta \in \Theta }, and let {\displaystyle \varepsilon >0} be arbitrary. For each {\displaystyle x\in C(\theta )}, there exists a neighborhood {\displaystyle U_{x}\times V_{x}} of {\displaystyle (\theta ,x)} such that whenever {\displaystyle (\theta ',x')\in U_{x}\times V_{x}}, we have {\displaystyle f(x',\theta ')<f(x,\theta )+\varepsilon }. The set of neighborhoods {\displaystyle \{V_{x}:x\in C(\theta )\}} covers {\displaystyle C(\theta )}, which is compact, so {\displaystyle V_{x_{1}},\dots ,V_{x_{n}}} suffice. Furthermore, since {\displaystyle C} is upper hemicontinuous, there exists a neighborhood {\displaystyle U'} of {\displaystyle \theta } such that whenever {\displaystyle \theta '\in U'} it follows that {\displaystyle C(\theta ')\subseteq \bigcup _{k=1}^{n}V_{x_{k}}}. Let {\displaystyle U=U'\cap U_{x_{1}}\cap \dots \cap U_{x_{n}}}. Then for all {\displaystyle \theta '\in U}, we have {\displaystyle f(x',\theta ')<f(x_{k},\theta )+\varepsilon } for each {\displaystyle x'\in C(\theta ')}, as {\displaystyle x'\in V_{x_{k}}} for some {\displaystyle k}. It follows that {\displaystyle f^{*}(\theta ')=\sup _{x'\in C(\theta ')}f(x',\theta ')<\max _{k=1,\dots ,n}f(x_{k},\theta )+\varepsilon \leq f^{*}(\theta )+\varepsilon ,} which was desired. {\displaystyle \square }

Theorem 2. If {\displaystyle f} is lower semicontinuous and {\displaystyle C} is lower hemicontinuous, then {\displaystyle f^{*}} is lower semicontinuous.

Fix {\displaystyle \theta \in \Theta }, and let {\displaystyle \varepsilon >0} be arbitrary. By definition of {\displaystyle f^{*}}, there exists {\displaystyle x\in C(\theta )} such that {\displaystyle f^{*}(\theta )<f(x,\theta )+{\frac {\varepsilon }{2}}}. Now, since {\displaystyle f} is lower semicontinuous, there exists a neighborhood {\displaystyle U_{1}\times V} of {\displaystyle (\theta ,x)} such that whenever {\displaystyle (\theta ',x')\in U_{1}\times V} we have {\displaystyle f(x,\theta )<f(x',\theta ')+{\frac {\varepsilon }{2}}}. Observe that {\displaystyle C(\theta )\cap V\neq \emptyset } (in particular, {\displaystyle x\in C(\theta )\cap V}). Therefore, since {\displaystyle C} is lower hemicontinuous, there exists a neighborhood {\displaystyle U_{2}} such that whenever {\displaystyle \theta '\in U_{2}} there exists {\displaystyle x'\in C(\theta ')\cap V}. Let {\displaystyle U=U_{1}\cap U_{2}}. Then whenever {\displaystyle \theta '\in U} there exists {\displaystyle x'\in C(\theta ')\cap V}, which implies {\displaystyle f^{*}(\theta )<f(x,\theta )+{\frac {\varepsilon }{2}}<f(x',\theta ')+\varepsilon \leq f^{*}(\theta ')+\varepsilon } which was desired. {\displaystyle \square }

Under the hypotheses of the Maximum theorem, {\displaystyle f^{*}} is continuous. It remains to verify that {\displaystyle C^{*}} is an upper hemicontinuous correspondence with compact values. Let {\displaystyle \theta \in \Theta }. To see that {\displaystyle C^{*}(\theta )} is nonempty, observe that the function {\displaystyle f_{\theta }:C(\theta )\to \mathbb {R} } by {\displaystyle f_{\theta }(x)=f(x,\theta )} is continuous on the compact set {\displaystyle C(\theta )}. The Extreme Value theorem implies that {\displaystyle C^{*}(\theta )} is nonempty. In addition, since {\displaystyle f_{\theta }} is continuous, it follows that {\displaystyle C^{*}(\theta )} is a closed subset of the compact set {\displaystyle C(\theta )}, which implies {\displaystyle C^{*}(\theta )} is compact. Finally, let {\displaystyle D:\Theta \rightrightarrows X} be defined by {\textstyle D(\theta )=\{x\in X:f(x,\theta )=f^{*}(\theta )\}}. Since {\displaystyle f} is a continuous function, {\displaystyle D} is a closed correspondence. Moreover, since {\displaystyle C^{*}(\theta )=C(\theta )\cap D(\theta )}, the preliminary Lemma implies that {\displaystyle C^{*}} is upper hemicontinuous. {\displaystyle \square }

Level justification

The full proof uses finite subcovers, product neighborhoods, closed sets and extrema of continuous real functions on compact sets. The new object is a set-valued map, and its two supplied hemicontinuity definitions say that nearby sets stay inside a containing open set or continue to meet an intersecting open set. These are related extensions of basic point-set continuity, illustrated by varying compact intervals. All correspondence lemmas are proved locally with those definitions, so no separate optimization or advanced set-valued-analysis theory is needed.

For finite optimized values over nonempty constraints: an upper-semicontinuous objective and upper-hemicontinuous compact-valued constraints give an upper-semicontinuous supremum; a lower-semicontinuous objective and lower-hemicontinuous constraints give a lower-semicontinuous supremum.

Review notes:

3. Continuous real functions on nonempty compact sets attain their extrema
Kind:

Theorem

Treatment:

Cited without proof

Statement used:

A continuous real function on a nonempty compact topological space attains a maximum and minimum.

Application in proof:

Ensure maximizers exist; the equality set is closed in each compact feasible set and hence compact.

Application level:

Undergraduate level

Level justification:

This is a standard undergraduate compactness result.

Review notes:

Closedness of the equality graph uses continuity of both f and the already-proved value v, not f alone. No Hausdorff assumption on X is needed for the closed-subset-of-compact step.
