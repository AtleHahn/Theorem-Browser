Philosophical significance
Major

Using choice and arbitrary point sets, area-preserving affine transformations can rearrange a partition into two full copies of the original region. The paradox limits the extension of additive area to all sets; it is not a physical duplication procedure and its transformations are more general than rigid motions.

Statement

Assume the axiom of choice. The unit square Q=[0,1]^2 can be partitioned into finitely many pairwise disjoint sets A_1,\ldots,A_r,B_1,\ldots,B_s such that there are area-preserving affine maps g_i,h_j:\mathbb R^2\to\mathbb R^2 for which

Q=\bigsqcup_{i=1}^r g_i(A_i)=\bigsqcup_{j=1}^s h_j(B_j).

Thus the two disjoint subcollections each reassemble into a full copy of Q; one copy may be translated to make the copies disjoint in the plane. The same paradoxical decomposition exists for the unit disk. An area-preserving affine map has the form x\mapsto Mx+b with a real invertible 2\times2 matrix M satisfying |\det M|=1. The pieces are arbitrary sets of points; measurability is not required.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Finite equidecomposition is explicit; planar affine maps and area determinants are linear algebra. Choice is a stated set-theoretic assumption, not a proof prerequisite hidden in the result.

Proof 1: Free affine actions and absorption of fixed sets (external)

A link to the proof can be found here (Section 8.1, Proposition 8.4 and Theorem 8.5, pp.119-120; pp.119–120.).

The source acts on the half-open square modulo the integer lattice by a free subgroup of \operatorname{SL}(2,\mathbb Z). Its transformations are finitely piecewise affine. After transferring the free-group paradox away from fixed points, it absorbs the countable union of fixed segments and points by translations. The final paragraph of Theorem 8.5 extends equidecomposability to any two bounded planar sets with nonempty interior.

Apply that result to the closed square Q and Q\cup(Q+v), choosing v so the copies are disjoint. Split each source piece according to which target copy it meets, and translate the second copy back. These are the two disjoint subcollections required above. The same argument applies to the disk. Subdivide each piecewise affine transformation into its finitely many affine pieces; all determinants retain absolute value one.

Level justification

The statement uses affine maps, determinants, finite partitions and choice. The argument adds reduced words in a free group, orbit representatives, countable exceptional sets and a piecewise form of Schröder–Bernstein. The free subgroup follows from two explicit shear matrices, and the partition and absorption dependencies have short set-theoretic proofs. Cumulatively these fit introductory algebra, linear algebra and elementary countability with a short preparation supplement.
