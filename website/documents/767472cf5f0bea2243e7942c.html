Statement

The nonzero entire function f(z)=\sin(\pi z) vanishes at every nonnegative integer and is of exponential type: |f(z)|\le C e^{\tau|z|} for some C,\tau>0. On the imaginary axis it admits a bound |f(iy)|\le C e^{\pi|y|}, but no bound of this form with a real exponent c<\pi. Thus replacing c<\pi by c\le\pi in the Carlson uniqueness conditions would be false.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Complex differentiability and entire functions add one direct extension of calculus; power-series coefficients, derivatives, absolute values and growth bounds are familiar analysis. The explicit inequalities require no specialist complex-analytic constructions to understand.

Proof 1: The sine counterexample — outline

To see that the strict imaginary-axis growth condition in Carlson’s theorem is sharp, consider the function f(z) = sin(πz). It vanishes on the integers; however, it grows exponentially on the imaginary axis with a growth rate of c = π, and indeed it is not identically zero.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

The explicit identity sin(pi z)=(exp(i pi z)-exp(-i pi z))/(2i) gives the growth bound, while |sin(pi i y)|=sinh(pi|y|) rules out c<pi by a real exponential limit. Beyond calculus, only the elementary meaning of entire for this familiar complex function is needed; no proof of Carlson uniqueness is used.
