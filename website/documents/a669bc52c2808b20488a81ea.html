Statement

A convex quadrilateral inscribed in a circle, with successive side lengths a,b,c,d and semiperimeter s=(a+b+c+d)/2, has area K=\sqrt{(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)(s-d)}.

Level
High school only
Level justification

The statement expresses a plane area using elementary lengths and square roots.

Proof 1: Triangle areas, cosine law and factorization

Let the cyclic vertices be A,B,C,D, with p=AB, q=AD, r=BC, s=CD. Write A=\angle DAB and C=\angle DCB, and put S=(p+q+r+s)/2.

The area K of the convex cyclic quadrilateral equals the sum of the areas of △ADB and △BDC:

{\displaystyle K={\frac {1}{2}}pq\sin A+{\frac {1}{2}}rs\sin C.}

But since □ABCD is a cyclic quadrilateral, ∠DAB = 180° − ∠DCB. Hence sin A = sin C. Therefore,

{\displaystyle K={\frac {1}{2}}pq\sin A+{\frac {1}{2}}rs\sin A}{\displaystyle K^{2}={\frac {1}{4}}(pq+rs)^{2}\sin ^{2}A}{\displaystyle 4K^{2}=(pq+rs)^{2}(1-\cos ^{2}A)=(pq+rs)^{2}-((pq+rs)\cos A)^{2}}

(using the trigonometric identity).

Solving for common side DB, in △ADB and △BDC, the law of cosines gives

{\displaystyle p^{2}+q^{2}-2pq\cos A=r^{2}+s^{2}-2rs\cos C.}

Substituting cos C = −cos A (since angles A and C are supplementary) and rearranging, we have

{\displaystyle (pq+rs)\cos A={\frac {1}{2}}(p^{2}+q^{2}-r^{2}-s^{2}).}

Substituting this in the equation for the area,

{\displaystyle 4K^{2}=(pq+rs)^{2}-{\frac {1}{4}}(p^{2}+q^{2}-r^{2}-s^{2})^{2}}{\displaystyle 16K^{2}=4(pq+rs)^{2}-(p^{2}+q^{2}-r^{2}-s^{2})^{2}.}

The right-hand side is of the form a2 − b2 = (a − b)(a + b) and hence can be written as

{\displaystyle [2(pq+rs)-p^{2}-q^{2}+r^{2}+s^{2}][2(pq+rs)+p^{2}+q^{2}-r^{2}-s^{2}]}

which, upon rearranging the terms in the square brackets, yields

{\displaystyle 16K^{2}=[(r+s)^{2}-(p-q)^{2}][(p+q)^{2}-(r-s)^{2}]}

that can be factored again into

{\displaystyle 16K^{2}=(q+r+s-p)(p+r+s-q)(p+q+s-r)(p+q+r-s).}

Introducing the semiperimeter S=(p+q+r+s)/2 yields

{\displaystyle 16K^{2}=16(S-p)(S-q)(S-r)(S-s).}

Taking the square root, we get

{\displaystyle K={\sqrt {(S-p)(S-q)(S-r)(S-s)}}.}

Proof 2: Specialize Bretschneider’s angle correction

Use Bretschneider’s formula for a convex quadrilateral with side lengths a,b,c,d, semiperimeter s, and \theta half the sum of two opposite angles:

{\displaystyle K={\sqrt {(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)(s-d)-abcd\cos ^{2}\theta }}}

It is a property of cyclic quadrilaterals (and ultimately of inscribed angles) that opposite angles of a quadrilateral sum to 180°. Consequently, in the case of an inscribed quadrilateral, θ is 90°, whence the term

{\displaystyle abcd\cos ^{2}\theta =abcd\cos ^{2}\left(90^{\circ }\right)=abcd\cdot 0=0,}

giving the basic form of Brahmagupta's formula.

Level
High school only
Level justification

In the stated Bretschneider formula, half the sum of opposite cyclic angles is 90 degrees, making the correction term zero. This uses only elementary geometry, trigonometry and substitution.

Proof 3: Specialize Coolidge’s diagonal correction using Ptolemy

Use Coolidge’s area formula for a convex quadrilateral, with successive sides a,b,c,d, semiperimeter s, and diagonals p,q:

{\displaystyle K={\sqrt {(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)(s-d)-\textstyle {1 \over 4}(ac+bd+pq)(ac+bd-pq)}}}

where p and q are the lengths of the diagonals of the quadrilateral. In a cyclic quadrilateral, pq = ac + bd according to Ptolemy's theorem, and the formula of Coolidge reduces to Brahmagupta's formula.

Level
High school only
Level justification

Ptolemy’s relation makes ac+bd-pq zero in the supplied Coolidge formula, leaving exactly the claimed area. This is elementary geometric formula substitution.
