Statement

For integers s\ge3 and j\ge1, define the jth s-gonal number by P_s(j)=((s-2)j^2-(s-4)j)/2. Every positive integer is a sum of at most s such s-gonal numbers. Repetitions are allowed.

Level
High school only
Level justification

The formula and all quantifiers require only elementary integer arithmetic.

Proof 1: Cauchy representation and the small cases (external)

A link to the proof can be found here (Theorems 5–6; §2 proof pp.7–9 and §3 auxiliary lemmas pp.9–13; small cases use Nathanson (1987) opening paragraph and cited Pepin/Dickson tables.).

Supporting passages: Nathanson, A short proof of Cauchy’s polygonal number theorem.

Write m=s-2. For m>=4 use Cheung–McNamer Theorem 5(a), which gives at most m+1 terms for n>=53m. Its proof chooses odd b and a remainder r, applies Cauchy’s four-square/sum lemma, and adds r ones; the technical interval estimates and residue choices are proved in §§2–3. For n<53m invoke Pepin/Dickson’s representation tables for n<120m, as cited by Nathanson and acknowledged by Cheung–McNamer; these tables are an imported finite-case dependency. For m=3 use Cheung–McNamer Theorem 6 and its printed tables: the six exceptional four-term cases have five-term representations 9=5+1+1+1+1, 21=5+5+5+5+1, 31=12+12+5+1+1, 43=35+5+1+1+1, 55=51+1+1+1+1, 89=70+12+5+1+1. For s=3,4 use Gauss’s triangular-number and Lagrange’s four-square theorems as named dependencies. Delete zero summands to meet j>=1; n>0 leaves at least one summand. The separate pentagonal treatment avoids the two-odd-integer residue argument at m=3.

Level justification

The interval and residue calculations are elementary, but Cauchy's lemma and the triangular-number case depend on three-square representation. Ankeny's supporting proof uses quadratic reciprocity, the two-square criterion, a lattice-point theorem and auxiliary primes supplied by Dirichlet's theorem. Including the character-series and nonvanishing arguments behind those prime choices exceeds the cumulative short preparation allowance.
