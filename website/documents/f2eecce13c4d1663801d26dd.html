Statement

A real or complex normed space X is finite-dimensional if and only if its closed unit ball is compact.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Real analysis supplies continuous functions, uniform bounds and norm convergence; linear algebra supplies linearity and finite dimension. The continuous-function norm is the supremum norm, and an isometry preserves it. These direct normed-space definitions suffice without dual or spectral theory.

Proof 1: Contradict sequential compactness by separated vectors — outline

Riesz's lemma guarantees that for any given 0<\alpha<1, every infinite-dimensional normed space contains a sequence x_1,x_2,\ldots of distinct unit vectors satisfying \|x_n-x_m\|>\alpha for m\ne n. These vectors are all separated from each other by a distance of more than \alpha while simultaneously also all lying on the unit sphere.

This sequence can be constructed by induction. Start by picking any element x_1 from the unit sphere. Let Y_{n-1} be the linear span of \{x_1,\ldots,x_{n-1}\} and, using Riesz's lemma, pick x_n from the unit sphere such that

d(x_n,Y_{n-1})>\alpha,\qquad d(x,Y)=\inf_{y\in Y}\|x-y\|.

Here finite-dimensional subspaces are closed and proper in an infinite-dimensional space; the strict bound follows by applying the lemma with a parameter between \alpha and 1.

This sequence x_1,x_2,\ldots contains no convergent subsequence, which implies that the closed unit ball is not compact. When the dimension of X is finite, the closed unit ball is compact by finite-dimensional compactness. Thus X is finite-dimensional if and only if its closed unit ball is compact.
