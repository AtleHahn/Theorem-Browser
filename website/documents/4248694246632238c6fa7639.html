Statement

If \pi(x) counts primes at most x, then \pi(x)\sim x/\log x as x\to\infty.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Understanding the statement involves Prime counting; Asymptotic limits; Logarithms. These prerequisites fit undergraduate coursework or a brief extension of it. A bright US college student should be able to understand this statement after approximately 30 minutes of focused background research; this assesses understanding the claim, not its proof.

Proof 1: Newman contour argument with zero-free-line preliminary (outline)

Let \pi(x) count primes at most x, let \vartheta(x)=\sum_{p\le x}\log p, and let \psi(x)=\sum_{p^k\le x}\log p, with prime p and integer k\ge1.

For \psi, the equivalence of the PNT with \psi(x)/x\to1 follows from the easy estimates

\psi(x)=\sum_{\substack{p\le x\\p\text{ is prime}}} \log p\left\lfloor\frac{\log x}{\log p}\right\rfloor \le\sum_{\substack{p\le x\\p\text{ is prime}}}\log x =\pi(x)\log x

and (using big O notation), for any 0<\varepsilon<1,

\begin{aligned} \psi(x) &\ge\sum_{\substack{x^{1-\varepsilon}\le p\le x\\p\text{ is prime}}}\log p\\ &\ge\sum_{\substack{x^{1-\varepsilon}\le p\le x\\p\text{ is prime}}}(1-\varepsilon)\log x\\ &=(1-\varepsilon)\left(\pi(x)+O(x^{1-\varepsilon})\right)\log x. \end{aligned}

The same lower estimate holds for \vartheta(x), while its upper bound \vartheta(x)\le\pi(x)\log x follows directly from its definition. After division by x, the error tends to zero. For either A=\vartheta or A=\psi, if \pi(x)\log x/x\to1, these inequalities give lower limit at least 1-\varepsilon and upper limit at most 1 for A(x)/x. Conversely, if A(x)/x\to1, they give lower limit at least 1 and upper limit at most 1/(1-\varepsilon) for \pi(x)\log x/x. Let \varepsilon\downarrow0.

Zero-free line

Let \zeta(s) denote the Riemann zeta function. We take for granted that ζ(s) is meromorphic in the half-plane Re(s) > 0, and is analytic there except for a simple pole at s = 1, and that there is a product formula

{\displaystyle \zeta (s)=\prod _{p}{\frac {1}{1-p^{-s}}}}

for Re(s) > 1. This product formula follows from the existence of unique prime factorization of integers, and shows that ζ(s) is never zero in this region, so that its logarithm is defined there and

{\displaystyle \log \zeta (s)=-\sum _{p}\log \left(1-p^{-s}\right)=\sum _{p,n}{\frac {p^{-ns}}{n}}\;.}

Write s = x + iy ; then

{\displaystyle {\big |}\zeta (x+iy){\big |}=\exp \left(\sum _{n,p}{\frac {\cos ny\log p}{np^{nx}}}\right)\;.}

Now observe the identity

{\displaystyle 3+4\cos \phi +\cos 2\phi =2(1+\cos \phi )^{2}\geq 0\;,}

so that

{\displaystyle \left|\zeta (x)^{3}\zeta (x+iy)^{4}\zeta (x+2iy)\right|=\exp \left(\sum _{n,p}{\frac {3+4\cos(ny\log p)+\cos(2ny\log p)}{np^{nx}}}\right)\geq 1}

for all x > 1. Suppose now that ζ(1 + iy) = 0. Certainly y is not zero, since ζ(s) has a simple pole at s = 1. Suppose that x > 1 and let x tend to 1 from above. Since {\displaystyle \zeta (s)} has a simple pole at s = 1 and ζ(x + 2iy) stays analytic, the left hand side in the previous inequality tends to 0, a contradiction.

Newman contour argument

The Chebyshev function \vartheta(x)=\sum_{p\leq x}\log p is obtained by dropping some of the terms from the series for \psi. By the comparison above, the PNT is equivalent to \lim_{x\to\infty}\vartheta(x)/x=1.

Likewise instead of {\displaystyle \ -{\tfrac {\ \zeta '(s)\ }{\zeta (s)}}\ } the function {\displaystyle \ \Phi (s)=\sum _{p}{\tfrac {\ \log p\ }{\;p^{s}\ }}\ } is used, which is obtained by dropping some terms in the series for {\displaystyle \ -{\tfrac {\ \zeta '(s)\ }{\zeta (s)}}~.} The functions {\displaystyle \ \Phi (s)\ } and {\displaystyle \ -{\tfrac {\ \zeta '(s)\ }{\zeta (s)}}\ } differ by a function holomorphic on {\displaystyle \ \Re (s)=1~.} As was shown in the zero-free-line argument, {\displaystyle \ \zeta (s)\ } has no zeroes on the line {\displaystyle \ \Re =1\ ,} so {\displaystyle \ \Phi (s)-{\tfrac {1}{\ s-1\ }}\ } has no singularities on {\displaystyle \ \Re (s)=1~.}

One further piece of information needed in Newman's proof, and which is the key to the estimates in his simple method, is that {\displaystyle \ {\tfrac {\ \vartheta (x)\ }{x}}\ } is bounded. We use Chebyshev’s bound \vartheta(x)=O(x) as a prerequisite.

Integration by parts shows how {\displaystyle \ \vartheta (x)\ } and {\displaystyle \ \Phi (s)\ } are related: For {\displaystyle \ \Re (s)>1\ ,}

{\displaystyle \Phi (s)~=~\int _{1}^{\infty }{\frac {\operatorname {d} \vartheta (x)}{\;x^{s}\ }}~=~s\int _{1}^{\infty }{\frac {\vartheta (x)}{\ x^{s+1}\ }}\operatorname {d} x~=~s\int _{0}^{\infty }{\frac {\;\vartheta (e^{t})\ }{\;e^{st}\ }}\operatorname {d} t~.}

Newman's method proves the PNT by showing the integral

{\displaystyle I\equiv \int _{0}^{\infty }\left({\frac {\ \vartheta (e^{t})\ }{e^{t}}}-1\right)\operatorname {d} t~.}

converges, and therefore the integrand goes to zero as {\displaystyle \ t\to \infty \ ,} which is the PNT. In general, the convergence of the improper integral does not imply that the integrand goes to zero at infinity, since it may oscillate, but since {\displaystyle \ \vartheta \ } is increasing, it is easy to show in this case. The source does not give the interval estimate establishing this monotonicity implication.

To show the convergence of {\displaystyle \ I\ ,} for {\displaystyle \ \Re (z)>0\ } let

{\displaystyle g_{T}(z)\equiv \int _{0}^{T}f(t)\ e^{-zt}\operatorname {d} t\quad } and {\displaystyle \quad g(z)\equiv \int _{0}^{\infty }f(t)\ e^{-zt}\operatorname {d} t\quad } where {\displaystyle \quad f(t)\equiv {\frac {\ \vartheta (e^{t})\ }{e^{t}}}-1\ }

then

{\displaystyle \lim _{T\to \infty }g_{T}(z)~=~g(z)~=~{\frac {\ \Phi (s)\ }{s}}-{\frac {1}{\ s-1\ }}\qquad {\text{where}}\quad z\equiv s-1\ }

which is equal to a function holomorphic on the line {\displaystyle \ \Re (z)=0~.}

The convergence of the integral {\displaystyle \ I\ ,} and thus the PNT, is proved by showing that {\displaystyle \ \lim _{T\to \infty }g_{T}(0)~=~g(0)~.} This involves change of order of limits since it can be written {\textstyle \ \lim _{T\to \infty }\ \lim _{z\to 0}g_{T}(z)~=~\lim _{z\to 0}\ \lim _{T\to \infty }g_{T}(z)\ } and therefore classified as a Tauberian theorem.

The difference {\displaystyle \ g(0)-g_{T}(0)\ } is expressed using Cauchy's integral formula and then shown to be small for large {\displaystyle \ T\ } by estimating the integrand: Fix {\displaystyle \ R>0\ } and {\displaystyle \ 0<\delta<R\ } so that {\displaystyle \ g(z)\ } is holomorphic in the region where {\displaystyle \ |z|\leq R~} and {\displaystyle ~\Re (z)\geq -\delta \ ,} and let {\displaystyle \ C\ } be the boundary of that region. Since 0 is in the interior of the region, Cauchy's integral formula gives

{\displaystyle g(0)-g_{T}(0)~=~{\frac {1}{\ 2\pi i\ }}\int _{C}{\bigl (}\ g(z)-g_{T}(z)\ {\bigr )}\ {\frac {\ \operatorname {d} z\ }{z}}~=~{\frac {1}{2\pi i}}\int _{C}{\bigl (}\ g(z)-g_{T}(z)\ {\bigr )}\ F(z)\ {\frac {\ \operatorname {d} z\ }{z}}\ }

where {\displaystyle \ F(z)\equiv e^{zT}\left(1+{\frac {\;z^{2}\ }{\;R^{2}\ }}\right)\ } is the factor introduced by Newman, which does not change the integral since {\displaystyle \ F\ } is entire and {\displaystyle \ F(0)=1~.}

To estimate the integral, break the contour {\displaystyle \ C\ } into two parts, {\displaystyle \ C=C_{+}+C_{-}\ } where {\displaystyle \ C_{+}\equiv C\cap \left\{z\ \vert \ \Re (z)>0\right\}\ } and {\displaystyle \ C_{-}\equiv C\cap \left\{z\ \vert \ \Re (z)\leq 0\right\}~.} Then

{\displaystyle \ g(0)-g_{T}(0)~~=~~\int _{C_{+}}\int _{T}^{\infty }H(t,z)\operatorname {d} t\ \operatorname {d} z~~-~\int _{C_{-}}\int _{0}^{T}H(t,z)\operatorname {d} t\operatorname {d} z~~+~\int _{C_{-}}g(z)\ F(z){\frac {\operatorname {d} z}{\ 2\pi iz\ }}\ ,}

where {\displaystyle \ H(t,z)\equiv f(t)\ e^{-tz}{\frac {F(z)}{\ 2\pi iz\ }}~.} Note that {\displaystyle \ {\frac {\ \vartheta (x)\ }{x}}\ ,} and hence {\displaystyle \ f(t)\ ,} are bounded; so let {\displaystyle \ B\ } be some upper bound: {\displaystyle \ B\geq {\bigl |}f(t){\bigr |}~.}

This bound, combined with the estimate {\displaystyle \ \left|F\right|\ \leq \ 2\ \exp \!{\Bigl (}T\ \Re (z){\Bigr )}{\frac {\ \left|\Re (z)\right|\ }{R}}\ } for {\displaystyle \ |z|=R\ ,} together give that the absolute value of the first integral must be {\displaystyle \ \leq {\frac {\ B\ }{R}}~.} The integrand over {\displaystyle \ C_{-}\ } in the second integral is holomorphic in the region between that contour and the left semicircle, which excludes zero, so by Cauchy's integral theorem, the contour {\displaystyle \ C_{-}\ } can be modified to a semicircle of radius {\displaystyle \ R\ } in the left half-plane without changing the integral, and the same argument as for the first integral gives the absolute value of the second integral must be {\displaystyle \ \leq {\frac {\ B\ }{R}}~.} Finally, letting {\displaystyle \ T\to \infty \ ,} the third integral goes to zero since {\displaystyle \ e^{zT}\ } and hence {\displaystyle \ F\ } goes to zero on the left part of the contour; at the endpoints \pm iR, the factor 1+z^2/R^2 vanishes. Boundedness on the fixed contour permits dominated convergence. Combining the two estimates and the limit get

{\displaystyle \limsup _{T\to \infty }\ {\bigl |}\ g(0)-g_{T}(0)\ {\bigr |}\ \leq \ {\frac {\ 2B\ }{R}}\ ~.}

This holds for any {\displaystyle \ R\ } so {\displaystyle \ \lim _{T\to \infty }g_{T}(0)=g(0)\ ,} and the PNT follows.

Statement

If \pi(x) counts primes at most x, then \pi(x)\sim x/\log x as x\to\infty.

Proof 1 — Newman contour argument with zero-free-line preliminary (outline)

Let \pi(x) count primes at most x, let \vartheta(x)=\sum_{p\le x}\log p, and let \psi(x)=\sum_{p^k\le x}\log p, with prime p and integer k\ge1.

For \psi, the equivalence of the PNT with \psi(x)/x\to1 follows from the easy estimates

\psi(x)=\sum_{\substack{p\le x\\p\text{ is prime}}} \log p\left\lfloor\frac{\log x}{\log p}\right\rfloor \le\sum_{\substack{p\le x\\p\text{ is prime}}}\log x =\pi(x)\log x

and (using big O notation), for any 0<\varepsilon<1,

\begin{aligned} \psi(x) &\ge\sum_{\substack{x^{1-\varepsilon}\le p\le x\\p\text{ is prime}}}\log p\\ &\ge\sum_{\substack{x^{1-\varepsilon}\le p\le x\\p\text{ is prime}}}(1-\varepsilon)\log x\\ &=(1-\varepsilon)\left(\pi(x)+O(x^{1-\varepsilon})\right)\log x. \end{aligned}

The same lower estimate holds for \vartheta(x), while its upper bound \vartheta(x)\le\pi(x)\log x follows directly from its definition. After division by x, the error tends to zero. For either A=\vartheta or A=\psi, if \pi(x)\log x/x\to1, these inequalities give lower limit at least 1-\varepsilon and upper limit at most 1 for A(x)/x. Conversely, if A(x)/x\to1, they give lower limit at least 1 and upper limit at most 1/(1-\varepsilon) for \pi(x)\log x/x. Let \varepsilon\downarrow0.

Zero-free line

Let \zeta(s) denote the Riemann zeta function. We take for granted that ζ(s) is meromorphic in the half-plane Re(s) > 0, and is analytic there except for a simple pole at s = 1, and that there is a product formula

{\displaystyle \zeta (s)=\prod _{p}{\frac {1}{1-p^{-s}}}}

for Re(s) > 1. This product formula follows from the existence of unique prime factorization of integers, and shows that ζ(s) is never zero in this region, so that its logarithm is defined there and

{\displaystyle \log \zeta (s)=-\sum _{p}\log \left(1-p^{-s}\right)=\sum _{p,n}{\frac {p^{-ns}}{n}}\;.}

Write s = x + iy ; then

{\displaystyle {\big |}\zeta (x+iy){\big |}=\exp \left(\sum _{n,p}{\frac {\cos ny\log p}{np^{nx}}}\right)\;.}

Now observe the identity

{\displaystyle 3+4\cos \phi +\cos 2\phi =2(1+\cos \phi )^{2}\geq 0\;,}

so that

{\displaystyle \left|\zeta (x)^{3}\zeta (x+iy)^{4}\zeta (x+2iy)\right|=\exp \left(\sum _{n,p}{\frac {3+4\cos(ny\log p)+\cos(2ny\log p)}{np^{nx}}}\right)\geq 1}

for all x > 1. Suppose now that ζ(1 + iy) = 0. Certainly y is not zero, since ζ(s) has a simple pole at s = 1. Suppose that x > 1 and let x tend to 1 from above. Since {\displaystyle \zeta (s)} has a simple pole at s = 1 and ζ(x + 2iy) stays analytic, the left hand side in the previous inequality tends to 0, a contradiction.

Newman contour argument

The Chebyshev function \vartheta(x)=\sum_{p\leq x}\log p is obtained by dropping some of the terms from the series for \psi. By the comparison above, the PNT is equivalent to \lim_{x\to\infty}\vartheta(x)/x=1.

Likewise instead of {\displaystyle \ -{\tfrac {\ \zeta '(s)\ }{\zeta (s)}}\ } the function {\displaystyle \ \Phi (s)=\sum _{p}{\tfrac {\ \log p\ }{\;p^{s}\ }}\ } is used, which is obtained by dropping some terms in the series for {\displaystyle \ -{\tfrac {\ \zeta '(s)\ }{\zeta (s)}}~.} The functions {\displaystyle \ \Phi (s)\ } and {\displaystyle \ -{\tfrac {\ \zeta '(s)\ }{\zeta (s)}}\ } differ by a function holomorphic on {\displaystyle \ \Re (s)=1~.} As was shown in the zero-free-line argument, {\displaystyle \ \zeta (s)\ } has no zeroes on the line {\displaystyle \ \Re =1\ ,} so {\displaystyle \ \Phi (s)-{\tfrac {1}{\ s-1\ }}\ } has no singularities on {\displaystyle \ \Re (s)=1~.}

One further piece of information needed in Newman's proof, and which is the key to the estimates in his simple method, is that {\displaystyle \ {\tfrac {\ \vartheta (x)\ }{x}}\ } is bounded. We use Chebyshev’s bound \vartheta(x)=O(x) as a prerequisite.

Integration by parts shows how {\displaystyle \ \vartheta (x)\ } and {\displaystyle \ \Phi (s)\ } are related: For {\displaystyle \ \Re (s)>1\ ,}

{\displaystyle \Phi (s)~=~\int _{1}^{\infty }{\frac {\operatorname {d} \vartheta (x)}{\;x^{s}\ }}~=~s\int _{1}^{\infty }{\frac {\vartheta (x)}{\ x^{s+1}\ }}\operatorname {d} x~=~s\int _{0}^{\infty }{\frac {\;\vartheta (e^{t})\ }{\;e^{st}\ }}\operatorname {d} t~.}

Newman's method proves the PNT by showing the integral

{\displaystyle I\equiv \int _{0}^{\infty }\left({\frac {\ \vartheta (e^{t})\ }{e^{t}}}-1\right)\operatorname {d} t~.}

converges, and therefore the integrand goes to zero as {\displaystyle \ t\to \infty \ ,} which is the PNT. In general, the convergence of the improper integral does not imply that the integrand goes to zero at infinity, since it may oscillate, but since {\displaystyle \ \vartheta \ } is increasing, it is easy to show in this case. The source does not give the interval estimate establishing this monotonicity implication.

To show the convergence of {\displaystyle \ I\ ,} for {\displaystyle \ \Re (z)>0\ } let

{\displaystyle g_{T}(z)\equiv \int _{0}^{T}f(t)\ e^{-zt}\operatorname {d} t\quad } and {\displaystyle \quad g(z)\equiv \int _{0}^{\infty }f(t)\ e^{-zt}\operatorname {d} t\quad } where {\displaystyle \quad f(t)\equiv {\frac {\ \vartheta (e^{t})\ }{e^{t}}}-1\ }

then

{\displaystyle \lim _{T\to \infty }g_{T}(z)~=~g(z)~=~{\frac {\ \Phi (s)\ }{s}}-{\frac {1}{\ s-1\ }}\qquad {\text{where}}\quad z\equiv s-1\ }

which is equal to a function holomorphic on the line {\displaystyle \ \Re (z)=0~.}

The convergence of the integral {\displaystyle \ I\ ,} and thus the PNT, is proved by showing that {\displaystyle \ \lim _{T\to \infty }g_{T}(0)~=~g(0)~.} This involves change of order of limits since it can be written {\textstyle \ \lim _{T\to \infty }\ \lim _{z\to 0}g_{T}(z)~=~\lim _{z\to 0}\ \lim _{T\to \infty }g_{T}(z)\ } and therefore classified as a Tauberian theorem.

The difference {\displaystyle \ g(0)-g_{T}(0)\ } is expressed using Cauchy's integral formula and then shown to be small for large {\displaystyle \ T\ } by estimating the integrand: Fix {\displaystyle \ R>0\ } and {\displaystyle \ 0<\delta<R\ } so that {\displaystyle \ g(z)\ } is holomorphic in the region where {\displaystyle \ |z|\leq R~} and {\displaystyle ~\Re (z)\geq -\delta \ ,} and let {\displaystyle \ C\ } be the boundary of that region. Since 0 is in the interior of the region, Cauchy's integral formula gives

{\displaystyle g(0)-g_{T}(0)~=~{\frac {1}{\ 2\pi i\ }}\int _{C}{\bigl (}\ g(z)-g_{T}(z)\ {\bigr )}\ {\frac {\ \operatorname {d} z\ }{z}}~=~{\frac {1}{2\pi i}}\int _{C}{\bigl (}\ g(z)-g_{T}(z)\ {\bigr )}\ F(z)\ {\frac {\ \operatorname {d} z\ }{z}}\ }

where {\displaystyle \ F(z)\equiv e^{zT}\left(1+{\frac {\;z^{2}\ }{\;R^{2}\ }}\right)\ } is the factor introduced by Newman, which does not change the integral since {\displaystyle \ F\ } is entire and {\displaystyle \ F(0)=1~.}

To estimate the integral, break the contour {\displaystyle \ C\ } into two parts, {\displaystyle \ C=C_{+}+C_{-}\ } where {\displaystyle \ C_{+}\equiv C\cap \left\{z\ \vert \ \Re (z)>0\right\}\ } and {\displaystyle \ C_{-}\equiv C\cap \left\{z\ \vert \ \Re (z)\leq 0\right\}~.} Then

{\displaystyle \ g(0)-g_{T}(0)~~=~~\int _{C_{+}}\int _{T}^{\infty }H(t,z)\operatorname {d} t\ \operatorname {d} z~~-~\int _{C_{-}}\int _{0}^{T}H(t,z)\operatorname {d} t\operatorname {d} z~~+~\int _{C_{-}}g(z)\ F(z){\frac {\operatorname {d} z}{\ 2\pi iz\ }}\ ,}

where {\displaystyle \ H(t,z)\equiv f(t)\ e^{-tz}{\frac {F(z)}{\ 2\pi iz\ }}~.} Note that {\displaystyle \ {\frac {\ \vartheta (x)\ }{x}}\ ,} and hence {\displaystyle \ f(t)\ ,} are bounded; so let {\displaystyle \ B\ } be some upper bound: {\displaystyle \ B\geq {\bigl |}f(t){\bigr |}~.}

This bound, combined with the estimate {\displaystyle \ \left|F\right|\ \leq \ 2\ \exp \!{\Bigl (}T\ \Re (z){\Bigr )}{\frac {\ \left|\Re (z)\right|\ }{R}}\ } for {\displaystyle \ |z|=R\ ,} together give that the absolute value of the first integral must be {\displaystyle \ \leq {\frac {\ B\ }{R}}~.} The integrand over {\displaystyle \ C_{-}\ } in the second integral is holomorphic in the region between that contour and the left semicircle, which excludes zero, so by Cauchy's integral theorem, the contour {\displaystyle \ C_{-}\ } can be modified to a semicircle of radius {\displaystyle \ R\ } in the left half-plane without changing the integral, and the same argument as for the first integral gives the absolute value of the second integral must be {\displaystyle \ \leq {\frac {\ B\ }{R}}~.} Finally, letting {\displaystyle \ T\to \infty \ ,} the third integral goes to zero since {\displaystyle \ e^{zT}\ } and hence {\displaystyle \ F\ } goes to zero on the left part of the contour; at the endpoints \pm iR, the factor 1+z^2/R^2 vanishes. Boundedness on the fixed contour permits dominated convergence. Combining the two estimates and the limit get

{\displaystyle \limsup _{T\to \infty }\ {\bigl |}\ g(0)-g_{T}(0)\ {\bigr |}\ \leq \ {\frac {\ 2B\ }{R}}\ ~.}

This holds for any {\displaystyle \ R\ } so {\displaystyle \ \lim _{T\to \infty }g_{T}(0)=g(0)\ ,} and the PNT follows.
