Statement

Let real random variables X,Y have joint probability density f_{X,Y} and marginal densities f_X,f_Y. At values (x,y) with f_X(x)>0 and f_Y(y)>0, define conditional densities by

f_{X\mid Y=y}(x)=f_{X,Y}(x,y)/f_Y(y),\qquad f_{Y\mid X=x}(y)=f_{X,Y}(x,y)/f_X(x).

Then f_{X\mid Y=y}(x)=f_{Y\mid X=x}(y)f_X(x)/f_Y(y).

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Conditional probabilities or conditional densities are explicitly defined as ratios. Calculus supplies densities and sums or integrals, and elementary set operations describe the events. These are small extensions sufficient for understanding the Bayes identity, without general conditional expectation.

Proof 1: Eliminate the joint density

For two continuous random variables X and Y, Bayes' theorem may be analogously derived from the definition of conditional density:

{\displaystyle f_{X\vert Y=y}(x)={\frac {f_{X,Y}(x,y)}{f_{Y}(y)}}}{\displaystyle f_{Y\vert X=x}(y)={\frac {f_{X,Y}(x,y)}{f_{X}(x)}}}

Bayes' theorem states:

{\displaystyle f_{X\vert Y=y}(x)={\frac {f_{Y\vert X=x}(y)f_{X}(x)}{f_{Y}(y)}}.}
Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

The algebra eliminates the same joint density from two given ratios. Understanding the actual objects requires continuous probability densities, joint densities and their one-variable marginals, accessible through single and double integration from undergraduate calculus. Conditional densities are explicitly defined by ratios here; no general regular conditional probability or measure-theoretic disintegration is required.
