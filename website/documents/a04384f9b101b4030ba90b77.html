Generalizing from an arbitrary individual is sound only when its name is independent of the assumptions and conclusion context. The eigenvariable condition formalizes the difference between reasoning about a genuinely arbitrary case and unjustifiably generalizing from a special one.

Statement

Let A be a first-order formula and let A[y/x] denote capture-avoiding substitution of the variable y for the free occurrences of x. Suppose the sequent \Gamma\vdash A[y/x],\Delta is logically valid and y is not free anywhere in \Gamma\vdash\forall xA,\Delta. Then \Gamma\vdash\forall xA,\Delta is logically valid.

Proof 1: Vary the fresh eigenvariable assignment

A sequent \Gamma\vdash\Delta means that if all formulas in the finite list \Gamma are true, at least one in the finite list \Delta is true. An empty left list is true and an empty right disjunction is false.

Of course concluding that \forall xA holds just from the fact that A[y/x] is true is not in general possible. If, however, the variable y is not free elsewhere in the conclusion (i.e. it can still be chosen freely, without influencing the other formulas), then, whenever \Gamma is true and every formula in \Delta false, the valid premise implies that A[y/x] holds for any value of y. This gives \forall xA.
