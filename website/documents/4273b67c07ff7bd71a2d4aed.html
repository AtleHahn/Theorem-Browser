Statement

Every orientation-preserving Euclidean isometry of \mathbb R^3 fixing a point is a rotation about a line through that point. Equivalently, if R is a real 3\times3 matrix with R^TR=I and \det R=1, there is a nonzero real vector n with Rn=n.

A bright US college student should be able to understand this statement after approximately 30 minutes of focused background research; this assesses understanding the claim, not its proof.

Proof 1: Determinant and transpose identities

Use the standard correspondence between an orientation-preserving Euclidean isometry fixing the origin and a real orthogonal matrix R with determinant 1. A nonzero fixed vector gives its rotation axis.

An orthogonal matrix has the fundamental property that its inverse is its transpose, that is

{\displaystyle \mathbf {R} ^{\mathsf {T}}\mathbf {R} =\mathbf {R} \mathbf {R} ^{\mathsf {T}}=\mathbf {I} ,}

where I is the 3 × 3 identity matrix and superscript T indicates the transposed matrix.

Computing the determinant shows that an orthogonal matrix ⁠{\displaystyle \mathbf {R} }⁠ has determinant ⁠{\displaystyle \det(\mathbf {R} )=\pm 1}⁠. Indeed,

{\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}1&=\det(\mathbf {I} )=\det \left(\mathbf {R} ^{\mathsf {T}}\mathbf {R} \right)=\det \left(\mathbf {R} ^{\mathsf {T}}\right)\det(\mathbf {R} )\\&=\det(\mathbf {R} )^{2}\\\end{aligned}}}

An orthogonal matrix with determinant +1 is a proper rotation, and one with a negative determinant −1 is an improper rotation, that is a reflection combined with a proper rotation.

It will now be shown that a proper rotation matrix R has at least one invariant vector n, i.e., Rn = n. Because this requires that (R − I)n = 0, we see that the vector n must be an eigenvector of the matrix R with eigenvalue λ = 1. Thus, this is equivalent to showing that det(R − I) = 0.

Use the two relations

{\displaystyle \det(-\mathbf {A} )=(-1)^{3}\det(\mathbf {A} )=-\det(\mathbf {A} )\quad }

for any 3 × 3 matrix A and

{\displaystyle \det \left(\mathbf {R} ^{-1}\right)=1\quad }

(since det(R) = 1) to compute

{\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}&\det(\mathbf {R} -\mathbf {I} )=\det \left((\mathbf {R} -\mathbf {I} )^{\mathsf {T}}\right)\\{}={}&\det \left(\mathbf {R} ^{\mathsf {T}}-\mathbf {I} \right)=\det \left(\mathbf {R} ^{-1}-\mathbf {R} ^{-1}\mathbf {R} \right)\\{}={}&\det \left(\mathbf {R} ^{-1}(\mathbf {I} -\mathbf {R} )\right)=\det \left(\mathbf {R} ^{-1}\right)\,\det(-(\mathbf {R} -\mathbf {I} ))\\{}={}&-\det(\mathbf {R} -\mathbf {I} )\\[3pt]\Longrightarrow \ 0={}&\det(\mathbf {R} -\mathbf {I} ).\end{aligned}}}

This shows that λ = 1 is a root (solution) of the characteristic equation, that is,

{\displaystyle \det(\mathbf {R} -\lambda \mathbf {I} )=0\quad {\hbox{for}}\quad \lambda =1.}

In other words, the matrix R − I is singular and has a non-zero kernel, that is, there is at least one non-zero vector, say n, for which

{\displaystyle (\mathbf {R} -\mathbf {I} )\mathbf {n} =\mathbf {0} \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad \mathbf {R} \mathbf {n} =\mathbf {n} .}

The line μn for real μ is invariant under R, i.e., μn is a rotation axis. This proves Euler's theorem.

Proof 2: Conjugate eigenvalues and determinant one

Note that R has either one real eigenvalue -1 or 1 and two complex eigenvalues e^{\pm i\theta} or three real eigenvalues from \{-1,1\}. Since det(R)=1 being the product of its three eigenvalues, we obtain that either all of them are 1 or only one of them is 1. Otherwise, the eigenvalues are \{-1,-1,-1\} or \{-1,1,1\} or \{-1,e^{\pm i\theta}\} implying det(R)=-1 which contradicts det(R)=1. So, there is a vector n of unit length such that R****n = n.

The line through this real fixed vector is a rotation axis, under the same matrix/isometry correspondence.
