Philosophical significance
Major

Choice permits a sphere, apart from countably many points, to be partitioned into congruent sets with a paradoxical part-whole relation. The result exposes limits of extending ordinary geometric area intuition to arbitrary sets, rather than describing a physical cutting procedure.

Review status

External proof editorially assessed; not independent mathematical certification

Statement

Assume the axiom of choice: every family of nonempty sets admits a choice of one element from each set. Let S^2=\{x\in\mathbb R^3:\|x\|=1\} be the unit sphere. There is a countable subset D\subset S^2 and a partition

S^2\setminus D=A\sqcup B\sqcup C

into three pairwise disjoint sets such that A, B, C and B\cup C are all congruent. Here congruent means that one set is the image of the other under a rigid motion of Euclidean three-dimensional space, and countable means finite or able to be listed in a sequence.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Basic undergraduate set theory provides countability and arbitrary subsets; the geometric congruences and finite partition are explicit. No measure theory or construction of the nonmeasurable pieces is needed to understand the statement.
