Statement

Let a transversal meet two distinct lines in a Euclidean plane at distinct points. At each intersection, call the angles opening toward the other intersection interior; the other two are exterior. Alternate interior angles are interior angles on opposite sides of the transversal. Corresponding angles lie on the same side of the transversal, one interior and one exterior. Consecutive interior angles are interior angles on the same side. Parallel here means nonintersecting. If the two lines are parallel, alternate interior angles and corresponding angles are equal, and each pair of consecutive interior angles sums to two right angles.

Proof 1: Use Euclid’s fifth postulate

If a transversal intersects two parallel lines, then the alternate interior angles are congruent. If not, then one is greater than the other, which implies its supplement is less than the supplement of the other angle. This implies that there are interior angles on the same side of the transversal which are less than two right angles, contradicting Euclid's fifth postulate: lines with such interior angles must intersect on that side.

On a transversal of two parallel lines, corresponding angles are congruent and the interior angles on the same side are equal to two right angles. These statements follow by applying the fact that opposite angles of intersecting lines are equal and that adjacent angles on a line are supplementary.

Proof 2: Construct a second parallel and use Playfair’s axiom

Local alternate-angle criterion

Suppose two lines cut by a transversal have an equal pair of alternate interior angles. If the two lines met, their meeting point together with the two transversal intersections would form a triangle. One of the equal alternate angles would be an exterior angle of this triangle, and the other an opposite interior angle. The triangle exterior-angle theorem says the exterior angle is strictly greater, a contradiction. Hence the lines cannot meet.

Suppose the alternate interior angles of the given parallel lines were unequal. Through the first transversal intersection construct another line making an alternate interior angle equal to the angle at the second intersection. This new line is distinct from the first given line, but the local alternate-angle criterion makes it parallel to the second given line. This contradicts Playfair’s axiom: through a point outside a line there is exactly one parallel to it. The alternate angles must therefore be equal. Once alternate interior angles are equal, vertical-angle equality makes corresponding angles equal. Adjacent angles on a straight line sum to two right angles, so the consecutive interior angles are supplementary.
