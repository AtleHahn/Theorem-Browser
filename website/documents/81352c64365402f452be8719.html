Statement

Let G=(X,Y,E) be a finite bipartite graph with disjoint vertex classes X,Y. A matching is a set of edges sharing no endpoints, and it saturates X when every x∈X is on a matching edge. Put N(W)=\{y\in Y:xy\in E\text{ for some }x\in W\}. An X-saturating matching exists if and only if |N(W)|\ge|W| for every W\subseteq X.

Proof 1: Hall’s marriage theorem for a finite bipartite graph

Necessity

In an {\displaystyle X}-perfect matching {\displaystyle M}, every edge incident to {\displaystyle W} connects to a distinct neighbor of {\displaystyle W} in {\displaystyle Y}, so the number of these matched neighbors is at least {\displaystyle |W|}. The number of all neighbors of {\displaystyle W} is at least as large.

Sufficiency

Consider the contrapositive: if there is no {\displaystyle X}-perfect matching then Hall's condition must be violated for at least one {\displaystyle W\subseteq X}. Let {\displaystyle M} be a maximum matching, and let {\displaystyle u} be any unmatched vertex in {\displaystyle X}. Consider all alternating paths (paths in {\displaystyle G} that alternately use edges outside and inside {\displaystyle M}) starting from {\displaystyle u}. Let {\displaystyle W} be the set of vertices in these paths that belong to {\displaystyle X} (including {\displaystyle u} itself) and let {\displaystyle Z} be the set of vertices in these paths that belong to {\displaystyle Y}. Then every vertex in {\displaystyle Z} is matched by {\displaystyle M} to a vertex in {\displaystyle W}, because an alternating path to an unmatched vertex could be used to increase the size of the matching by toggling whether each of its edges belongs to {\displaystyle M} or not. Therefore, the size of {\displaystyle W} is at least the number {\displaystyle |Z|} of these matched neighbors of {\displaystyle Z}, plus one for the unmatched vertex {\displaystyle u}. That is, {\displaystyle |W|\geq |Z|+1}. However, for every vertex {\displaystyle v\in W}, every neighbor {\displaystyle w} of {\displaystyle v} belongs to {\displaystyle Z}: an alternating path to {\displaystyle w} can be found either by removing the matched edge {\displaystyle vw} from the alternating path to {\displaystyle v}, or by adding the unmatched edge {\displaystyle vw} to the alternating path to {\displaystyle v}. Therefore, {\displaystyle Z=N_{G}(W)} and {\displaystyle |W|\geq |N_{G}(W)|+1}, showing that Hall's condition is violated.
