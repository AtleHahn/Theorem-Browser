Statement

Let g(s)=\sum_{n\geq1}a(n)n^{-s} be uniformly convergent for \Re s>\sigma. For c>\max(0,\sigma) and x>0, put A(x)=\sum_{n\leq x}^{\prime}a(n), giving the final term weight 1/2 when x is a positive integer. Then A(x)=\frac1{2\pi i}\operatorname{PV}\int_{c-i\infty}^{c+i\infty}g(z)x^z\,dz/z, where PV means the limit of symmetric vertical truncations.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Series and improper integrals belong to analysis; complex powers use the exponential. The vertical contour is parametrized by a real variable and principal value is explicitly symmetric truncation. Thus the transform identity is readable without distribution theory.

Proof 1: Laplace inversion outline

An easy sketch of the proof comes from taking Abel's sum formula

g(s)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{a(n)}{n^s} =s\int_1^\infty A(x)x^{-(s+1)}\,dx.

The midpoint values at the jumps of A do not affect this integral. This is nothing but a Laplace transform under the variable change x=e^t:

\frac{g(s)}s=\int_0^\infty A(e^t)e^{-st}\,dt.

Inverting it one gets Perron's formula. Here Laplace inversion is used with its midpoint convention at jumps and symmetric vertical truncations. Abel summation and Laplace inversion are prerequisites; the source supplies this transform-inversion sketch, not proofs of those theorems.

Level
Beyond undergraduate level
Level justification

The sketch combines Dirichlet-series Abel summation with complex Laplace inversion using principal-value vertical limits and jump midpoints. Verifying the inversion hypotheses for the step function and the convergence conventions is not developed locally; the cumulative analytic burden warrants retaining beyond undergraduate provisionally.
