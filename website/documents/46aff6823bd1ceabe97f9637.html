Oscillation of the inverse-square Euler equation

Statement

For a\in\mathbb R\setminus\{0\}, consider y^{\prime\prime}+(1/4-a)x^{-2}y=0 on x>0. Its general complex solution is y=Ax^{1/2+\sqrt a}+Bx^{1/2-\sqrt a}, with A,B\in\mathbb C. A nonzero real solution has infinitely many zeros tending to infinity if a<0, whereas for a>0 no nonzero real solution does.

Proof 1: Substitute powers and distinguish real and imaginary exponents — outline

To find the solutions for q(x)=(1/4-a)x^{-2}, substitute the 'Ansatz'

{\displaystyle y(x)=x^{n}}

which gives

{\displaystyle n(n-1)+{\frac {1}{4}}-a=\left(n-{\frac {1}{2}}\right)^{2}-a=0}

This means that (for non-zero {\displaystyle a}) the general solution is

{\displaystyle y(x)=Ax^{{\frac {1}{2}}+{\sqrt {a}}}+Bx^{{\frac {1}{2}}-{\sqrt {a}}}}

where {\displaystyle A} and {\displaystyle B} are arbitrary complex constants; this uses the standard basis theorem for second-order linear equations.

It is not hard to see that for positive {\displaystyle a} the nonzero real solutions do not oscillate while for negative {\displaystyle a=-\omega ^{2}} the identity

{\displaystyle x^{{\frac {1}{2}}\pm i\omega }={\sqrt {x}}\ e^{\pm (i\omega )\ln {x}}={\sqrt {x}}\ (\cos {(\omega \ln x)}\pm i\sin {(\omega \ln x)})}

shows that they do.
