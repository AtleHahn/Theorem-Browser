Statement

Let N be a fixed nonnegative integer. A gambler starts with a deterministic fortune x and wagers on independent fair coin flips at times 1,\ldots,N. Each wager is determined by the past flips and has absolute value at most a fixed finite constant c. Winning adds the wager and losing subtracts it. If the gambler stops at a stopping time \tau\le N determined by the information then available, including any forced stop at bankruptcy, and X_t denotes the fortune, then \mathbb E X_\tau=x.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

A stopping rule or a strategy depending on an entire observed history requires college probability/game-theory concepts rather than a fixed elementary experiment. A bright US college student should be able to understand the statement after about 30 minutes of focused background research; this rating concerns understanding the claim, not proving it.

Proof 1: Bounded-lifetime application of optional stopping

The gambler's fortune over time is a martingale, and the time \tau at which they decide to quit (or go broke and are forced to quit) is a stopping time. The finite lifetime gives the bounded-stopping-time hypothesis of the optional stopping theorem. So the theorem says that

\mathbb E[X_\tau]=\mathbb E[X_0]=x.

In other words, the gambler leaves with the same amount of money on average as when they started.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

There are finitely many coin histories, so conditional expectations are finite weighted averages. A martingale and stopping time here mean fair conditional increments and a history-dependent stopping decision. These explicit finite-process notions and the supplied bounded stopping theorem are a small extension of undergraduate probability.
