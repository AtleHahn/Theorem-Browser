Proof 1: Arcsine integral equals half pi times log two — outline

Values of the inverse sine integral can be determined by exchanging the order of integration using Fubini's theorem. By expanding the integrand and swapping the integration variables, an elementary antiderivative can be found. Here the integrand is nonnegative, justifying the exchange with the improper-integral interpretation:

{\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}&\int _{0}^{1}{\frac {\arcsin(x)}{x}}\,\mathrm {d} x=\int _{0}^{1}\int _{0}^{1}{\frac {{\sqrt {1-x^{2}}}\,y}{(1-x^{2}y^{2}){\sqrt {1-y^{2}}}}}\,\mathrm {d} y\,\mathrm {d} x\\&=\int _{0}^{1}\int _{0}^{1}{\frac {{\sqrt {1-x^{2}}}\,y}{(1-x^{2}y^{2}){\sqrt {1-y^{2}}}}}\,\mathrm {d} x\,\mathrm {d} y=\int _{0}^{1}{\frac {\pi \,y}{2{\sqrt {1-y^{2}}}(1+{\sqrt {1-y^{2}}}\,)}}\,\mathrm {d} y\\&=\left\{{\frac {\pi }{2}}\ln \left[2\left(1+{\sqrt {1-y^{2}}}\,\right)^{-1}\right]\right\}_{y=0}^{y=1}={\frac {\pi }{2}}\ln(2)\end{aligned}}}
Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

The proof exchanges nonnegative improper real integrals, then evaluates the inner integral and a logarithmic antiderivative. These involve ordinary real functions on the unit square, trigonometric substitutions and monotone improper limits, not arbitrary measure spaces. For fixed 0<x<1, substituting y=sin theta in the first inner integral gives arcsin(x)/x; for fixed 0<y<1 the other inner integral follows from x=sin theta and then tan theta. Undergraduate calculus and real analysis suffice.
