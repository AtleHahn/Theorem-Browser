Statement

Let D\subseteq\mathbb C be nonempty, connected and open, and let h:D\to\mathbb C be holomorphic. The following are equivalent: (i) h\equiv0; (ii) its zero set has an accumulation point in D; (iii) there is c\in D with h^{(n)}(c)=0 for every integer n\ge0.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Real analysis supplies differentiability and power series; complex linear coordinates supply the plane. Holomorphic means complex differentiable on an open set, with derivatives and series interpreted over the complex numbers. The zero-set or coefficient assertion then uses familiar limits, connectedness and series; no contour-integration theory is needed to understand it.

Proof 1: Equivalent characterizations of the zero holomorphic function — proof

(i \Rightarrow ii) holds trivially.

(ii \Rightarrow iii). Let c\in D be an accumulation point of the zeros of h. It is enough to show that h^{(n)}(c)=0 for all n\geq0.

If this is not the case, let m be the smallest nonnegative integer with h^{(m)}(c)\neq0. By holomorphy, we have the following Taylor series representation in some open neighborhood U of c:

\begin{aligned} h(z)&=(z-c)^m\cdot\left[\frac{h^{(m)}(c)}{m!}+\frac{(z-c)\cdot h^{(m+1)}(c)}{(m+1)!}+\cdots\right]\\ &=(z-c)^m\cdot u(z). \end{aligned}

By continuity, u is non-zero in some small open disk B around c. But then h\neq0 on the punctured set B-\{c\}. This contradicts the assumption that c is an accumulation point of \{h=0\}.

(iii \Rightarrow i). Define the set on which h has zero Taylor expansion:

T=\left\{z\in D\mid h^{(k)}(z)=0\text{ for all }k\geq0\right\} =\bigcap_{k=0}^{\infty}\left\{z\in D\mid h^{(k)}(z)=0\right\}.

We'll show T is nonempty, open, and closed. Then by connectedness of D, T must be all of D, which implies h=0 on T=D.

By (iii), c\in T, hence T is nonempty.

As h is holomorphic on D, for every w\in T, the Taylor series of h at w has non-zero radius of convergence. Therefore, the open disk B_r(w) also lies in T for some r>0. So T is open.

By holomorphy of h, it has holomorphic derivatives, so all h^{(n)} are continuous. This means that \{z\in D\mid h^{(k)}(z)=0\} is closed in D for all k. T is an intersection of closed sets, so it's closed.

Q.E.D.

The proof uses a supplied local Taylor-expansion theorem for holomorphic functions, then factors out the first nonzero power and uses continuity to exclude accumulating zeros. The propagation step is the standard nonempty-open-and-closed argument in a connected space. Starting from real power series, complex arithmetic and basic topology, the additional prerequisite is complex differentiability and its stated analytic-expansion property; no contour integration or proof of that property is required.
