Statement

In a finite partially ordered set, the minimum number of chains partitioning the set equals the maximum size of an antichain. A chain consists of mutually comparable elements; an antichain of mutually incomparable elements.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Partial orders, minimal elements, chains and antichains are undergraduate discrete-mathematical concepts; the finite or coordinatewise setting does not remove that abstraction. A bright US college student should be able to understand the statement after about 30 minutes of focused background research; this rating concerns understanding the claim, not proving it.

Proof 1: Apply perfect-graph complementation to Mirsky

We use Mirsky's theorem: the largest chain size in a finite partially ordered set equals the minimum number of antichains in a partition. The comparability graph joins distinct comparable elements; a clique consists of pairwise adjacent vertices, and a proper coloring partitions the vertices into independent sets.

Mirsky's theorem and Dilworth's theorem are also related to each other through the theory of perfect graphs. An undirected graph is perfect if, in every induced subgraph, the chromatic number equals the size of the largest clique. In the comparability graph of a partially ordered set, a clique represents a chain and a coloring represents a partition into antichains, and induced subgraphs of comparability graphs are themselves comparability graphs, so Mirsky's theorem states that comparability graphs are perfect. Analogously, Dilworth's theorem states that every complement graph of a comparability graph is perfect. The perfect graph theorem of Lovász (1972) states that the complements of perfect graphs are always perfect, and can be used to deduce Dilworth's theorem from Mirsky's theorem and vice versa (Golumbic 1980).

In the complement, cliques are antichains and color classes are chains, so perfection gives the required equality.
