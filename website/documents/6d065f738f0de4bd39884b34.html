Statement

On ([0,1],\mathcal B,\lambda) with Lebesgue probability measure, for positive integers n define X_n=n\,1_{(0,1/n)}. Then X_n\to0 pointwise and \mathbb E|X_n|=1 for all n, but the family is not uniformly integrable, where UI means \lim_{K\to\infty}\sup_n\mathbb E(|X_n|1_{\{|X_n|\ge K\}})=0.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

The example consists of explicit step functions on an interval. Their integrals are elementary areas, and the stated uniform-integrability condition is a supremum of the given tail integrals. No general measure construction is required for this example.

Proof 1: Constant-mass shrinking intervals

Let \Omega=[0,1]\subset\mathbb R, with Lebesgue probability measure, and define

X_n(\omega)=\begin{cases}n,&\omega\in(0,1/n),\\0,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Clearly X_n\in L^1, and indeed \mathbb E|X_n|=1 for all n. However,

\mathbb E\bigl(|X_n|1_{\{|X_n|\geq K\}}\bigr)=1\qquad\text{for all }n\geq K,

and comparing with the uniform-tail definition, it is seen that the sequence is not uniformly integrable.

The area under the strip is always equal to 1, but X_n\to0 pointwise: for any fixed \omega>0, eventually 1/n\leq\omega, while at zero X_n is always zero.

The alternative criterion of L^1 boundedness and uniform absolute continuity of the integrals also shows the failure. Its first clause is satisfied, as the L^1 norms of all X_n are 1, i.e., bounded. But the second clause does not hold: given any \delta>0, there is an interval (0,1/n) with measure less than \delta and

\int_{(0,1/n)}|X_m|\,d\lambda=1\qquad\text{for all }m\geq n.

Level justification

Undergraduate integration and sequence limits suffice here: uniform integrability is explicitly defined, and all integrals are interval indicators, so general Lebesgue integration theory is not needed to follow this example.
