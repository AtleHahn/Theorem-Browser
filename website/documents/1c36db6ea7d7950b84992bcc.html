Statement

The map F:\mathbb R^2\to\mathbb R^2, F(x,y)=(e^x\cos y,e^x\sin y), has a C^1 local inverse near every point of its domain but is not globally injective.

Proof 1: An everywhere locally invertible map that is not injective

Consider the vector-valued function {\displaystyle F:\mathbb {R} ^{2}\to \mathbb {R} ^{2}\!} defined by:

{\displaystyle F(x,y)={\begin{bmatrix}{e^{x}\cos y}\\{e^{x}\sin y}\\\end{bmatrix}}.}

The Jacobian matrix of it at {\displaystyle (x,y)} is:

{\displaystyle JF(x,y)={\begin{bmatrix}{e^{x}\cos y}&{-e^{x}\sin y}\\{e^{x}\sin y}&{e^{x}\cos y}\\\end{bmatrix}}}

with the determinant:

{\displaystyle \det JF(x,y)=e^{2x}\cos ^{2}y+e^{2x}\sin ^{2}y=e^{2x}.\,\!}

The determinant {\displaystyle e^{2x}\!} is nonzero everywhere. Thus the inverse function theorem guarantees that, for every point p in {\displaystyle \mathbb {R} ^{2}\!}, there exists a neighborhood about p over which F is invertible. This does not mean F is invertible over its entire domain: in this case F is not even injective since it is periodic: {\displaystyle F(x,y)=F(x,y+2\pi )\!}.

Level justification

Compute the two-by-two Jacobian determinant, apply the real inverse function theorem and exhibit distinct inputs differing by one trigonometric period. Multivariable calculus and elementary matrices suffice.
