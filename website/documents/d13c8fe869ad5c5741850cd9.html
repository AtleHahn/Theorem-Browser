Statement

For real x, an antiderivative of the principal inverse tangent \arctan x\in(-\pi/2,\pi/2) is x\arctan x-\tfrac12\ln(1+x^2).

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Understanding the statement involves single-variable differentiation and integration. These prerequisites fit undergraduate coursework or a brief extension of it. A bright US college student should be able to understand this statement after approximately 30 minutes of focused background research; this assesses understanding the claim, not its proof.

Proof 1: Apply the inverse-antiderivative formula to tangent

For a continuous invertible function f between real intervals and a primitive F of f, the inverse-antiderivative formula gives y f^{-1}(y)-F(f^{-1}(y)) as a primitive of f^{-1}. Take f(t)=\tan t on (-\pi/2,\pi/2), with inverse the principal arctangent and primitive F(t)=-\ln|\cos t|. With this f and f^{-1}(x)=\arctan(x),

\int\arctan(x)\,dx=x\arctan(x)+\ln|\cos(\arctan(x))|+C.

Since \cos(\arctan x)>0 and 1+\tan^2t=1/\cos^2t, this is x\arctan x-\tfrac12\ln(1+x^2).

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Tangent on (-pi/2,pi/2) is invertible with a nonzero derivative and primitive -log|cos t|. Apply the inverse-antiderivative formula, then use positivity of cosine on that interval and the Pythagorean identity. This is single-variable calculus.

Proof 2: Integration by parts with the inverse tangent

Consider the inverse tangent function {\displaystyle \arctan(x)}:

{\displaystyle I=\int \arctan(x)\,dx.}

Rewrite this as

{\displaystyle \int \arctan(x)\cdot 1\,dx.}

Now let:

{\displaystyle u=\arctan(x)\ \Rightarrow \ du={\frac {dx}{1+x^{2}}}}{\displaystyle dv=dx\ \Rightarrow \ v=x}

then

{\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}\int \arctan(x)\,dx&=x\arctan(x)-\int {\frac {x}{1+x^{2}}}\,dx\\[8pt]&=x\arctan(x)-{\frac {\ln(1+x^{2})}{2}}+C\end{aligned}}}

using a combination of the inverse chain rule method and the natural logarithm integral condition.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Set u=arctan x and dv=dx in integration by parts. The remaining integral x/(1+x^2) is evaluated by the substitution t=1+x^2. All steps are standard single-variable calculus.
