With nonatomic valuations, there exists an allocation maximizing the worst normalized share, then the next worst, and so on. This gives mathematical substance to a priority-for-the-worst-off fairness criterion, while the choice of that criterion and entitlement weights remains a normative judgment.

Statement

Let (U,\Sigma) be a measurable space and let V_1,\ldots,V_n be nonatomic countably additive probability measures on it, where n\ge1 is an integer. Let w_i>0 with \sum_iw_i=1. For each measurable partition X=(X_1,\ldots,X_n), sort the real numbers V_i(X_i)/w_i into nondecreasing order to obtain r(X)\in\mathbb R^n. There exists a partition whose vector r(X) is lexicographically maximal: at the first coordinate where two vectors differ, the maximal vector has the larger coordinate.

Level
Beyond undergraduate level
Level justification

The measure and partition hypotheses exceed calculus/linear algebra even though the ordered value vector is finite.

Proof 1: Compact lexicographic optimization — outline

For a measurable partition X=(X_1,\ldots,X_k) of U (empty pieces allowed), put M_X[i,j]=V_i(X_j). The external Dubins–Spanier theorem says that, for fixed k, the set \mathbb M of all these real n\times k matrices is compact. Take k=n. The compactness part of the Dubins–Spanier theorem implies existence of a leximin-optimal division, by maximizing the sorted relative-value vector lexicographically on the compact valuation-matrix set.

Level
Beyond undergraduate level
Level justification

Use compactness of the measurable partition-value matrices and maximize successive sorted coordinates. The finite optimization is undergraduate, but nonatomic vector-measure partitions and their compactness require graduate measure theory.
