Statement

Let (X,d) be a nonempty complete metric space and T:X\to X satisfy d(Tx,Ty)\le qd(x,y) for all x,y\in X, where 0\le q<1. Then T has a unique fixed point x^*. For every x_0\in X, the iterates x_n=T(x_{n-1}) converge to x^*.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Real analysis and basic topology supply metric spaces, Cauchy completeness and convergence. A contraction is the stated distance inequality and a fixed point satisfies T(x)=x. These are small extensions illustrated by x mapped to x/2 on the real line; no new theory is required to understand the assertion.

Proof 1: Cauchy sequence of successive iterates

Let {\displaystyle x_{0}\in X} be arbitrary and define a sequence {\displaystyle (x_{n})_{n\in \mathbb {N} }} by setting {\displaystyle x_{n}=T(x_{n-1})}. We first note that for all {\displaystyle n\in \mathbb {N} ,} we have the inequality

{\displaystyle d(x_{n+1},x_{n})\leq q^{n}d(x_{1},x_{0}).}

This follows by induction on {\displaystyle n}, using the fact that {\displaystyle T} is a contraction mapping. Then we can show that {\displaystyle (x_{n})_{n\in \mathbb {N} }} is a Cauchy sequence. In particular, let {\displaystyle m,n\in \mathbb {N} } such that {\displaystyle m>n}:

{\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}d(x_{m},x_{n})&\leq d(x_{m},x_{m-1})+d(x_{m-1},x_{m-2})+\cdots +d(x_{n+1},x_{n})\\[5pt]&\leq q^{m-1}d(x_{1},x_{0})+q^{m-2}d(x_{1},x_{0})+\cdots +q^{n}d(x_{1},x_{0})\\[5pt]&=q^{n}d(x_{1},x_{0})\sum _{k=0}^{m-n-1}q^{k}\\[5pt]&\leq q^{n}d(x_{1},x_{0})\sum _{k=0}^{\infty }q^{k}\\[5pt]&=q^{n}d(x_{1},x_{0})\left({\frac {1}{1-q}}\right).\end{aligned}}}

If d(x_1,x_0)=0, the sequence is constant and hence Cauchy. Otherwise, let {\displaystyle \varepsilon >0} be arbitrary. Since {\displaystyle q\in [0,1)}, we can find a large {\displaystyle N\in \mathbb {N} } so that

{\displaystyle q^{N}<{\frac {\varepsilon (1-q)}{d(x_{1},x_{0})}}.}

Therefore, by choosing {\displaystyle m} and {\displaystyle n} greater than {\displaystyle N} we may write:

{\displaystyle d(x_{m},x_{n})\leq q^{n}d(x_{1},x_{0})\left({\frac {1}{1-q}}\right)<\left({\frac {\varepsilon (1-q)}{d(x_{1},x_{0})}}\right)d(x_{1},x_{0})\left({\frac {1}{1-q}}\right)=\varepsilon .}

This proves that the sequence {\displaystyle (x_{n})_{n\in \mathbb {N} }} is Cauchy. By completeness of {\displaystyle (X,d)}, the sequence has a limit {\displaystyle x^{*}\in X.} Furthermore, {\displaystyle x^{*}} must be a fixed point of {\displaystyle T}:

{\displaystyle x^{*}=\lim _{n\to \infty }x_{n}=\lim _{n\to \infty }T(x_{n-1})=T\left(\lim _{n\to \infty }x_{n-1}\right)=T(x^{*}).}

As a contraction mapping, {\displaystyle T} is continuous, so bringing the limit inside {\displaystyle T} was justified. Lastly, {\displaystyle T} cannot have more than one fixed point in {\displaystyle (X,d)}, since any pair of distinct fixed points {\displaystyle p_{1}} and {\displaystyle p_{2}} would contradict the contraction of {\displaystyle T}:

{\displaystyle d(p_{1},p_{2})=d(T(p_{1}),T(p_{2}))\leq qd(p_{1},p_{2})<d(p_{1},p_{2}),}

where the equality is due to {\displaystyle p_{1},p_{2}} being fixed points of {\displaystyle T}, the first inequality is due to {\displaystyle T} being a contraction mapping, and the last inequality is due to {\displaystyle q<1} and {\displaystyle d(p_{1},p_{2})>0} as {\displaystyle p_{1}\neq p_{2}}.
