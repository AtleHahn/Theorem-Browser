Statement

Write i^2=-1 and e^z=\sum_{m=0}^\infty z^m/m! for the complex exponential. Define \cos z=(e^{iz}+e^{-iz})/2, \sin z=(e^{iz}-e^{-iz})/(2i), \cosh z=(e^z+e^{-z})/2, \sinh z=(e^z-e^{-z})/2. For real x, \cos(ix)=\cosh x, \sin(ix)=i\sinh x, \cosh(ix)=\cos x, and \sinh(ix)=i\sin x.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

The locally defined function expressions are algebraic, but the explicit infinite-power-series definition of the complex exponential requires limits and series from undergraduate analysis.

Proof 1: Substitute imaginary arguments in the exponential definitions

These formulas can even serve as the definition of the trigonometric functions for complex arguments x. For example, letting x = iy, we have:

{\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}\cos iy&={\frac {e^{-y}+e^{y}}{2}}=\cosh y,\\\sin iy&={\frac {e^{-y}-e^{y}}{2i}}={\frac {e^{y}-e^{-y}}{2}}i=i\sinh y.\end{aligned}}}

In addition

{\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}\cosh ix&={\frac {e^{ix}+e^{-ix}}{2}}=\cos x,\\\sinh ix&={\frac {e^{ix}-e^{-ix}}{2}}=i\sin x.\end{aligned}}}

The substitutions use only i squared=-1, but the complete statement defines e^z by an infinite complex power series. Understanding that definition requires undergraduate series and limits even though the identities themselves follow by algebra.
