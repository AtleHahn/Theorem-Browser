Statement

For a vector space X over its scalar field \mathbb K\in\{\mathbb R,\mathbb C\}, the algebraic dual X^{\#}, consisting of all scalar-valued linear maps on X, is closed in \mathbb K^X with the topology of pointwise convergence.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Linear algebra supplies scalar-valued linear maps and basic topology supplies product topology, closedness and compactness. Here the dual consists simply of all such maps, and the polar is explicitly an intersection of scalar inequalities. No continuous-dual or locally convex-space theory is needed to understand these concrete subsets of a product.

Proof 1: Pass additivity and homogeneity to the pointwise limit

Let {\displaystyle f\in \mathbb {K} ^{X}} and suppose that {\displaystyle f_{\bullet }=\left(f_{i}\right)_{i\in I}} is a net in {\displaystyle X^{\#}} that converges to {\displaystyle f} in {\displaystyle \mathbb {K} ^{X}.} To conclude that {\displaystyle f\in X^{\#},} it must be shown that {\displaystyle f} is a linear functional. So let {\displaystyle s} be a scalar and let {\displaystyle x,y\in X.} For any {\displaystyle z\in X,} let {\displaystyle f_{\bullet }(z):I\to \mathbb {K} } denote {\displaystyle f_{\bullet }}'s net of values at {\displaystyle z}

{\displaystyle f_{\bullet }(z)~{\stackrel {\scriptscriptstyle {\text{def}}}{=}}~\left(f_{i}(z)\right)_{i\in I}.}

Because {\displaystyle f_{\bullet }\to f} in {\displaystyle \mathbb {K} ^{X},} which has the topology of pointwise convergence, {\displaystyle f_{\bullet }(z)\to f(z)} in {\displaystyle \mathbb {K} } for every {\displaystyle z\in X.} By using {\displaystyle x,y,sx,{\text{ and }}x+y,} in place of {\displaystyle z,} it follows that each of the following nets of scalars converges in {\displaystyle \mathbb {K} :}

{\displaystyle f_{\bullet }(x)\to f(x),\quad f_{\bullet }(y)\to f(y),\quad f_{\bullet }(x+y)\to f(x+y),\quad {\text{ and }}\quad f_{\bullet }(sx)\to f(sx).}

Proof that {\displaystyle f(sx)=sf(x):} Let {\displaystyle M:\mathbb {K} \to \mathbb {K} } be the "multiplication by {\displaystyle s}" map defined by {\displaystyle M(c)~{\stackrel {\scriptscriptstyle {\text{def}}}{=}}~sc.} Because {\displaystyle M} is continuous and {\displaystyle f_{\bullet }(x)\to f(x)} in {\displaystyle \mathbb {K} ,} it follows that {\displaystyle M\left(f_{\bullet }(x)\right)\to M(f(x))} where the right hand side is {\displaystyle M(f(x))=sf(x)} and the left hand side is

{\displaystyle {\begin{alignedat}{4}M\left(f_{\bullet }(x)\right){\stackrel {\scriptscriptstyle {\text{def}}}{=}}&~M\circ f_{\bullet }(x)&&{\text{ by definition of notation }}\\=&~\left(M\left(f_{i}(x)\right)\right)_{i\in I}~~~&&{\text{ because }}f_{\bullet }(x)=\left(f_{i}(x)\right)_{i\in I}:I\to \mathbb {K} \\=&~\left(sf_{i}(x)\right)_{i\in I}&&M\left(f_{i}(x)\right)~{\stackrel {\scriptscriptstyle {\text{def}}}{=}}~sf_{i}(x)\\=&~\left(f_{i}(sx)\right)_{i\in I}&&{\text{ by linearity of }}f_{i}\\=&~f_{\bullet }(sx)&&{\text{ notation }}\end{alignedat}}}

which proves that {\displaystyle f_{\bullet }(sx)\to sf(x).} Because also {\displaystyle f_{\bullet }(sx)\to f(sx)} and limits in {\displaystyle \mathbb {K} } are unique, it follows that {\displaystyle sf(x)=f(sx),} as desired. Proof that {\displaystyle f(x+y)=f(x)+f(y):} Define a net {\displaystyle z_{\bullet }=\left(z_{i}\right)_{i\in I}:I\to \mathbb {K} \times \mathbb {K} } by letting {\displaystyle z_{i}~{\stackrel {\scriptscriptstyle {\text{def}}}{=}}~\left(f_{i}(x),f_{i}(y)\right)} for every {\displaystyle i\in I.} Because {\displaystyle f_{\bullet }(x)=\left(f_{i}(x)\right)_{i\in I}\to f(x)} and {\displaystyle f_{\bullet }(y)=\left(f_{i}(y)\right)_{i\in I}\to f(y),} it follows that {\displaystyle z_{\bullet }\to (f(x),f(y))} in {\displaystyle \mathbb {K} \times \mathbb {K} .} Let {\displaystyle A:\mathbb {K} \times \mathbb {K} \to \mathbb {K} } be the addition map defined by {\displaystyle A(x,y)~{\stackrel {\scriptscriptstyle {\text{def}}}{=}}~x+y.} The continuity of {\displaystyle A} implies that {\displaystyle A\left(z_{\bullet }\right)\to A(f(x),f(y))} in {\displaystyle \mathbb {K} } where the right hand side is {\displaystyle A(f(x),f(y))=f(x)+f(y)} and the left hand side is

{\displaystyle A\left(z_{\bullet }\right)~{\stackrel {\scriptscriptstyle {\text{def}}}{=}}~A\circ z_{\bullet }=\left(A\left(z_{i}\right)\right)_{i\in I}=\left(A\left(f_{i}(x),f_{i}(y)\right)\right)_{i\in I}=\left(f_{i}(x)+f_{i}(y)\right)_{i\in I}=\left(f_{i}(x+y)\right)_{i\in I}=f_{\bullet }(x+y)}

which proves that {\displaystyle f_{\bullet }(x+y)\to f(x)+f(y).} Because also {\displaystyle f_{\bullet }(x+y)\to f(x+y),} it follows that {\displaystyle f(x+y)=f(x)+f(y),} as desired. {\displaystyle \blacksquare }

Level justification

The only algebraic condition is linearity. The proof checks that scalar addition and multiplication commute with pointwise limits, hence the limit remains linear. From linear algebra and basic point-set topology, the additional preparation is the definition of a net and the characterization of closed sets by net limits. This single extension of the familiar sequence argument is small; no continuous-dual, weak-star completeness or locally convex theory is used.
