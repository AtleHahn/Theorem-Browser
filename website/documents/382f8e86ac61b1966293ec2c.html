Statement

Let D\subseteq\mathbb C be open, a\in D, and f holomorphic on D\setminus\{a\}. The following are equivalent: (1) f extends holomorphically across a; (2) f extends continuously across a; (3) f is bounded on some punctured neighborhood of a; (4) \lim_{z\to a}(z-a)f(z)=0.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Complex differentiability extends the calculus derivative; limits, compact sets, uniform approximation and zeros of functions are familiar analysis. Zero multiplicity is the order of the first nonzero Taylor coefficient, and circle integrals are parametrized ordinary integrals. These limited definitions suffice for the full claim.

Proof 1: Multiply by a double zero and divide the Taylor series — outline

The implications 1 ⇒ 2 ⇒ 3 ⇒ 4 are trivial. To prove 4 ⇒ 1, we first recall that the holomorphy of a function at {\displaystyle a} is equivalent to it being analytic at {\displaystyle a}, i.e. having a power series representation. Define

{\displaystyle h(z)={\begin{cases}(z-a)^{2}f(z)&z\neq a,\\0&z=a.\end{cases}}}

Clearly, {\displaystyle h} is holomorphic on {\displaystyle D\smallsetminus \{a\}}, and there exists

{\displaystyle h'(a)=\lim _{z\to a}{\frac {(z-a)^{2}f(z)-0}{z-a}}=\lim _{z\to a}(z-a)f(z)=0}

by 4, hence {\displaystyle h} is holomorphic on {\displaystyle D} and has a Taylor series about {\displaystyle a}:

{\displaystyle h(z)=c_{0}+c_{1}(z-a)+c_{2}(z-a)^{2}+c_{3}(z-a)^{3}+\ldots \,.}

We have {\displaystyle c_{0}=h(a)=0} and {\displaystyle c_{1}=h'(a)=0}; therefore

{\displaystyle h(z)=c_{2}(z-a)^{2}+c_{3}(z-a)^{3}+\ldots \,.}

Hence, near a, where {\displaystyle z\neq a}, we have:

{\displaystyle f(z)={\frac {h(z)}{(z-a)^{2}}}=c_{2}+c_{3}(z-a)+\ldots \,.}

However,

{\displaystyle g(z)=c_{2}+c_{3}(z-a)+\cdots \,.}

is holomorphic on a neighborhood of a. It agrees with f off a there, and together with the unchanged f elsewhere gives a holomorphic extension on D.
