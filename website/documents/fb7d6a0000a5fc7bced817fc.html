Statement

Let f,g be real-valued functions differentiable on an open interval I, and let a,b\in\mathbb R. Then (af+bg)^{\prime}(x)=af^{\prime}(x)+bg^{\prime}(x) for every x\in I.

Proof 1: Combine constant multiples with the sum rule

One special case of the product rule is the constant multiple rule, which states: if c is a number, and {\displaystyle f(x)} is a differentiable function, then {\displaystyle c\cdot f(x)} is also differentiable, and its derivative is {\displaystyle (cf)'(x)=c\cdot f'(x).} This follows from the product rule since the derivative of any constant is zero. This, combined with the sum rule for derivatives, shows that differentiation is linear.

Level justification

Proof 2: Expand the difference quotient and take limits

Fix x\in I. The functions f and g and their linear combination are considered on the common open interval I.

Let {\displaystyle a,b\in \mathbb {R} }. Let {\displaystyle f,g} be functions. Let {\displaystyle j} be a function, where {\displaystyle j} is defined only where {\displaystyle f} and {\displaystyle g} are both defined. (In other words, the domain of {\displaystyle j} is the intersection of the domains of {\displaystyle f} and {\displaystyle g}.) Let {\displaystyle x} be in the domain of {\displaystyle j}. Let {\displaystyle j(x)=af(x)+bg(x)}.

We want to prove that {\displaystyle j^{\prime}(x)=af^{\prime}(x)+bg^{\prime}(x)}.

By definition, we can see that

{\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}j^{\prime}(x)&=\lim _{h\rightarrow 0}{\frac {j(x+h)-j(x)}{h}}\\&=\lim _{h\rightarrow 0}{\frac {\left(af(x+h)+bg(x+h)\right)-\left(af(x)+bg(x)\right)}{h}}\\&=\lim _{h\rightarrow 0}\left(a{\frac {f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}}+b{\frac {g(x+h)-g(x)}{h}}\right)\\\end{aligned}}}

In order to use the limits law for the sum of limits, we need to know that {\textstyle \lim _{h\to 0}a{\frac {f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}}} and {\textstyle \lim _{h\to 0}b{\frac {g(x+h)-g(x)}{h}}} both individually exist. For these smaller limits, we need to know that {\textstyle \lim _{h\to 0}{\frac {f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}}} and {\textstyle \lim _{h\to 0}{\frac {g(x+h)-g(x)}{h}}} both individually exist to use the coefficient law for limits. By definition, {\textstyle f^{\prime}(x)=\lim _{h\to 0}{\frac {f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}}} and {\textstyle g^{\prime}(x)=\lim _{h\to 0}{\frac {g(x+h)-g(x)}{h}}}. So, if we know that {\displaystyle f^{\prime}(x)} and {\displaystyle g^{\prime}(x)} both exist, we will know that {\textstyle \lim _{h\to 0}{\frac {f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}}} and {\textstyle \lim _{h\to 0}{\frac {g(x+h)-g(x)}{h}}} both individually exist. This allows us to use the coefficient law for limits to write

{\displaystyle \lim _{h\to 0}a{\frac {f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}}=a\lim _{h\to 0}{\frac {f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}}}

and

{\displaystyle \lim _{h\to 0}b{\frac {g(x+h)-g(x)}{h}}=b\lim _{h\to 0}{\frac {g(x+h)-g(x)}{h}}.}

With this, we can go back to apply the limit law for the sum of limits, since we know that {\textstyle \lim _{h\rightarrow 0}a{\frac {f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}}} and {\textstyle \lim _{h\rightarrow 0}b{\frac {g(x+h)-g(x)}{h}}} both individually exist. From here, we can directly go back to the derivative we were working on.

{\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}j^{\prime}(x)&=\lim _{h\rightarrow 0}\left(a{\frac {f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}}+b{\frac {g(x+h)-g(x)}{h}}\right)\\&=\lim _{h\rightarrow 0}\left(a{\frac {f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}}\right)+\lim _{h\rightarrow 0}\left(b{\frac {g(x+h)-g(x)}{h}}\right)\\&=a\lim _{h\rightarrow 0}\left({\frac {f(x+h)-f(x)}{h}}\right)+b\lim _{h\rightarrow 0}\left({\frac {g(x+h)-g(x)}{h}}\right)\\&=af^{\prime}(x)+bg^{\prime}(x)\end{aligned}}}

Finally, we have shown what we claimed in the beginning: {\displaystyle j^{\prime}(x)=af^{\prime}(x)+bg^{\prime}(x)}.
