Statement

On the unit sphere let A,B be distinct nonantipodal points, and let C_1,C_2 lie in the same open hemisphere bounded by the great circle AB. Let A^*=-A, B^*=-B. If A^*,B^*,C_1,C_2 lie on one small circle, then the minor-arc triangles ABC_1 and ABC_2 have equal spherical area.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Three-dimensional geometry and calculus supply spheres, planes, angles and surface area. Great circles are central plane sections and small circles are other plane sections; a lune is the region between two semicircles. The explicit antipodes and arcs specify the configurations without abstract differential geometry.

Proof 1: Double each triangle to a spherical parallelogram — outline

Use the spherical parallelogram equal-area lemma: parallelograms on the same base with the same midpoint great circle have equal areas. A spherical parallelogram has congruent opposite sides; its diagonals bisect each other and divide it into congruent triangles.

Given two spherical triangles {\displaystyle \triangle ABC_{1}} and {\displaystyle \triangle ABC_{2}} each with its apex on the same arc of the small circle {\displaystyle l} through points {\displaystyle A^{*~\!\!}} and {\displaystyle B^{*}\!,} construct new segments {\displaystyle C_{1}D_{1}} and {\displaystyle C_{2}D_{2}} congruent to {\displaystyle AB} with vertices {\displaystyle D_{1}} and {\displaystyle D_{2}} on {\displaystyle l.} The two quadrilaterals {\displaystyle \square ABC_{1}D_{1}} and {\displaystyle \square ABC_{2}D_{2}} are spherical parallelograms, each formed by pasting together the respective triangle and a congruent copy. The parallelograms share their midpoint great circle. By the equal-area lemma, the two parallelograms have the same area, so the original triangles must also have the same area.

Proof 2: Project to the midpoint great circle — outline

Let C vary on the specified arc of the small circle l through A^*,B^*.

Let {\displaystyle m} be the great circle through the midpoints {\displaystyle M_{1}} of {\displaystyle AC} and {\displaystyle M_{2}} of {\displaystyle BC,} and let {\displaystyle A',} {\displaystyle B',} and {\displaystyle C'} be the perpendicular projections of the triangle vertices onto {\displaystyle m.} The resulting pair of right triangles {\displaystyle \triangle AA'M_{1}} and {\displaystyle \triangle CC'M_{1}} have equal angles at {\displaystyle M_{1}} (vertical angles) and equal hypotenuses, so they are congruent; so are the triangles {\displaystyle \triangle BB'M_{2}} and {\displaystyle \triangle CC'M_{2}}. Therefore, the area of triangle {\displaystyle \triangle ABC} is equal to the area of Saccheri quadrilateral {\displaystyle \square ABB'A',} as each consists of one triangle from each congruent pair and the quadrilateral {\displaystyle \square ABM_{2}M_{1}} pasted together. (If {\displaystyle C'} falls outside the arc {\displaystyle A'B',} then one of the two pairs of triangles will have negative signed area.) Because the great circle {\displaystyle m,} and therefore the quadrilateral {\displaystyle \square ABB'A',} is the same for any choice of {\displaystyle C} lying on the Lexell circle {\displaystyle l,} the area of the corresponding triangle {\displaystyle \triangle ABC} is constant.

Proof 3: Stereographic tangents and an inscribed angle — outline

Use stereographic projection, which preserves angles and maps spherical circles to circles or lines; take A to the origin and A^* to infinity. Write C for a variable apex on the specified arc l.

To prove relationships about a general spherical triangle {\displaystyle \triangle ABC,} without loss of generality vertex {\displaystyle A} can be taken as the point which projects to the origin. The sides of the spherical triangle then project to two straight segments and a circular arc. If the tangent lines to the circular side at the other two vertices intersect at point {\displaystyle E,} a planar straight-sided quadrilateral {\displaystyle \square ABEC} can be formed whose external angle at {\displaystyle E} is the spherical excess {\displaystyle \varepsilon =\angle A+\angle B+\angle C-\pi } of the spherical triangle. Let {\displaystyle O} be the center in the plane of the circular arc to which side {\displaystyle BC} projects. Then planar quadrilateral {\displaystyle \square OBEC} is a right kite, so the central angle {\displaystyle \angle BOC} is equal to the external angle at {\displaystyle E,} the triangle's spherical excess {\displaystyle \varepsilon .} The projected great circle through B,C also contains B^*. Planar angle {\displaystyle \angle BB^{*~\!\!}C} is an inscribed angle subtending the same arc, so by the inscribed angle theorem has measure {\displaystyle {\tfrac {1}{2}}\varepsilon .} This relationship is preserved for any choice of {\displaystyle C}; therefore, the spherical excess of the triangle is constant whenever {\displaystyle C} remains on the Lexell circle {\displaystyle l,} which projects to a line through {\displaystyle B^{*~\!\!}} in the plane. (If the area of the triangle is greater than a half-hemisphere, a similar argument can be made, but the point {\displaystyle E} is no longer internal to the angle {\displaystyle \angle BOC.})
