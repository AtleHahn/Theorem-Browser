Proof 1: A logarithmic integral equals pi squared over twelve — outline

The integral of the product of the reciprocal function and the natural logarithm is a polylogarithmic integral. Fubini’s theorem allows this to be evaluated by translating the integrand into a combination of rational fractions. The denominators do not vanish on the square, so the exchange of integrals is valid.

{\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}&\int _{0}^{1}{\frac {\ln(x+1)}{x}}\,\mathrm {d} x=\int _{0}^{1}\int _{0}^{1}{\frac {4}{3(x^{2}+2xy+1)}}+{\frac {2x}{3(x^{2}y+1)}}-{\frac {1}{3(xy+1)}}\,\mathrm {d} y\,\mathrm {d} x\\&{\hphantom {\quad \quad \quad }}=\int _{0}^{1}\int _{0}^{1}{\frac {4}{3(x^{2}+2xy+1)}}+{\frac {2x}{3(x^{2}y+1)}}-{\frac {1}{3(xy+1)}}\,\mathrm {d} x\,\mathrm {d} y\\&{\hphantom {\quad \quad \quad }}=\int _{0}^{1}{\frac {2\arccos(y)}{3{\sqrt {1-y^{2}}}}}\,\mathrm {d} y=\left[{\frac {\pi ^{2}}{12}}-{\frac {1}{3}}\arccos(y)^{2}\right]_{y=0}^{y=1}\\&{\hphantom {\quad \quad \quad }}={\frac {\pi ^{2}}{12}}\end{aligned}}}

Level justification

Integrate the explicit rational function over the unit square in the two orders and evaluate the resulting elementary antiderivative. Undergraduate multivariable integration suffices; the square integrand is continuous.
