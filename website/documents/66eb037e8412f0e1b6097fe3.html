Statement

A finite graph is planar if it can be drawn in the plane with vertices as points and edges as curves intersecting only at common endpoints. A minor is obtained by deleting vertices or edges and contracting edges (identifying their endpoints), with loops and repeated edges removable. Let K_5 be the complete graph on five vertices and K_{3,3} the bipartite graph with two parts of three vertices and every cross-part edge. A finite graph is planar if and only if it has neither K_5 nor K_{3,3} as a minor.

High school only
Level justification

Planarity, minors and the two forbidden finite graphs are explicitly drawing and edge-operation notions.

Proof 1: Kuratowski subdivisions and contractions (outline)

Use Kuratowski's theorem, according to which a graph is planar if and only if it does not contain as a subgraph a subdivision of one of the two forbidden graphs K_5 and K_{3,3}. A subdivision replaces edges by internally disjoint paths; in particular, K_5 and K_{3,3} are nonplanar.

If a given graph is planar, so are all its minors: vertex and edge deletion obviously preserve planarity, and edge contraction can also be done in a planarity-preserving way, by leaving one of the two endpoints of the contracted edge in place and routing all of the edges that were incident to the other endpoint along the path of the contracted edge. Thus a planar graph has neither forbidden minor.

Conversely, a nonplanar graph contains a subdivision of one of the two forbidden graphs by Kuratowski's theorem. A subdivision can be converted into a minor of the same type by contracting all but one edge in each path formed by the subdivision process.

Level justification

Use the explicit subdivision criterion and contract subdivided paths; route edges along a contracted edge to preserve a plane drawing. Elementary finite graph operations and plane drawings suffice.
