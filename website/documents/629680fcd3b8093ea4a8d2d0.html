Statement

For an ordinary improper integral define \widehat h(\xi)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-2\pi i x\xi}h(x)\,dx whenever it exists. Suppose k\geq1, f\in C^k(\mathbb R), all derivatives through order k tend to zero at both infinities, and \int_{\mathbb R}(|f|+|f^{(k)}|)<\infty. Then |\widehat f(\xi)|\leq I/(1+|2\pi\xi|^k), where I=\int_{\mathbb R}(|f|+|f^{(k)}|). For k\geq2, \widehat f is absolutely integrable.

Proof 1: Decay bound from an integrable derivative — proof

Repeated integration by parts gives the Fourier derivative identity

\widehat{f^{(k)}}(\xi)=(2\pi i\xi)^k\widehat f(\xi).

The integrations can first be performed on finite intervals; the endpoint terms vanish by the hypotheses, and the integrability of f and f^{(k)} permits passage to the limit.

Writing \mathcal Ff=\widehat f, the proof uses the fact, which is immediate from the definition of the Fourier transform, that

{\displaystyle \vert {\mathcal {F}}f(\xi )\vert \leq \int _{-\infty }^{\infty }\vert f(y)\vert \,dy.}

Using the same idea on the Fourier derivative identity above gives

{\displaystyle \vert (2\pi i\xi )^{k}{\mathcal {F}}f(\xi )\vert \leq \int _{-\infty }^{\infty }\vert f^{(k)}(y)\vert \,dy.}

Summing these two inequalities and then dividing by 1+|2\pi\xi|^k gives the stated inequality.

In other words, under these conditions the Fourier transform decays at infinity at least as quickly as 1/|\xi|^k. In particular, if k\ge2 then the Fourier transform is integrable.
