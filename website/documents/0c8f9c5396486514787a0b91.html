Statement

Let f:E\to F be a linear map between real or complex normed spaces. If f(E) is not meager in F (not a countable union of nowhere dense sets), then f is nearly open: for every neighborhood U of zero, \overline{f(U)} contains a neighborhood of zero. Continuity of f is not required.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Normed spaces combine linear algebra with a norm, and basic topology supplies closure and neighborhoods. Meager is explicitly a countable union of nowhere dense sets; near openness is also defined. These limited topological definitions suffice without dual-space theory.

Proof 1: Category and differences of image balls

Let U be a neighborhood of zero. Shrinking U, we can assume U is an open ball centered at zero. We have

f(E)=f\left(\bigcup_{n\ge1}nU\right)=\bigcup_{n\ge1}f(nU).

Thus, some \overline{f(nU)} contains an interior point y; that is, for some radius r>0,

B(y,r)\subset\overline{f(nU)}.

Then for any v in F with \|v\|<r, by linearity, convexity and (-1)U\subset U,

v=v-y+y\in\overline{f(-nU)}+\overline{f(nU)} \subset\overline{f(2nU)},

which proves the result by dividing by 2n.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Cover the linear image by images of scaled balls, use the definition of nonmeagerness to obtain interior in one closure, and subtract two nearby points. Undergraduate normed-space topology suffices with meager as the explicitly defined countable nowhere-dense condition; no Baire theorem or operator theory is needed.
