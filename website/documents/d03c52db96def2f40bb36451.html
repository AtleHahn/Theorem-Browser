Statement

Allow parallel halfplanes as infinite-radius circles tangent at infinity. For each integer n\ge3 there is a ring of n interior-disjoint generalized circles surrounding a unit circle, tangent to it and cyclically tangent to their neighbors, whose smallest radius is 1/(F_{2n-3}-1), where F_1=F_2=1 and F_{j+2}=F_{j+1}+F_j.

Level
High school only
Level justification

The halfplane/infinite-radius and tangency-at-infinity conventions are supplied. The remaining ring and Fibonacci data use elementary circle geometry and recurrence arithmetic.

Proof 1: Insert circles tangent to the previous pair — outline

Use the line-inclusive Descartes theorem: for four mutually tangent generalized circles with oriented curvatures k_i (zero for a line), (\sum_{i=1}^4 k_i)^2=2\sum_{i=1}^4 k_i^2.

An infinite sequence of circles can be constructed, containing rings for each {\displaystyle n} that exactly meet the bound of the ring lemma, showing that it is tight. The construction allows halfplanes to be considered as degenerate circles with infinite radius, and includes additional tangencies between the circles beyond those required in the statement of the lemma. It begins by sandwiching the unit circle between two parallel halfplanes; in the geometry of circles, these are considered to be tangent to each other at the point at infinity. Each successive circle after these first two is tangent to the central unit circle and to the two most recently added circles. The first {\displaystyle n} circles of this construction form a ring, whose minimum radius can be calculated by Descartes' theorem to be 1/(F_{2n-3}-1).

Level justification

Interpret lines as zero-curvature circles under the stated convention and apply the explicit Descartes equation to the circle-insertion construction. School circle geometry, quadratic algebra and Fibonacci recurrence arithmetic suffice; the source omits the recurrence and geometric verification, which is a completeness limitation rather than a reason to assign advanced theory.
