Statement

Let C be a counterclockwise piecewise smooth simple closed curve in \mathbb R^2, bounding the compact region D. Let L,M be real C^1 functions on a neighborhood of D. Assume D is both of the forms a\le x\le b, g_1(x)\le y\le g_2(x) and c\le y\le d, h_1(y)\le x\le h_2(y), with piecewise smooth boundary graphs. Then \oint_C(L\,dx+M\,dy)=\iint_D(M_x-L_y)\,dA.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Understanding the statement involves Real partial derivatives; Line and double integrals; Oriented planar boundaries. These prerequisites fit undergraduate coursework or a brief extension of it. A bright US college student should be able to understand this statement after approximately 30 minutes of focused background research; this assesses understanding the claim, not its proof.

Proof 1: Combine the two coordinate integration arguments — outline

Assume region {\displaystyle D} is a type I region and can thus be characterized by

{\displaystyle D=\{(x,y)\mid a\leq x\leq b,g_{1}(x)\leq y\leq g_{2}(x)\}}

where {\displaystyle g_{1}} and {\displaystyle g_{2}} are continuous functions on {\displaystyle [a,b].} Compute the double integral of L_y:

{\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}\iint _{D}{\frac {\partial L}{\partial y}}\,dA&=\int _{a}^{b}\,\int _{g_{1}(x)}^{g_{2}(x)}{\frac {\partial L}{\partial y}}(x,y)\,dy\,dx\\&=\int _{a}^{b}\left[L(x,g_{2}(x))-L(x,g_{1}(x))\right]\,dx.\end{aligned}}}

Now compute the line integral of L\,dx. {\displaystyle C} can be rewritten as the union of four curves: {\displaystyle C_{1},C_{2},C_{3},C_{4}.}

With {\displaystyle C_{1}}, use the parametric equations: {\displaystyle x=x,y=g_{1}(x),a\leq x\leq b}. Then

{\displaystyle \int _{C_{1}}L(x,y)\,dx=\int _{a}^{b}L(x,g_{1}(x))\,dx.}

With {\displaystyle C_{3}}, use the graph y=g_2(x) traversed from x=b to x=a. Then

{\displaystyle \int _{C_{3}}L(x,y)\,dx=\int _{b}^{a}L(x,g_2(x))\,dx=-\int _{a}^{b}L(x,g_{2}(x))\,dx.}

The integral over {\displaystyle C_{3}} is negated because it goes in the negative direction from {\displaystyle b} to {\displaystyle a}, as {\displaystyle C} is oriented positively (anticlockwise). On {\displaystyle C_{2}} and {\displaystyle C_{4},} {\displaystyle x} remains constant, meaning

{\displaystyle \int _{C_{4}}L(x,y)\,dx=\int _{C_{2}}L(x,y)\,dx=0.}

Therefore,

{\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}\oint _{C}L\,dx&=\int _{C_{1}}L(x,y)\,dx+\int _{C_{2}}L(x,y)\,dx+\int _{C_{3}}L(x,y)\,dx+\int _{C_{4}}L(x,y)\,dx\\&=\int _{a}^{b}L(x,g_{1}(x))\,dx-\int _{a}^{b}L(x,g_{2}(x))\,dx.\end{aligned}}}

Combining the double-integral calculation with the boundary-integral calculation gives \oint_C L\,dx=-\iint_D L_y\,dA for regions of type I.

A similar treatment yields \oint_C M\,dy=\iint_D M_x\,dA for regions of type II. In the notation of the statement, integrating M_x horizontally gives \iint_D M_x\,dA=\int_c^d[M(h_2(y),y)-M(h_1(y),y)]\,dy. On the right boundary y increases and on the left boundary it decreases; horizontal connecting sides contribute zero to M\,dy. Their sum is the same expression.

Putting the two together, we get the result for regions of type III. The source prescribes this symmetric second calculation without writing out its details.
