Statement

Write \Gamma(t)=\int_0^\infty u^{t-1}e^{-u}\,du for real t>0, \zeta(t)=\sum_{n\ge1}n^{-t} for real t>1, and \gamma=\lim_{N\to\infty}(\sum_{n=1}^N1/n-\log N). For real |x|<1, \log\Gamma(1+x)=-\gamma x+\sum_{k=2}^\infty\frac{\zeta(k)}k(-x)^k.

Proof 1: Expand the logarithm of the Weierstrass product — outline

Weierstrass's definition of the gamma function

{\displaystyle \Gamma (x)={\frac {\,e^{-\gamma \,x\,}}{x}}\,\prod _{n=1}^{\infty }\left(\,1+{\frac {x}{n}}\,\right)^{-1}e^{x/n}\!}

gives, for real |x|<1, the expression

{\displaystyle \log \Gamma (1+x)=-\gamma \,x+\sum _{k=2}^{\infty }{\frac {\,\zeta (k)\,}{k}}\,(-x)^{k}}

where {\textstyle \zeta (k)} is the Riemann zeta function.

The product is used for x>0, with \Gamma(1+x)=x\Gamma(x); its corresponding product for \Gamma(1+x) extends to x>-1. The source states the equivalence without displaying the logarithmic expansion or the interchange of sums.

Level justification

Take logarithms of the supplied product, expand log(1+x/n), and interchange absolutely convergent sums for |x|<1. Undergraduate real analysis of products and power series suffices.
