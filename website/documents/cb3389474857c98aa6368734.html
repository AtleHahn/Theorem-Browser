Statement

Let a<b and let f:[a,b]\to\mathbb R be continuous, differentiable on (a,b), and satisfy f(a)=f(b). Then f'(c)=0 for some c\in(a,b).

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Understanding the statement involves Continuous real functions; Differentiability on an open interval. These prerequisites fit undergraduate coursework or a brief extension of it. A bright US college student should be able to understand this statement after approximately 30 minutes of focused background research; this assesses understanding the claim, not its proof.

Proof 1: Apply the extremum argument and equate the one-sided derivatives

By assumption, f is continuous on [a, b], and by the extreme value theorem attains both its maximum and its minimum in [a, b]. If these are both attained at the endpoints of [a, b], then f is constant on [a, b] and so the derivative of f is zero at every point in (a, b).

Suppose then that the maximum is obtained at an interior point c of (a, b) (the argument for the minimum is very similar, just consider −f). We shall examine the right- and left-hand difference-quotient limits separately.

For a real h such that c + h is in [a, b], the value f(c + h) is smaller or equal to f(c) because f attains its maximum at c. Therefore, for every h > 0,

{\displaystyle {\frac {f(c+h)-f(c)}{h}}\leq 0,}

hence

{\displaystyle f'(c^{+}):=\lim _{h\to 0^{+}}{\frac {f(c+h)-f(c)}{h}}\leq 0,}

where the limit exists by differentiability.

Similarly, for every h < 0, the inequality turns around because the denominator is now negative and we get

{\displaystyle {\frac {f(c+h)-f(c)}{h}}\geq 0,}

hence

{\displaystyle f'(c^{-}):=\lim _{h\to 0^{-}}{\frac {f(c+h)-f(c)}{h}}\geq 0,}

where this limit also exists by differentiability.

Finally, when the above right- and left-hand limits agree (in particular when f is differentiable), then the derivative of f at c must be zero.
