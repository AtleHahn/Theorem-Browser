Statement

An inversion with center O and radius R>0 maps P\ne O to the point P' on the ray OP satisfying OP\cdot OP'=R^2. A generalized sphere means a sphere or a plane. Take an annular hexlet: a central sphere of radius r>0 between parallel planes separated by 2r, together with six radius-r spheres whose centers form a regular hexagon of circumradius 2r in the midplane. As the ring rotates about its central axis, its spheres have as envelope (a surface tangent to the moving spheres) the torus with inner radius r and outer radius 3r. Suppose an inversion maps three fixed mutually tangent generalized spheres to this central sphere and the two planes. Inverting the rotating annular spheres and their torus back gives a one-parameter family of hexlet spheres enveloped by an inverse torus, called a Dupin cyclide.

Level justification

The torus and inverse cyclide are described explicitly, but an envelope of a continuously moving sphere family requires a tangent-surface interpretation. This is a limited parametrized-surface extension of undergraduate multivariable calculus.

Proof 1: Rotate the annular ring and invert its torus — outline

An infinite variety of hexlets may be generated by rotating the six balls s1–s6 in their plane by an arbitrary angle before re-inverting them. The envelope produced by such rotations is the torus that surrounds the central sphere c of radius r and is sandwiched between the two bounding planes a and b; thus, the torus has an inner radius r and outer radius 3r. After the re-inversion, this torus becomes a Dupin cyclide.

Here s_1,\ldots,s_6 are the annular spheres; inversion preserves their tangencies with the envelope. The source leaves the envelope verification implicit.

Level justification

Understand the envelope of a continuously moving sphere family as a tangent surface, identify the torus and transfer tangencies by inversion. Elementary parametrized surfaces and Euclidean geometry suffice; the cyclide is defined here simply as an inverse torus.
