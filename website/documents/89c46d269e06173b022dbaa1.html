Statement

If \gamma_1,\ldots,\gamma_n are distinct complex algebraic numbers and b_1,\ldots,b_n\in\mathbb Z are not all zero, then \sum_{i=1}^n b_i e^{\gamma_i}\ne0.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Polynomial roots, rational linear independence and complex exponentials belong to undergraduate algebra and calculus. Algebraic numbers are polynomial roots over Q; algebraic independence is the explicitly given absence of a polynomial relation. These definitions suffice without number-field or Galois theory.

Proof 1: Symmetrize a relation and isolate its largest exponent

Use the complete-root exponential-sum lemma: Let R_1,\ldots,R_r be pairwise disjoint nonempty finite sets of distinct complex roots, each the full root set of a nonconstant integer polynomial. The numbers \sum_{\alpha\in R_t}e^\alpha, 1\le t\le r, are linearly independent over \mathbb Q.

Assume the integer coefficients are not all zero. Assuming

{\displaystyle b(1)e^{\gamma (1)}+\cdots +b(n)e^{\gamma (n)}=0,}

we will derive a contradiction.

Let us choose a polynomial with integer coefficients which vanishes on all the {\displaystyle \gamma (k)}'s and let {\displaystyle \gamma (1),\ldots ,\gamma (n),\gamma (n+1),\ldots ,\gamma (N)} be all its distinct roots. Let b(n + 1) = ... = b(N) = 0.

Let S_N denote the permutation group of \{1,\ldots,N\}. The polynomial

{\displaystyle P(x_{1},\dots ,x_{N})=\prod _{\sigma \in S_{N}}(b(1)x_{\sigma (1)}+\cdots +b(N)x_{\sigma (N)})}

vanishes at {\displaystyle (e^{\gamma (1)},\dots ,e^{\gamma (N)})} by assumption. Since the product is symmetric, for any {\displaystyle \tau \in S_{N}} the monomials {\displaystyle x_{\tau (1)}^{h_{1}}\cdots x_{\tau (N)}^{h_{N}}} and {\displaystyle x_{1}^{h_{1}}\cdots x_{N}^{h_{N}}} have the same coefficient in the expansion of P.

Thus, expanding {\displaystyle P(e^{\gamma (1)},\dots ,e^{\gamma (N)})} accordingly and grouping the terms with the same exponent, we see that the resulting exponents {\displaystyle h_{1}\gamma (1)+\dots +h_{N}\gamma (N)} form a union of complete conjugacy groups and, if two terms have conjugate exponents, they are multiplied by the same coefficient.

So we are in the situation of the complete-root exponential-sum lemma. To reach a contradiction it suffices to see that at least one of the coefficients is non-zero. This is seen by equipping C with the lexicographic order on real and then imaginary parts and by choosing for each factor in the product the term with non-zero coefficient which has maximum exponent according to this ordering: the product of these terms has non-zero coefficient in the expansion and does not get simplified by any other term. This proves the statement. ∎
