Statement

Let a>b>0 be relatively prime integers and let n\geq1 be an integer. There is a prime p that divides a^n-b^n and divides none of the integers a^k-b^k for 1\leq k<n, except in exactly the following cases:

  • n=1 and a-b=1;
  • n=2 and a+b is a power of 2;
  • n=6, a=2, and b=1.

In each exceptional case no such prime exists. A prime with the stated property is called a primitive prime divisor; relatively prime means having no common positive divisor other than 1.

Level
High school only
Level justification

Only elementary integer arithmetic and divisibility are needed; primitive prime divisor is explicitly defined by finitely many earlier powers.

For n>1, the corrected bound in Lemma7 is (x-1)^{\varphi(n)}<\Phi_n(x)\leq(x+1)^{\varphi(n)} for x>1, with upper equality only when n=2. Indeed |x-\zeta|>x-1 for every primitive nth root, while |x-\zeta|\leq x+1. These bounds suffice for all three cases in Section2. In its final case, separate q=1: then \Psi_p(a,b)=a^p-b^p>p when a-b=1. For q\geq2, the displayed lower estimate remains strict with the corrected upper bound, forcing p=3,q=2 and then a=2,b=1. Finally n=1 reduces to prime-divisor existence for a-b>1. If n=2 and a+b is a power of two, every prime divisor already divides a-b; for (a,b,n)=(2,1,6) the primes 3,7 already occur at exponents 2,3.
