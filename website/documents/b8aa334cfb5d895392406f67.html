Statement

For a finite measure space (S,\mathcal B,m) and \delta>0, call a measurable set \delta-indivisible if it cannot be split into two measurable subsets both having measure at least \delta. There is a partition of S into at most \lfloor m(S)/\delta\rfloor+1 such sets.

Level
Beyond undergraduate level
Level justification

The statement uses arbitrary measurable sets and their finite positive measure, without complex measures or integration.

Proof 1: Repeated splitting and finite total mass

If S is empty, the empty partition suffices. Otherwise, start with the partition \pi_1=\{S\}. Assume that \pi_n is already defined. If all sets in \pi_n are already \delta-indivisible, then set \pi_{n+1}=\pi_n. If not, pick one set B\in\pi_n that is not \delta-indivisible. Then B can be partitioned into two disjoint measurable subsets B_1,B_2\in\mathcal B satisfying m(B_1)\geq\delta and m(B_2)\geq\delta. In this case, set

\pi_{n+1}=(\pi_n\setminus\{B\})\cup\{B_1,B_2\}.

This process must stabilize after at most \lfloor m(S)/\delta\rfloor splittings, since each splitting creates a new set of measure at least \delta. Indeed, after any splitting all cells have measure at least \delta, and their disjointness bounds their number by \lfloor m(S)/\delta\rfloor. Starting from one cell, the final partition therefore has at most \lfloor m(S)/\delta\rfloor+1 cells; if no split occurs, it has one.

Level
Beyond undergraduate level
Level justification

The termination count is finite, but the full construction splits arbitrary measurable subsets of a general finite measure space. Retain beyond undergraduate provisionally for that measure-theoretic setting rather than replacing it by a finite weighted set.
