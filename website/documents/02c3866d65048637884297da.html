Statement

Let x,y belong to a unital semiring and satisfy xy=yx. For every integer n\ge0, (x+y)^n=\sum_{k=0}^n\binom nk x^{n-k}y^k, where integer coefficients act by repeated addition and zero powers are the unit. For integers n\ge0 and 0\le k\le n, write \binom nk=n!/(k!(n-k)!), with 0!=1.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Elementary algebra and order theory supply operations, distributivity, meets and joins. Semirings remove additive inverses from familiar ring axioms; Boolean complementation or finite lattice representation introduces explicitly described algebraic conditions. These are closely related definitions rather than a new prerequisite chain.

Proof 1: Count words and commute the two elements

Count the formal choices of x and y before evaluating them in the semiring; xy=yx permits rearranging the factors.

Expanding (x + y)n yields the sum of the 2n products of the form e1e2 ... en where each ei is x or y. Rearranging factors shows that each product equals xn−kyk for some k between 0 and n. For a given k, the following are proved equal in succession:

  • the number of formal terms with xn−kyk in the expansion
  • the number of n-character x,y strings having y in exactly k positions
  • the number of k-element subsets of {1, 2, ..., n}
  • {\displaystyle {\tbinom {n}{k}},} either by definition, or by a short combinatorial argument if one is defining {\displaystyle {\tbinom {n}{k}}} as {\displaystyle {\tfrac {n!}{k!(n-k)!}}.}

This proves the binomial theorem.

For example:

The coefficient of xy2 in

{\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}(x+y)^{3}&=(x+y)(x+y)(x+y)\\&=xxx+xxy+xyx+{\underline {xyy}}+yxx+{\underline {yxy}}+{\underline {yyx}}+yyy\\&=x^{3}+3x^{2}y+{\underline {3xy^{2}}}+y^{3}\end{aligned}}}

equals {\displaystyle {\tbinom {3}{2}}=3} because there are three x,y strings of length 3 with exactly two y's, namely,

{\displaystyle xyy,\;yxy,\;yyx,}

corresponding to the three 2-element subsets of {1, 2, 3}, namely,

{\displaystyle \{2,3\},\;\{1,3\},\;\{1,2\},}

where each subset specifies the positions of the y in a corresponding string.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

The counting argument is elementary, but the full claim is in an arbitrary unital semiring. From introductory ring theory, omitting additive inverses is a small definitional extension. Distributivity and the single commutation relation suffice; no structure theory of semirings is needed.
