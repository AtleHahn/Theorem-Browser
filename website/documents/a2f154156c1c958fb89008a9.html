Statement

A right triangle with positive integer side lengths cannot have area equal to the square of an integer.

Proof 1: Multiply the sum and difference of the leg equations

Let the right triangle have sides (u, v, w), where the area equals uv/2 and, by the Pythagorean theorem, u2 + v2 = w2. If the area were equal to the square of an integer s

uv/2 = s2

then by algebraic manipulations it would also be the case that

2uv = 4s2 and −2uv = −4s2.

Adding u2 + v2 = w2 to these equations gives

u2 + 2uv + v2 = w2 + 4s2 and u2 − 2uv + v2 = w2 − 4s2,

which can be expressed as

(u + v)2 = w2 + 4s2 and (u − v)2 = w2 − 4s2.

Multiplying these equations together yields

(u2 − v2)2 = w4 − 16s4.

The legs cannot be equal, since a positive integer right triangle with equal legs would make \sqrt2 rational. We use Fermat's difference-of-fourth-powers theorem: there can be no nonzero integer solution to the equation x4 − y4 = z2, of which this is a special case with z = u2 − v2, x = w and y = 2s.

High school only
Level justification

Combine the squared sum and difference of the legs, multiply, and apply the supplied fourth-power obstruction. The Pythagorean identity and irrationality of sqrt(2) exclude equal legs. All applied claims are elementary integer and Euclidean statements.
