Statement

Let x_1,\ldots,x_N be nonzero vectors in \mathbb R^n, where N,n,k are positive integers, and let 0<\varepsilon<1. Let A be a k\times n matrix with independent standard normal entries, and P=A/\sqrt{k}. With c=\frac{k}{2}(\varepsilon^2/2-\varepsilon^3/3), the probability that (1-\varepsilon)\|x_j\|^2\leq\|Px_j\|^2\leq(1+\varepsilon)\|x_j\|^2 holds for every j is at least 1-2Ne^{-c}. In particular, for d>0 and k\geq4(d+1)\ln(2N)/(\varepsilon^2(1-2\varepsilon/3)), this probability is at least 1-(2N)^{-d}.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

The standard normal law is given by its elementary Gaussian density, and independence gives finite products of such laws. Calculus and linear algebra then interpret quantiles or matrix norm inequalities. These limited finite-dimensional probability additions do not require general stochastic processes.

Proof 1: Chi-square concentration and the union bound

Construct a random matrix {\displaystyle A\sim {\mathcal {N}}(0,1)^{k\times n}}, obtained by independently sampling each entry from the standard normal distribution. Define {\textstyle P:={\frac {A}{\sqrt {k}}}}, then for any nonzero vector {\displaystyle x\in \mathbb {R} ^{n}}, let the projected vector be {\displaystyle {\hat {x}}:=Ax}. Standard geometric arguments show that {\textstyle r:={\frac {\|{\hat {x}}\|^{2}}{\|x\|^{2}}}} is chi-square distributed, that is, {\textstyle r\sim \chi ^{2}(k)}. Thus, it satisfies a concentration inequality for the chi-squared distribution:

{\displaystyle \Pr((1-\epsilon)k\le r\le(1+\epsilon)k)\geq 1-2e^{-{\frac {k}{2}}\left({\frac {1}{2}}\epsilon ^{2}-{\frac {1}{3}}\epsilon ^{3}\right)}}

By the union bound, the probability that this relation is true for all of {\displaystyle x_{1},\dots ,x_{N}} is at least {\textstyle 1-2Ne^{-{\frac {k}{2}}({\frac {1}{2}}\epsilon ^{2}-{\frac {1}{3}}\epsilon ^{3})}}.

Since \|Px\|^2/\|x\|^2=r/k, these are the asserted norm bounds. For d>0, when {\textstyle k\geq {\frac {4(d+1)\ln 2N}{\epsilon ^{2}\left(1-{\frac {2\epsilon }{3}}\right)}}}, the probability is greater than, or equal to {\textstyle 1-{\frac {1}{(2N)^{d}}}}.
