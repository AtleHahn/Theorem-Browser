Statement

For an integer base b\ge2, hereditary base-b notation expands a nonnegative integer in base b, then recursively expands all exponents the same way. Coefficients lie in \{0,\ldots,b-1\}. Let B_{b,c}(a) replace every base b in that notation by c\ge b. The Goodstein sequence starts at G_m(1)=m and, while positive, obeys G_m(n+1)=B_{n+1,n+2}(G_m(n))-1; it stops at zero. For every nonnegative integer m, this sequence reaches zero after finitely many terms.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Hereditary base notation recursively rewrites exponents before changing base; understanding the specified process requires undergraduate recursive reasoning, although its arithmetic examples and termination claim do not require graduate ordinal theory. A bright US college student should be able to understand the statement after about 30 minutes of focused background research; this rating concerns understanding the claim, not proving it.

Proof 1: Descend through hereditary ordinal values — outline

Given a Goodstein sequence G_m, we construct a parallel sequence P_m of ordinal numbers in Cantor normal form that is strictly decreasing while the Goodstein terms remain positive. A common misunderstanding of this proof is to believe that G_m goes to 0 because it is dominated by P_m. The domination plays no role: the important point is that a positive Goodstein term always has a next term, and each such step strictly decreases the corresponding ordinal.

If m=0, the sequence has already terminated. Otherwise, proceed as follows. In the ordinal expressions below, finite coefficients are written on the right.

We define a function {\displaystyle f=f(u,k)} that computes the hereditary base {\displaystyle k} representation of {\displaystyle u} and then replaces each occurrence of the base {\displaystyle k} with the first infinite ordinal number {\displaystyle \omega }. For example, {\displaystyle f(100,3)=f(3^{3^{1}+1}+2\cdot 3^{2}+1,3)=\omega ^{\omega ^{1}+1}+\omega ^{2}\cdot 2+1=\omega ^{\omega +1}+\omega ^{2}\cdot 2+1}.

Each term {\displaystyle P_{m}(n)} of the sequence {\displaystyle P_{m}} is then defined as {\displaystyle f(G_{m}(n),n+1)}. For example, {\displaystyle G_{3}(1)=3=2^{1}+2^{0}} and {\displaystyle P_{3}(1)=f(2^{1}+2^{0},2)=\omega ^{1}+\omega ^{0}=\omega +1}. Addition, multiplication and exponentiation of ordinal numbers are well defined.

Cantor normal-form comparison makes f(u,k) strictly increasing in u for fixed k; the recursive comparison is left implicit in the source. While G_m(n)>0, we claim that {\displaystyle f(G_{m}(n),n+1)>f(G_{m}(n+1),n+2)}:

Let {\displaystyle G'_{m}(n)} be {\displaystyle G_{m}(n)} after applying the first, base-changing operation in generating the next element of the Goodstein sequence, but before the second minus 1 operation in this generation. Observe that {\displaystyle G_{m}(n+1)=G'_{m}(n)-1}.

Then {\displaystyle f(G_{m}(n),n+1)=f(G'_{m}(n),n+2)}. Now we apply the minus 1 operation, and {\displaystyle f(G'_{m}(n),n+2)>f(G_{m}(n+1),n+2)}, as {\displaystyle G'_{m}(n)=G_{m}(n+1)+1}.

For example, {\displaystyle G_{4}(1)=2^{2}} and {\displaystyle G_{4}(2)=2\cdot 3^{2}+2\cdot 3+2}, so {\displaystyle f(2^{2},2)=\omega ^{\omega }} and {\displaystyle f(2\cdot 3^{2}+2\cdot 3+2,3)=\omega ^{2}\cdot 2+\omega \cdot 2+2}, which is strictly smaller. Note that in order to calculate {\displaystyle f(G_{m}(n),n+1)}, we first need to write {\displaystyle G_{m}(n)} in hereditary base {\displaystyle n+1} notation, as for instance the expression {\displaystyle \omega ^{\omega }-1} is not an ordinal.

Thus the sequence {\displaystyle P_{m}} is strictly decreasing. As the standard order < on ordinals is well-founded, an infinite strictly decreasing sequence cannot exist, or equivalently, every strictly decreasing sequence of ordinals terminates (and cannot be infinite). But {\displaystyle P_{m}(n)} is calculated directly from {\displaystyle G_{m}(n)}. Hence the sequence {\displaystyle G_{m}} must terminate as well, meaning that it must reach {\displaystyle 0}.
