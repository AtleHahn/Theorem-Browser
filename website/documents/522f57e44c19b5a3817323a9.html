Statement

For k\ge1, let g(k) be the least s such that every positive integer is a sum of at most s positive integer kth powers. Put q=\lfloor(3/2)^k\rfloor. Then g(k)\ge2^k+q-2. In particular g(2)\ge4, g(3)\ge9 and g(4)\ge19.

Level
High school only
Level justification

The definition of g and the explicit floor-expression lower bound use only integer arithmetic.

Proof 1: Remainder obstruction below the next power (outline)

Let {\displaystyle q} and {\displaystyle r} be defined by the Euclidean division

{\displaystyle 3^{k}=2^{k}q+r,\quad 0\leq r<2^{k},}

or explicitly by {\displaystyle q=\lfloor (3/2)^{k}\rfloor } and {\displaystyle r=2^{k}\{(3/2)^{k}\}}, where {\displaystyle \lfloor x\rfloor } and {\displaystyle \{x\}} respectively denote the integral and fractional part of a real number {\displaystyle x}.

Since the number {\displaystyle 2^{k}q-1} is less than {\displaystyle 3^{k}}, as a sum of positive integer powers, it can only be expressed using {\displaystyle 1^{k}} and {\displaystyle 2^{k}}. Using modular arithmetic, one shows that the fewest number of terms is achieved by the formula

{\displaystyle 2^{k}q-1=\underbrace {1^{k}+\ldots +1^{k}} _{2^{k}-1{\text{ times}}}+\underbrace {2^{k}+\ldots +2^{k}} _{q-1{\text{ times}}},}

and it follows that

{\displaystyle g(k)\geq 2^{k}+q-2,}

Some simple computations show that 7 requires 4 squares, 23 requires 9 cubes, and 79 requires 19 fourth powers; these examples show that g(2)\geq4, g(3)\geq9, and g(4)\geq19.
