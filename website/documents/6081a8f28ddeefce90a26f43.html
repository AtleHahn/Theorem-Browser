Statement

Let A,B,C,D be the consecutive vertices of a quadrilateral in Euclidean space, and let

E=(A+B)/2,\quad F=(B+C)/2,\quad G=(C+D)/2,\quad H=(D+A)/2.

Then E,F,G,H form a planar parallelogram, possibly degenerate, even when A,B,C,D are not coplanar.

If ABCD is a simple planar quadrilateral, convex or concave, then

\operatorname{area}(EFGH)=\tfrac12\operatorname{area}(ABCD).

For a crossed planar quadrilateral the same equality holds for signed areas, with both polygons taken in the displayed vertex orders. In oriented planar coordinates define the signed area of a polygon P_1\cdots P_m by \tfrac12\sum_{j=1}^m\det(P_j,P_{j+1}), with P_{m+1}=P_1; thus the crossed case uses the difference of its two lobe areas.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

The geometric clauses are elementary, but the general signed-area convention and spatial affine formulation use basic undergraduate linear algebra.

Proof 1: Midpoint vectors and directed triangle areas (external)

A link to the proof can be found here (Proving the area relationship; Area of Crossed Quadrilaterals, computation following Figure6).

For the spatial clause, F-E=G-H=(C-A)/2 and H-E=G-F=(D-B)/2. Thus the four points lie in the affine plane spanned by these two vectors (or a line or point) and form a parallelogram. To express the source’s directed-area argument in the stated convention, bilinearity gives

\det(E,F)+\det(F,G)+\det(G,H)+\det(H,E) =\tfrac12\{\det(A,B)+\det(B,C)+\det(C,D)+\det(D,A)\}.

The diagonal terms cancel in pairs. Multiplying by 1/2 proves the signed-area claim regardless of crossings. For a simple planar polygon its ordinary area is the absolute value of its signed area, so this also proves the convex and concave clauses.
