Statement

Let (a_n) be a real or complex sequence that is eventually nonzero. Set R=\limsup_{n\to\infty}|a_{n+1}/a_n| and r=\liminf_{n\to\infty}|a_{n+1}/a_n|. If R<1, then \sum a_n converges absolutely. If r>1, it diverges; more generally, eventual ratios at least one imply divergence. Thus, when the ratio limit L exists, L<1 implies absolute convergence and L>1 divergence.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Understanding the statement involves Real or complex sequences; Absolute convergence; Consecutive-term ratios; Limits superior and inferior. These prerequisites fit undergraduate coursework or a brief extension of it. A bright US college student should be able to understand this statement after approximately 30 minutes of focused background research; this assesses understanding the claim, not its proof.

Proof 1: Geometric bounds and the term test

Suppose that {\displaystyle r=\liminf _{n\to \infty }\left|{\frac {a_{n+1}}{a_{n}}}\right|>1}. The sequence is nonzero from some index onward. Then there exists some {\displaystyle \ell \in (1;r)} such that there exists a natural number {\displaystyle n_{0}\geq 2} satisfying {\displaystyle a_{n_{0}}\neq 0} and {\displaystyle \left|{\frac {a_{n+1}}{a_{n}}}\right|>\ell } for all {\displaystyle n\geq n_{0}}, for otherwise there would be arbitrarily large n with |a_{n+1}/a_n|\le\ell, contradicting that r>\ell is the limit inferior. Then we notice that for {\displaystyle n\geq n_{0}+1}, {\displaystyle |a_{n}|>\ell |a_{n-1}|>\ell ^{2}|a_{n-2}|>...>\ell ^{n-n_{0}}\left|a_{n_{0}}\right|}. Notice that {\displaystyle \ell >1} so {\displaystyle \ell ^{n}\to \infty } as {\displaystyle n\to \infty } and {\displaystyle \left|a_{n_{0}}\right|>0}, this implies {\displaystyle (a_{n})} diverges so the series {\displaystyle \sum _{n=1}^{\infty }a_{n}} diverges by the n-th term test. Now suppose {\displaystyle R=\limsup _{n\to \infty }\left|{\frac {a_{n+1}}{a_{n}}}\right|<1}. Similar to the above case, we may find a natural number {\displaystyle n_{1}} and a {\displaystyle c\in (R;1)} such that {\displaystyle |a_{n}|\leq c^{n-n_{1}}\left|a_{n_{1}}\right|} for {\displaystyle n\geq n_{1}}. Then

{\displaystyle \sum _{n=1}^{\infty }|a_{n}|=\sum _{k=1}^{n_{1}-1}|a_{k}|+\sum _{n=n_{1}}^{\infty }|a_{n}|\leq \sum _{k=1}^{n_{1}-1}|a_{k}|+\sum _{n=n_{1}}^{\infty }c^{n-n_{1}}|a_{n_{1}}|=\sum _{k=1}^{n_{1}-1}|a_{k}|+\left|a_{n_{1}}\right|\sum _{n=0}^{\infty }c^{n}.}

The series {\displaystyle \sum _{n=0}^{\infty }c^{n}} is the geometric series with common ratio {\displaystyle c\in (0;1)}, hence {\displaystyle \sum _{n=0}^{\infty }c^{n}={\frac {1}{1-c}}} which is finite. The sum {\displaystyle \sum _{k=1}^{n_{1}-1}|a_{k}|} is a finite sum and hence it is bounded, this implies the series {\displaystyle \sum _{n=1}^{\infty }|a_{n}|} converges by the monotone convergence theorem and the series {\displaystyle \sum _{n=1}^{\infty }a_{n}} converges by the absolute convergence test. When the limit {\displaystyle \left|{\frac {a_{n+1}}{a_{n}}}\right|} exists and equals to {\displaystyle L} then {\displaystyle r=R=L}, this gives the original ratio test.

More generally, if |a_{n+1}/a_n|\ge1 eventually, the nonzero magnitudes are nondecreasing and do not approach zero; the series diverges by the term test.

Proof 2: Constant auxiliary sequence in Kummer’s test

Apply Kummer’s criterion to b_n=|a_n|>0 on a nonzero tail, with auxiliary sequence \zeta_n=1. Its parameter is b_n/b_{n+1}-1. If R<1, the ratios b_{n+1}/b_n are eventually bounded above by some c<1, so this parameter is bounded below by 1/c-1>0 and \sum b_n converges. For the divergence assertions, eventual nondecrease of the nonzero b_n prevents a_n\to0; this applies in particular if r>1. A ratio limit equals both its limsup and liminf.
