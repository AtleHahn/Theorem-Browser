Statement

Let B>0, let x:\mathbb R\to\mathbb C be continuous and integrable, and let X(f)=\int_{\mathbb R}x(t)e^{-2\pi i ft}\,dt vanish for |f|\ge B. Let 0<T\le1/(2B). The values x(nT) for all n\in\mathbb Z uniquely determine x.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Calculus and analysis supply continuous integrable functions and improper integrals. The Fourier transform is explicitly defined by a complex-exponential integral, and the conclusion is uniqueness from a specified sample sequence. No L2 quotient or distribution theory is involved.

Proof 1: Isolate the unaliased spectrum using Poisson summation — outline

Put f_s=1/T and x[n]=T x(nT). The samples of x(t) are sufficient to create a periodic summation of X(f). By Poisson summation in Fourier-series form,

X_{1/T}(f):=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}X(f-k/T) =\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}T x(nT)e^{-2\pi i fnT}.

This is a periodic function and its equivalent representation as a Fourier series, whose coefficients are x[n]. Here the series is interpreted in L^2 on one frequency period; uniqueness of Fourier coefficients also suffices.

Copies of X(f) are shifted by multiples of the sampling rate f_s=1/T and combined by addition. Since X(f)=0 for |f|\geq B and f_s\geq2B, these copies have no overlapping interiors. The k=0 term can be recovered by the product

X(f)=H(f)X_{1/T}(f),

where a sufficient choice, including B=f_s/2, is

H(f)= \begin{cases} 1,&|f|<f_s/2,\\ 0,&|f|>f_s/2. \end{cases}

Values at the two endpoints do not affect recovery almost everywhere. The sampling theorem is proved since Fourier inversion makes X(f) determine the continuous function x(t) uniquely.

Proof 2: Identify each sample as a Fourier coefficient

Put C=1/(2T)\geq B, and use ordinary frequency f throughout. Fourier inversion gives

x(t)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}X(f)e^{2\pi i ft}\,df =\int_{-C}^{C}X(f)e^{2\pi i ft}\,df,

because X(f) is zero outside that band. If we let t=n/(2C)=nT, where n is any integer, we obtain

x(nT)=\int_{-C}^{C}X(f)e^{2\pi i fnT}\,df.

On the left are values of x(t) at the sampling points. The integral on the right will be recognized as essentially the nth coefficient in a Fourier-series expansion of the function X(f), taking the interval [-C,C] as a fundamental period. More precisely, multiplying by T gives the actual Fourier coefficient:

T x(nT)=T\int_{-C}^{C}X(f)e^{2\pi i fnT}\,df,

for the expansion basis e^{-2\pi i fnT}.

This means that the values of the samples x(nT) determine the Fourier coefficients in the series expansion of X(f). Thus they determine X(f) almost everywhere, since it is zero outside [-C,C], and on that interval it is determined by Fourier-series uniqueness if its Fourier coefficients are determined. But X(f) determines the original continuous function x(t) completely by Fourier inversion. Therefore the original samples determine the function x(t) completely.
