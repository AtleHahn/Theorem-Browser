Statement

For z\in\mathbb C, k\geq1 integer, define \binom zk=z(z-1)\cdots(z-k+1)/k! and \binom z0=1. Then \binom{z-1}{k}+\binom{z-1}{k-1}=\binom zk.

Proof 1: Falling-factorial factorization

An alternative algebraic proof using the alternative definition of binomial coefficients: {\displaystyle {\tbinom {z}{k}}={\frac {z(z-1)\cdots (z-k+1)}{k!}}}. Indeed

{\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}{z-1 \choose k}+{z-1 \choose k-1}&={\frac {(z-1)\cdots ((z-1)-k+1)}{k!}}+{\frac {(z-1)\cdots ((z-1)-(k-1)+1)}{(k-1)!}}\\&={\frac {(z-1)\cdots (z-k)}{k!}}+{\frac {(z-1)\cdots (z-k+1)}{(k-1)!}}\\&={\frac {(z-1)\cdots (z-k+1)}{(k-1)!}}\left[{\frac {z-k}{k}}+1\right]\\&={\frac {(z-1)\cdots (z-k+1)}{(k-1)!}}\cdot {\frac {z}{k}}\\&={\frac {z(z-1)\cdots (z-k+1)}{k!}}\\&={\binom {z}{k}}.\end{aligned}}}

An empty product is 1. Since the falling-factorial expression is the extended definition when z is a complex number, this proof shows that Pascal's rule holds for every complex z. No factor involving z is divided out.

Level justification
