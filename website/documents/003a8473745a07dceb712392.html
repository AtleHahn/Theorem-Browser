Statement

Let g:U\to V and f:V\to\mathbb R^k, where U\subset\mathbb R^n and V\subset\mathbb R^m are open, g is differentiable at a\in U, and f is differentiable at g(a). For each 1\le i\le n, \partial_i(f\circ g)(a)=\sum_{\ell=1}^m (\partial_\ell f)(g(a))\,\partial_i g_\ell(a), an identity in \mathbb R^k. For k=1 this is \partial_i(f\circ g)(a)=\nabla f(g(a))\cdot\partial_i g(a).

Level

Level justification

Understanding the statement involves total differentiability; partial derivatives; finite-dimensional vector maps; gradients. These prerequisites fit undergraduate coursework or a brief extension of it. A bright US college student should be able to understand this statement after approximately 30 minutes of focused background research; this assesses understanding the claim, not its proof.

Proof 1: Multiply the Jacobian identity by a basis vector

Use the total-derivative chain rule J_{f\circ g}(a)=J_f(g(a))J_g(a). All derivatives below are evaluated at \mathbf x=a or \mathbf u=g(a), as appropriate.

Another way of writing the chain rule is used when f and g are expressed in terms of their components as y = f(u) = (f1(u), …, fk(u)) and u = g(x) = (g1(x), …, gm(x)). In this case, the total-derivative chain rule is usually written as:

{\displaystyle {\frac {\partial (y_{1},\ldots ,y_{k})}{\partial (x_{1},\ldots ,x_{n})}}={\frac {\partial (y_{1},\ldots ,y_{k})}{\partial (u_{1},\ldots ,u_{m})}}{\frac {\partial (u_{1},\ldots ,u_{m})}{\partial (x_{1},\ldots ,x_{n})}}.}

The chain rule for total derivatives implies a chain rule for partial derivatives. Recall that when the total derivative exists, the partial derivative in the i-th coordinate direction is found by multiplying the Jacobian matrix by the i-th basis vector. By doing this to the formula above, we find:

{\displaystyle {\frac {\partial (y_{1},\ldots ,y_{k})}{\partial x_{i}}}={\frac {\partial (y_{1},\ldots ,y_{k})}{\partial (u_{1},\ldots ,u_{m})}}{\frac {\partial (u_{1},\ldots ,u_{m})}{\partial x_{i}}}.}

Since the entries of the Jacobian matrix are partial derivatives, we may simplify the above formula to get:

{\displaystyle {\frac {\partial (y_{1},\ldots ,y_{k})}{\partial x_{i}}}=\sum _{\ell =1}^{m}{\frac {\partial (y_{1},\ldots ,y_{k})}{\partial u_{\ell }}}{\frac {\partial u_{\ell }}{\partial x_{i}}}.}

More conceptually, this rule expresses the fact that a change in the xi direction may change all of g1 through gm, and any of these changes may affect f.

In the special case where k = 1, so that f is a real-valued function, then this formula simplifies even further:

{\displaystyle {\frac {\partial y}{\partial x_{i}}}=\sum _{\ell =1}^{m}{\frac {\partial y}{\partial u_{\ell }}}{\frac {\partial u_{\ell }}{\partial x_{i}}}.}

This can be rewritten as a dot product. Recalling that u = (g1, …, gm), the partial derivative ∂u / ∂xi is also a vector, and the chain rule says that:

{\displaystyle {\frac {\partial y}{\partial x_{i}}}=\nabla y\cdot {\frac {\partial \mathbf {u} }{\partial x_{i}}}.}
