Statement

Let \vdash denote theoremhood in a classical modal deductive system, with propositional tautologies and modus ponens. Assume necessitation (\vdash A implies \vdash\Box A), distribution \vdash\Box(A\to B)\to(\Box A\to\Box B), and \vdash\Box A\to\Box\Box A. Suppose for each sentence P there is a sentence \Psi with \vdash\Psi\leftrightarrow(\Box\Psi\to P). Then \vdash\Box P\to P implies \vdash P.

Level
High school only
Level justification

Every modal inference rule and the fixed-point hypothesis are explicitly supplied as symbolic formulas. The conclusion concerns a finite deduction using those rules, not modal semantics or arithmetic coding.

Proof 1: Box the fixed point and use internal necessitation

Much of the proof does not make use of the assumption {\displaystyle \Box P\to P}, so for ease of understanding, the proof below is subdivided to leave the parts depending on {\displaystyle \Box P\to P} until the end.

Let {\displaystyle P} be any modal sentence.

  1. Use the fixed-point hypothesis to choose a sentence {\displaystyle \Psi } such that {\displaystyle \vdash \Psi \leftrightarrow (\Box \Psi \rightarrow P)}.

  2. {\displaystyle \vdash \Psi \rightarrow (\Box \Psi \rightarrow P)}, from 1.

  3. {\displaystyle \vdash \Box (\Psi \rightarrow (\Box \Psi \rightarrow P))}, from 2 by the necessitation rule.

  4. {\displaystyle \vdash \Box \Psi \rightarrow \Box (\Box \Psi \rightarrow P)}, from 3 and the box distributivity rule.

  5. {\displaystyle \vdash \Box (\Box \Psi \rightarrow P)\rightarrow (\Box \Box \Psi \rightarrow \Box P)}, box distributivity rule " {\displaystyle \vdash \Box (A\rightarrow B)\rightarrow (\Box A\rightarrow \Box B)} " with {\displaystyle A=\Box \Psi } and {\displaystyle B=P}.

  6. {\displaystyle \vdash \Box \Psi \rightarrow (\Box \Box \Psi \rightarrow \Box P)}, from 4 and 5.

  7. {\displaystyle \vdash \Box \Psi \rightarrow \Box \Box \Psi }, internal necessitation rule.

  8. {\displaystyle \vdash \Box \Psi \rightarrow \Box P}, from 6 and 7.

    Now comes the part of the proof where the hypothesis is used.

  9. Assume that {\displaystyle \vdash \Box P\rightarrow P}. Roughly speaking, it is a theorem that if {\displaystyle P} is provable, then it is, in fact true. This is a claim of soundness.

  10. {\displaystyle \vdash \Box \Psi \rightarrow P}, from 8 and 9.

  11. {\displaystyle \vdash (\Box \Psi \rightarrow P)\rightarrow \Psi }, from 1.

  12. {\displaystyle \vdash \Psi }, from 10 and 11.

  13. {\displaystyle \vdash \Box \Psi }, from 12 by the necessitation rule.

  14. {\displaystyle \vdash P}, from 13 and 10.
