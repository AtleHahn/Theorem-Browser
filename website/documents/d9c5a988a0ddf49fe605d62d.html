Statement

Let L/K be a finite cyclic Galois extension of degree n, and let \sigma generate its Galois group. If a\in L satisfies N(a)=\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}\sigma^j(a)=1, then a=b/\sigma(b) for some b\in L^\times.

Level justification

Arbitrary fields and Galois theory exceed undergraduate calculus and linear algebra.

Proof 1: Scalar extension and a cyclic weighted-shift eigenvector

It suffices to prove the equivalent convention a=x/\tau^{-1}(x) for an arbitrary generator \tau; then take \tau=\sigma^{-1}.

Let {\displaystyle L/K} be cyclic of degree {\displaystyle n,} and {\displaystyle \tau } generate {\displaystyle \operatorname {Gal} (L/K)}. Pick any {\displaystyle a\in L} of norm

{\displaystyle N(a):=a\tau (a)\tau ^{2}(a)\cdots \tau ^{n-1}(a)=1.}

By clearing denominators, solving {\displaystyle a=x/\tau ^{-1}(x)\in L} is the same as showing that {\displaystyle a\tau ^{-1}(\cdot ):L\to L} has {\displaystyle 1} as an eigenvalue. We extend this to a map of {\displaystyle L}-vector spaces via

{\displaystyle {\begin{cases}1_{L}\otimes a\tau ^{-1}(\cdot ):L\otimes _{K}L\to L\otimes _{K}L\\\ell \otimes \ell '\mapsto \ell \otimes a\tau ^{-1}(\ell ').\end{cases}}}

The primitive element theorem gives {\displaystyle L=K(\alpha )} for some {\displaystyle \alpha }. Since {\displaystyle \alpha } has minimal polynomial

{\displaystyle f(t)=(t-\alpha )(t-\tau (\alpha ))\cdots \left(t-\tau ^{n-1}(\alpha )\right)\in K[t],}

we can identify

{\displaystyle L\otimes _{K}L{\stackrel {\sim }{\to }}L\otimes _{K}K[t]/f(t){\stackrel {\sim }{\to }}L[t]/f(t){\stackrel {\sim }{\to }}L^{n}}

via

{\displaystyle \ell \otimes p(\alpha )\mapsto \ell \left(p(\alpha ),p(\tau \alpha ),\ldots ,p(\tau ^{n-1}\alpha )\right).}

Here we wrote the second factor as a {\displaystyle K}-polynomial in {\displaystyle \alpha }.

Under this identification, our map becomes

{\displaystyle {\begin{cases}a\tau ^{-1}(\cdot ):L^{n}\to L^{n}\\\ell \left(p(\alpha ),\ldots ,p(\tau ^{n-1}\alpha )\right)\mapsto \ell \left(ap(\tau ^{n-1}\alpha ),\tau ap(\alpha ),\ldots ,\tau ^{n-1}ap(\tau ^{n-2}\alpha )\right).\end{cases}}}

That is to say under this map

{\displaystyle (\ell _{1},\ldots ,\ell _{n})\mapsto (a\ell _{n},\tau a\ell _{1},\ldots ,\tau ^{n-1}a\ell _{n-1}).}

{\displaystyle (1,\tau a,\tau a\tau ^{2}a,\ldots ,\tau a\cdots \tau ^{n-1}a)} is an eigenvector with eigenvalue {\displaystyle 1} iff {\displaystyle a} has norm {\displaystyle 1}.

Singularity of a finite matrix is unchanged by extension of scalars. Thus the original K-linear map x\mapsto a\tau^{-1}(x) also has a nonzero fixed vector x\in L, giving a=x/\tau^{-1}(x).
