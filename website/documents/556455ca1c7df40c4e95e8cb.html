Statement

Let I be [a,b], [a,b) or [a,\infty), with a<b in the finite-endpoint cases. Let u,\beta be continuous real functions with \beta\ge0, and let \alpha be a real nondecreasing function. If u(t)\le\alpha(t)+\int_a^t\beta(s)u(s)\,ds, then u(t)\le\alpha(t)\exp(\int_a^t\beta(s)\,ds). Neither u nor \alpha is required to be nonnegative.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Understanding the statement involves Real differentiation; Definite integration; Exponential functions. These prerequisites fit undergraduate coursework or a brief extension of it. A bright US college student should be able to understand this statement after approximately 30 minutes of focused background research; this assesses understanding the claim, not its proof.

Proof 1: Bound the inhomogeneity and evaluate the exponential integral

Since \alpha is non-decreasing, it is integrable on every closed and bounded subinterval of I.

(a) Define

{\displaystyle v(s)=\exp {\biggl (}{-}\int _{a}^{s}\beta (r)\,\mathrm {d} r{\biggr )}\int _{a}^{s}\beta (r)u(r)\,\mathrm {d} r,\qquad s\in I.}

Using the product rule, the chain rule, the derivative of the exponential function and the fundamental theorem of calculus, we obtain for the derivative

{\displaystyle v'(s)={\biggl (}\underbrace {u(s)-\int _{a}^{s}\beta (r)u(r)\,\mathrm {d} r} _{\leq \,\alpha (s)}{\biggr )}\beta (s)\exp {\biggl (}{-}\int _{a}^{s}\beta (r)\mathrm {d} r{\biggr )},\qquad s\in I^\circ,}

where we used the assumed integral inequality for the upper estimate. Since β and the exponential are non-negative, this gives an upper estimate for the derivative of {\displaystyle v(s)}. Since {\displaystyle v(a)=0}, integration of this inequality from a to t gives

{\displaystyle v(t)\leq \int _{a}^{t}\alpha (s)\beta (s)\exp {\biggl (}{-}\int _{a}^{s}\beta (r)\,\mathrm {d} r{\biggr )}\mathrm {d} s.}

Using the definition of {\displaystyle v(t)} from the first step, and then this inequality and the property {\displaystyle e^{a}e^{b}=e^{a+b}}, we obtain

{\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}\int _{a}^{t}\beta (s)u(s)\,\mathrm {d} s&=\exp {\biggl (}\int _{a}^{t}\beta (r)\,\mathrm {d} r{\biggr )}v(t)\\&\leq \int _{a}^{t}\alpha (s)\beta (s)\exp {\biggl (}\underbrace {\int _{a}^{t}\beta (r)\,\mathrm {d} r-\int _{a}^{s}\beta (r)\,\mathrm {d} r} _{=\,\int _{s}^{t}\beta (r)\,\mathrm {d} r}{\biggr )}\mathrm {d} s.\end{aligned}}}

Substituting this result into the assumed integral inequality gives Grönwall's inequality.

(b) If the function α is non-decreasing, then part (a), the fact α(s) ≤ α(t), and the fundamental theorem of calculus imply that

{\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}u(t)&\leq \alpha (t)+\alpha (t)\int _{a}^{t}\beta (s)\exp \left(\int _{s}^{t}\beta (r)dr\right)ds\\&= \alpha (t)+\alpha (t){\biggl (}{-}\exp {\biggl (}\int _{s}^{t}\beta (r)\,\mathrm {d} r{\biggr )}{\biggr |}_{s=a}^{s=t}{\biggr )}\\&=\alpha (t)\exp {\biggl (}\int _{a}^{t}\beta (r)\,\mathrm {d} r{\biggr )},\qquad t\in I.\end{aligned}}}

Level justification

Differentiate the integrating factor, integrate the inequality and bound the monotone inhomogeneity by its endpoint value. Monotone functions are Riemann integrable on compact intervals, so ordinary undergraduate real analysis and calculus suffice.
