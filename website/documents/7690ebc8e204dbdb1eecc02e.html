Statement

For an integer n<-2, the equation a^n+b^n=c^n has no solution in nonzero integers a,b,c.

Level
High school only
Level justification

The assertion concerns nonzero integers, negative integer powers and an equation.

Proof 1: Clear the reciprocal powers

If such a solution existed, put m=|n|>2 and multiply by a^mb^mc^m to obtain

(bc)^m+(ac)^m=(ab)^m.

All three coordinates are nonzero integers. Fermat’s Last Theorem in its equivalent nonzero-integer formulation excludes this.

Level
High school only
Level justification

The supplied proof only clears negative integer powers and invokes the nonzero-integer form of Fermat’s Last Theorem. Its positive-integer statement extends to nonzero integers: even powers ignore signs, and for odd powers negative terms can be moved to the other side; a zero term is excluded. No modularity or elliptic-curve theory is needed to understand this application. This is high-school algebra conditional on the explicitly cited theorem, not a self-contained proof of that theorem.

Proof 1 — Clear the reciprocal powers

Statement used:

For every integer m>2, there are no nonzero integers x,y,z with x^m+y^m=z^m.

Application in proof:

Set m=|n| and (x,y,z)=(bc,ac,ab). Multiplying the assumed reciprocal-power identity by a^m b^m c^m gives the forbidden equation; all three products are nonzero integers.

Application level:

High school only

Level justification:

Integer powers, signs and multiplication suffice. The advanced proof of Fermat’s Last Theorem is not required. The database statement uses positive integers; the even/odd sign reduction gives exactly the nonzero-integer form invoked here.
