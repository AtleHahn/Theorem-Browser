Statement

For x>1, let f(x)=x+\sin x\cos x and g(x)=f(x)e^{\sin x}. Both tend to +\infty. The ratio f(x)/g(x) has no limit as x\to\infty, but f^{\prime}(x)/g^{\prime}(x), on its domain of definition, tends to 0. The denominator derivative vanishes at arbitrarily large points.

Proof 1: Cancel a cosine factor on the derivative quotient’s domain

Let {\displaystyle f(x)=x+\sin x\cos x} and {\displaystyle g(x)=f(x)e^{\sin x}.} Then there is no limit for {\displaystyle f(x)/g(x)} as {\displaystyle x\to \infty .} However,

{\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}{\frac {f'(x)}{g'(x)}}&={\frac {2\cos ^{2}x}{(2\cos ^{2}x)e^{\sin x}+(x+\sin x\cos x)e^{\sin x}\cos x}}\\&={\frac {2\cos x}{2\cos x+x+\sin x\cos x}}e^{-\sin x},\end{aligned}}}

which tends to 0 as {\displaystyle x\to \infty }, while the original expression before cancellation of {\displaystyle \cos x} is undefined at infinitely many points.

Indeed, f/g=e^{-\sin x} has no limit because of the recurring sine oscillations. The numerator factors of the simplified derivative quotient are bounded while its denominator grows with x. Bounded trigonometric terms and e^{-1}\le e^{\sin x}\le e also give f,g\to+\infty. The derivative-ratio limit is taken only where the original quotient is defined.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Differentiate, cancel only on the original quotient domain and compare bounded trigonometric terms with x. Undergraduate calculus supplies all rules and the oscillating subsequences needed to exclude the original limit.
