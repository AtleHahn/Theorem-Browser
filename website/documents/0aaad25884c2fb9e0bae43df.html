Proof 1: A convergent series with an oscillating comparison ratio — comparison argument

Use the one-sided comparison test: for nonnegative a_n and positive b_n, if \limsup_{n\to\infty}a_n/b_n=c with 0\le c<\infty and \sum_n b_n converges, necessarily \sum_n a_n converges.

Let a_n=(1-(-1)^n)/n^2 and b_n=1/n^2 for all positive integers n. Now

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{a_n}{b_n}=\lim_{n\to\infty}(1-(-1)^n)

does not exist, so we cannot apply the standard limit comparison test. However,

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{a_n}{b_n}=\limsup_{n\to\infty}(1-(-1)^n)=2\in[0,\infty),

and since \sum_{n=1}^\infty 1/n^2 converges, the one-sided comparison test implies that \sum_{n=1}^\infty(1-(-1)^n)/n^2 converges.
