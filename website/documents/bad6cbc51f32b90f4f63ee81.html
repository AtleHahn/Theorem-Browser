Statement

Assume the axiom of choice, in its equivalent Zorn or Teichmüller–Tukey maximality forms. Every vector space V over any field has a basis: a linearly independent subset whose finite linear combinations span V.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Introductory ring/group theory and linear algebra supply polynomial coefficients, ideals, determinants, bases and matrix products. Elementary transvections are explicitly given elementary row-operation matrices, and division ring means every nonzero element has an inverse. These direct definitions suffice without specialist theory.

Proof 1: Chain unions of linearly independent sets

The named maximality principle used below is an equivalent form of the assumed axiom of choice.

Stated dependency

Zorn’s lemma: a partially ordered set in which every chain (including the empty chain) has an upper bound has a maximal element. A chain is a totally ordered subset; an element m is maximal if m\leq y implies m=y.

Argument

If V = {0}, then the empty set is a basis for V. Now, suppose that V ≠ {0}. Let P be the set consisting of all linearly independent subsets of V. Since V is not the zero vector space, there exists a nonzero element v of V, so P contains the linearly independent subset {v}. Furthermore, P is partially ordered by set inclusion. Finding a maximal linearly independent subset of V is the same as finding a maximal element in P.

To apply Zorn's lemma, take a chain T in P (that is, T is a subset of P that is totally ordered). If T is the empty set, then {v} is an upper bound for T in P. Suppose then that T is non-empty. We need to show that T has an upper bound, that is, there exists a linearly independent subset B of V containing all the members of T.

Take B to be the union of all the sets in T. We wish to show that B is an upper bound for T in P. To do this, it suffices to show that B is a linearly independent subset of V.

Suppose otherwise, that B is not linearly independent. Then there exist distinct vectors v1, v2, ..., vk ∈ B and scalars a1, a2, ..., ak, not all zero, such that

{\displaystyle a_{1}\mathbf {v} _{1}+a_{2}\mathbf {v} _{2}+\cdots +a_{k}\mathbf {v} _{k}=\mathbf {0} .}

Since B is the union of all the sets in T, there are some sets S1, S2, ..., Sk ∈ T such that vi ∈ Si for every i = 1, 2, ..., k. As T is totally ordered, one of the sets S1, S2, ..., Sk must contain the others, so there is some set Si that contains all of v1, v2, ..., vk. This tells us there is a linearly dependent set of vectors in Si, contradicting that Si is linearly independent (because it is a member of P).

The hypothesis of Zorn's lemma has been checked, and thus there is a maximal element in P, in other words a maximal linearly independent subset B of V.

Finally, we show that B is indeed a basis of V. It suffices to show that B is a spanning set of V. Suppose for the sake of contradiction that B is not spanning. Then there exists some v ∈ V not covered by the span of B. This says that B ∪ {v} is a linearly independent subset of V that is larger than B, contradicting the maximality of B. Therefore, B is a spanning set of V, and thus, a basis of V.

Proof 2: Maximize the finite-character family of independent sets — outline

The named maximality principle used below is an equivalent form of the assumed axiom of choice.

A family of subsets has finite character when a set belongs to it exactly when all of its finite subsets belong. The Teichmüller–Tukey lemma says that a nonempty such family has an inclusion-maximal member. Let V be a vector space. Consider the collection \mathcal F of linearly independent sets of vectors. This is a collection of finite character, and it is nonempty because it contains the empty set. Thus, a maximal set exists, which must then span V and be a basis for V.
