Statement

A partial order is a reflexive, antisymmetric, transitive relation \le. Distinct elements are comparable when one is below the other. A chain has every pair comparable; an antichain has no distinct comparable pair. A partition consists of disjoint subsets whose union is the whole set. Let a finite partial order be partitioned into k nonempty chains C_1,\ldots,C_k and have an antichain of size k. In each C_i, let x_i be its greatest element belonging to some antichain of size k. Then \{x_1,\ldots,x_k\} is an antichain.

Level
High school only
Level justification

Order, chains, antichains and partitions are defined locally; greatest choices are over finite sets.

Proof 1: Comparing each representative with a witness on the other chain

Let P denote the given order and choose an antichain A_0 of size k.

Clearly, {\displaystyle A_{0}\cap C_{i}\neq \emptyset } for {\displaystyle i=1,2,\dots ,k}. For {\displaystyle i=1,2,\dots ,k}, let {\displaystyle x_{i}} be the maximal element in {\displaystyle C_{i}} that belongs to an antichain of size {\displaystyle k} in P, and set {\displaystyle A:=\{x_{1},x_{2},\dots ,x_{k}\}}. We claim that {\displaystyle A} is an antichain. Let {\displaystyle A_{i}} be an antichain of size {\displaystyle k} that contains {\displaystyle x_{i}}. Fix arbitrary distinct indices {\displaystyle i} and {\displaystyle j}. Then {\displaystyle A_{i}\cap C_{j}\neq \emptyset }. Let {\displaystyle y\in A_{i}\cap C_{j}}. Then {\displaystyle y\leq x_{j}}, by the definition of {\displaystyle x_{j}}. This implies that {\displaystyle x_{i}\not \geq x_{j}}, since {\displaystyle x_{i}\not \geq y}. By interchanging the roles of {\displaystyle i} and {\displaystyle j} in this argument we also have {\displaystyle x_{j}\not \geq x_{i}}. This verifies that {\displaystyle A} is an antichain.
