Statement

Let f:\mathbb N_0\to\mathbb C, define \Delta f(k)=f(k+1)-f(k), \Delta^0f=f, and \Delta^{n+1}f=\Delta(\Delta^nf). For each nonnegative integer k, f(k)=\sum_{n=0}^{k}\binom{k}{n}\Delta^nf(0).

Level
High school only
Level justification

The statement gives an explicit finite identity involving compositions, products or successive differences of numerical functions. Its definitions require only elementary algebra and finite sums. This rating concerns understanding the statement, independently of the difficulty of its proof.

Proof 1: The integer values of the Newton construction — outline

Suppose f possesses all finite forward differences \Delta^n f(0). Consider then the Newton expression

g(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\binom zn\,\Delta^nf(0),

where \binom z0=1 and \binom zn=z(z-1)\cdots(z-n+1)/n! for n\geq1. At a nonnegative integer k, all terms with n>k vanish, so this expression is a finite sum. By construction, one then has that f(k)=g(k) for all nonnegative integers k.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

At an integer argument the formally infinite expression truncates, and the identity is the finite binomial reconstruction of forward differences. Numerical forward differences are a small undergraduate discrete-algebra extension, with no analytic convergence needed.
