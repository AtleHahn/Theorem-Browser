Statement

If a finite group G has order p^a q^b for primes p,q and nonnegative integers a,b, then G is solvable. Here solvability means that the derived series, formed by repeatedly taking the subgroup generated by commutators, eventually reaches the identity subgroup.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Introductory abstract algebra supplies finite groups, actions, normal subgroups, quotient groups and Sylow subgroups. The displayed definitions of orbits, centralizers, solvability or series equivalence are direct uses or small extensions of these familiar structures; no representation theory is needed for comprehension.

Proof 1: Minimal counterexample and the class-sum character argument

The proof is by contradiction. Let {\displaystyle p^{a}q^{b}} be the smallest product of two prime powers, such that there is a non-solvable group {\displaystyle G} whose order is equal to this number.

{\displaystyle G} is a simple group with trivial center and {\displaystyle a} is not zero.

If {\displaystyle G} had a nontrivial proper normal subgroup {\displaystyle H}, then (because of the minimality of {\displaystyle G}), {\displaystyle H} and {\displaystyle G/H} would be solvable, so {\displaystyle G} as well, which would contradict our assumption. So {\displaystyle G} is simple.

If either exponent were zero, or the primes were equal, {\displaystyle G} would be a finite prime-power group, hence nilpotent, and therefore solvable.

Similarly, {\displaystyle G} cannot be abelian, otherwise it would be solvable. As {\displaystyle G} is simple, its center must therefore be trivial.

A prime-power conjugacy class

There is an element {\displaystyle g} of {\displaystyle G} which has {\displaystyle q^{d}} conjugates, for some {\displaystyle d>0}.

By the first statement of Sylow's theorem, {\displaystyle G} has a subgroup {\displaystyle S} of order {\displaystyle p^{a}}. Because {\displaystyle S} is a nontrivial {\displaystyle p}-group, its center {\displaystyle Z(S)} is nontrivial. Write G_g=\{h\in G:hg=gh\} for the centralizer. Fix a nontrivial element {\displaystyle g\in Z(S)}. The number of conjugates of {\displaystyle g} is equal to the index of its centralizer {\displaystyle G_{g}}, which divides the index {\displaystyle q^{b}} of {\displaystyle S} (because {\displaystyle S} is a subgroup of {\displaystyle G_{g}}). Hence this number is of the form {\displaystyle q^{d}}. Moreover, the integer {\displaystyle d} is strictly positive, since {\displaystyle g} is nontrivial and therefore not central in {\displaystyle G}.

Choosing a character

There exists a nontrivial irreducible representation {\displaystyle \rho } with character {\displaystyle \chi }, such that its dimension {\displaystyle n} is not divisible by {\displaystyle q} and the complex number {\displaystyle \chi (g)} is not zero.

Let {\displaystyle (\chi _{i})_{1\leq i\leq h}} be the family of irreducible characters of {\displaystyle G} over {\displaystyle \mathbb {C} } (here {\displaystyle \chi _{1}} denotes the trivial character). Because {\displaystyle g} is not in the same conjugacy class as 1, the orthogonality relation for the columns of the group's character table gives:

{\displaystyle 0=\sum _{i=1}^{h}\chi _{i}(1)\chi _{i}(g)=1+\sum _{i=2}^{h}\chi _{i}(1)\chi _{i}(g).}

Now the {\displaystyle \chi _{i}(g)} are algebraic integers, because they are sums of roots of unity. If all the nontrivial irreducible characters which don't vanish at {\displaystyle g} take a value divisible by {\displaystyle q} at 1, we deduce that

{\displaystyle -{\frac {1}{q}}=\sum _{i\geq 2,~\chi _{i}(g)\neq 0}{\frac {\chi _{i}(1)}{q}}\chi _{i}(g)}

is an algebraic integer (since it is a sum of integer multiples of algebraic integers), which is absurd. This proves the statement.

Integrality of the class-sum scalar

The complex number {\displaystyle q^{d}\chi (g)/n} is an algebraic integer.

Identify integer-valued class functions with elements of the center of the integral group ring by their coefficients. Multiplication is group-ring convolution. The set of integer-valued class functions on {\displaystyle G}, {\displaystyle Z(\mathbb {Z} [G])}, is a commutative ring, finitely generated as a module over {\displaystyle \mathbb {Z} }. All of its elements are thus integral over {\displaystyle \mathbb {Z} }, in particular the mapping {\displaystyle u} which takes the value 1 on the conjugacy class of {\displaystyle g} and 0 elsewhere.

The mapping {\displaystyle A\colon Z(\mathbb {Z} [G])\rightarrow \operatorname {End} (\mathbb {C} ^{n})} which sends a class function {\displaystyle f} to

{\displaystyle \sum _{s\in G}f(s)\rho (s)}

is a ring homomorphism. Because {\displaystyle \rho (s)^{-1}A(u)\rho (s)=A(u)} for all {\displaystyle s}, Schur's lemma implies that {\displaystyle A(u)} is a homothety {\displaystyle \lambda I_{n}}. Its trace {\displaystyle n\lambda } is equal to

{\displaystyle \sum _{s\in G}u(s)\chi (s)=q^{d}\chi (g).}

Because the homothety {\displaystyle \lambda I_{n}} is the homomorphic image of an integral element, this proves that the complex number {\displaystyle \lambda =q^{d}\chi (g)/n} is an algebraic integer.

Integrality of the normalized character

The complex number {\displaystyle \chi (g)/n} is an algebraic integer.

Since {\displaystyle q} is relatively prime to {\displaystyle n}, by Bézout's identity there are two integers {\displaystyle x} and {\displaystyle y} such that:

{\displaystyle xq^{d}+yn=1\quad {\text{therefore}}\quad {\frac {\chi (g)}{n}}=x{\frac {q^{d}\chi (g)}{n}}+y\chi (g).}

Because a linear combination with integer coefficients of algebraic integers is again an algebraic integer, this proves the statement.

Scalar image and contradiction

The image of {\displaystyle g}, under the representation {\displaystyle \rho }, is a homothety.

Let {\displaystyle \zeta } be the complex number {\displaystyle \chi (g)/n}. It is a nonzero algebraic integer, so its norm {\displaystyle N(\zeta )} (i.e. the product of its conjugates, that is the roots of its minimal polynomial over {\displaystyle \mathbb {Q} }) is a nonzero integer. Now {\displaystyle \zeta } is the average of roots of unity (the eigenvalues of {\displaystyle \rho (g)}), hence so are its conjugates, so they all have an absolute value less than or equal to 1. Because the absolute value of their product {\displaystyle N(\zeta )} is greater than or equal to 1, their absolute value must all be 1, in particular {\displaystyle \zeta }, which means that the eigenvalues of {\displaystyle \rho (g)} are all equal, and, since a finite-order complex matrix is diagonalizable, {\displaystyle \rho (g)} is a homothety.

Conclusion

Let {\displaystyle N} be the kernel of {\displaystyle \rho }. The homothety {\displaystyle \rho (g)} is central in {\displaystyle \operatorname {Im} (\rho )} (which is canonically isomorphic to {\displaystyle G/N}), whereas {\displaystyle g} is not central in {\displaystyle G}. Consequently, the normal subgroup {\displaystyle N} of the simple group {\displaystyle G} is nontrivial, hence it is equal to {\displaystyle G}, which contradicts the fact that {\displaystyle \rho } is a nontrivial representation.

This contradiction proves the theorem.

Level justification

These connected prerequisites cannot be acquired as one small extension of introductory algebra.
