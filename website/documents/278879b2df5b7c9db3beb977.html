Statement

For a,b,c,d\in\mathbb Q with a\ne0, the polynomial ax^3+bx^2+cx+d is a product of two positive-degree polynomials over \mathbb Q if and only if it has a rational root.

Proof 1: Use the linear factor and polynomial division

If the coefficients of a cubic equation are rational numbers, one can obtain an equivalent equation with integer coefficients, by multiplying all coefficients by a common multiple of their denominators. Such an equation

{\displaystyle ax^{3}+bx^{2}+cx+d=0,}

with integer coefficients, is said to be reducible if the polynomial on the left-hand side is the product of polynomials of lower degrees. By Gauss's lemma, if the equation is reducible, one can suppose that the factors have integer coefficients.

In fact, if the equation is reducible, one of the factors must have degree one, and thus have the form

{\displaystyle qx-p,}

with q and p being coprime integers.

Thus one root is x_1=p/q. Conversely, if p/q is a rational root, with p,q coprime and q\ne0, polynomial long division by qx-p gives the other factor

{\displaystyle {\frac {a}{q}}\,x^{2}+{\frac {bq+ap}{q^{2}}}\,x+{\frac {cq^{2}+bpq+ap^{2}}{q^{3}}}.}

This is a quadratic polynomial over the rationals, so the cubic is reducible.

Level
High school only
Level justification

Clear denominators, use the stated integer-factor version of Gauss’s lemma, and divide by the rational linear factor. Polynomial degrees, rational arithmetic and long division suffice without abstract field theory.
