Statement

Let R be a commutative ring with identity, n\ge1 an integer and A\in M_n(R). Let R[A]=\{\sum_{j=0}^m r_jA^j:m\ge0,\ r_j\in R\} and write \operatorname{adj}(tI_n-A)=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}B_it^i. Then every B_i lies in the commutative ring R[A].

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Undergraduate linear algebra supplies determinants, cofactors, matrices and permutation signs; introductory algebra supplies commutative rings and polynomial coefficients. The displayed formula uses these directly, so moving the entries from a field to a commutative ring is a small extension, with no additional structural theory required.

Proof 1: Characteristic-adjugate coefficients are polynomials in the matrix

Let p(t)=\det(tI_n-A) and B=\operatorname{adj}(tI_n-A). The right-hand fundamental relation for the adjugate gives

p(t)I_n=(tI_n-A)B.

Thus B is the quotient and zero the remainder of division of p(t)I_n on the left by the monic polynomial I_nt-A, in the ring M_n(R)[t] of polynomials with matrix coefficients.

Indeed, even over a non-commutative ring, Euclidean division by a monic polynomial P is defined, and always produces a unique quotient and remainder with the same degree condition as in the commutative case, provided it is specified at which side one wishes P to be a factor (here that is to the left).

To see that quotient and remainder are unique (which is the important part of the statement here), it suffices to write {\displaystyle PQ+r=PQ'+r'} as {\displaystyle P(Q-Q')=r'-r} and observe that since P is monic, P(Q−Q′) cannot have a degree less than that of P, unless Q = Q′.

The dividend p(t)In and divisor Int − A in the adjugate identity both lie in the subring (R[A])[t], where R[A] is the subring of the matrix ring M(n, R) generated by A: the R-linear span of all powers of A. Therefore, the Euclidean division can in fact be performed within that commutative polynomial ring, and of course it then gives the same quotient B and remainder 0 as in M_n(R)[t]; in particular this shows that B in fact lies in (R[A])[t].

Level justification

The proof uses the same elementary monic polynomial-division algorithm in two coefficient rings: all matrices and the commutative subring generated by A. Because the divisor is monic, no division of coefficients is needed, and the supplied degree argument proves uniqueness even with noncommuting coefficients. The ring R[A] is defined explicitly. These are a small extension of undergraduate polynomial and matrix algebra.
