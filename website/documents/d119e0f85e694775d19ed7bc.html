Statement

Let A be a set with at least three alternatives and let N be a positive integer. A preference is a complete transitive binary relation R on A: if aRb and bRc then aRc, and for every a,b at least one of aRb,bRa holds. Write a\succ b for aRb and not bRa; both directions holding means indifference. Let \Pi(A) be the set of these preferences, with ties allowed.

A social welfare function is a function F:\Pi(A)^N\to\Pi(A) defined on every profile of the N voters' preferences. It is Pareto efficient if unanimous strict preference for a over b implies societal strict preference for a over b. It satisfies independence of irrelevant alternatives (IIA) if two profiles having the same individual comparisons of a with b have the same societal comparison of that pair. A dictator is a voter whose strict preferences always prevail for every profile and every pair.

Assume F is Pareto efficient and satisfies IIA. A coalition is a subset of voters. It is decisive over (x,y) if strict preference for x over y by all its members forces that societal preference, whatever the other voters do; it is decisive if this holds for every ordered pair of distinct alternatives. It is weakly decisive over (x,y) if it forces x\succ y whenever its members prefer x to y and all voters outside it prefer y to x.

If a coalition is weakly decisive over one ordered pair of distinct alternatives, then it is decisive.

Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Undergraduate familiarity with sets, functions, binary relations and quantifiers suffices. The preference, independence and coalition conditions are defined explicitly; they describe finite voter profiles rather than a new mathematical theory. Careful reading and a small preference-table example can clarify the nested conditions within the allowance.

Proof 1: Coalition comparison argument

Let G be a coalition weakly decisive over an ordered pair (x,y) of distinct alternatives.

Let {\displaystyle z} be an outcome distinct from {\displaystyle x,y}.

Claim: {\displaystyle G} is decisive over {\displaystyle (x,z)}.

Let everyone in {\displaystyle G} vote {\displaystyle x} over {\displaystyle z}. By IIA, changing the votes on {\displaystyle y} does not matter for {\displaystyle x,z}. So change the votes such that {\displaystyle x\succ _{i}y\succ _{i}z} in {\displaystyle G} and {\displaystyle y\succ _{i}x} and {\displaystyle y\succ _{i}z} outside of {\displaystyle G}.

By Pareto, {\displaystyle y\succ z}. By coalition weak-decisiveness over {\displaystyle (x,y)}, {\displaystyle x\succ y}. Thus {\displaystyle x\succ z}. {\displaystyle \square }

Similarly, {\displaystyle G} is decisive over {\displaystyle (z,y)}.

By iterating the above two claims (note that decisiveness implies weak-decisiveness), we find that {\displaystyle G} is decisive over all ordered pairs in {\displaystyle \{x,y,z\}}. Then iterating that, we find that {\displaystyle G} is decisive over all ordered pairs in {\displaystyle A}.

Level justification

The proof manipulates complete transitive relations and functions of finite preference profiles, using the explicitly defined Pareto and pairwise-independence conditions. From undergraduate relations, functions and quantified reasoning, decisiveness is a small set of related definitions. The argument changes only comparisons with a third alternative, invokes the two axioms and transitivity, then repeats the finite three-alternative reasoning for any chosen pair. No economic theory or general representation theorem is required.
