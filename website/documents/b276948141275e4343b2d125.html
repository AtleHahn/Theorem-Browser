Statement

Let f:G\to H be a group homomorphism. Its image \operatorname{im}f=\{f(g):g\in G\} is a subgroup of H, and the map \psi:G/\ker f\to\operatorname{im}f, g\ker f\mapsto f(g), is a group isomorphism. Here \ker f=\{g\in G:f(g)=e_H\}.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Introductory group and ring theory supplies subgroups, quotients, commutators and homomorphisms. Any added terms such as perfect, nilpotent or virtually free describe explicit subgroup conditions; they are small algebraic extensions without representation or geometric-group theory.

Proof 1: First isomorphism theorem for groups — proof

The proof follows from two basic facts about homomorphisms, namely their preservation of the group operation, and their mapping of the identity element to the identity element. We need to show that if f:G\to H is a homomorphism of groups, then:

  1. \operatorname{im}(f) is a subgroup of H.
  2. G/\ker(f) is isomorphic to \operatorname{im}(f).

For the first claim, the operation that is preserved by f is the group operation. If a,b\in\operatorname{im}(f), then there exist elements a',b'\in G such that f(a')=a and f(b')=b. For these a and b, we have ab=f(a')f(b')=f(a'b')\in\operatorname{im}(f) (since f preserves the group operation), and thus the closure property is satisfied in \operatorname{im}(f). The identity element e\in H is also in \operatorname{im}(f) because f maps the identity element of G to it. Since every element a' in G has an inverse (a')^{-1} such that f((a')^{-1})=(f(a'))^{-1} (because f preserves the inverse property as well), we have an inverse for each element f(a')=a in \operatorname{im}(f); therefore, \operatorname{im}(f) is a subgroup of H.

For the second claim, use that the kernel of a group homomorphism is normal, so that G/\ker(f) is a group. Construct a map \psi:G/\ker(f)\to\operatorname{im}(f) by \psi(a\ker(f))=f(a). This map is well-defined, as if a\ker(f)=b\ker(f), then b^{-1}a\in\ker(f) and so f(b^{-1}a)=e\Rightarrow f(b^{-1})f(a)=e, which gives f(a)=f(b). This map is an isomorphism. \psi is surjective onto \operatorname{im}(f) by definition. To show injectivity, if \psi(a\ker(f))=\psi(b\ker(f)), then f(a)=f(b), which implies b^{-1}a\in\ker(f), so a\ker(f)=b\ker(f).

Finally,

\begin{aligned} \psi((a\ker(f))(b\ker(f))) &=\psi(ab\ker(f))=f(ab)\\ &=f(a)f(b)=\psi(a\ker(f))\psi(b\ker(f)), \end{aligned}

hence \psi preserves the group operation. Hence \psi is an isomorphism between G/\ker(f) and \operatorname{im}(f), which completes the proof.
