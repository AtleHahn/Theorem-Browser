Statement

Let f(t)=\sum_{n\ge0}a_nt^n with a_n\in\mathbb Q have positive complex radius of convergence and represent an algebraic function there. For each prime p, regard the same series over \mathbb Q_p. Its p-adic radius of convergence is positive, and it is at least 1 for all but finitely many primes.

Proof 1: Interpret the denominator bound p-adically — outline

Eisenstein's theorem states that there exists a non-zero integer A, such that A^{n+1}a_n are all integers.

This has an interpretation in terms of p-adic numbers: with an appropriate extension of the idea, the p-adic radius of convergence of the series is at least 1, for almost all p (i.e., the primes outside a finite set S). In fact that statement is a little weaker, in that it disregards any initial partial sum of the series, in a way that may vary according to p. For the other primes the radius is non-zero.

Level justification

The coefficient estimate is elementary once supplied, but the proof interprets an algebraic analytic function simultaneously over C and the completed non-Archimedean fields Q_p and invokes the p-adic convergence-radius criterion. Algebraic power series, p-adic completions and their analytic convergence together exceed the conservative small-extension allowance. The difficulty is the framework of the application, not proving Eisenstein’s theorem.

Kind:

Theorem

Treatment:

Statement used:

For a power series over Q_p its radius of convergence is 1/limsup_n |a_n|_p^(1/n), with reciprocal zero and infinity interpreted conventionally.

Application in proof:

Infer radius at least |A|_p>0, and at least 1 when p does not divide A.

Beyond undergraduate level

Level justification:

This is the non-Archimedean analytic version of the radius criterion, requiring p-adic absolute values and convergence.

Treatment:

Statement used:

For an integer m, |m|_p<=1; a fixed nonzero integer A has |A|_p=1 for all but its finitely many prime divisors.

Application in proof:

Turn the integral-coefficient bound into a uniform radius lower bound away from those primes.

The numerical valuation fact follows from prime exponents in an integer and needs no p-adic analysis by itself.
