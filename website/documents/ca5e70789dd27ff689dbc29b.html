Statement

Let K be a nonempty compact convex subset of \mathbb R^m, where m is a positive integer. Every continuous map f:K\to K has a fixed point.

Proof 1: Radial retraction and homology obstruction

Let D^n=\{x\in\mathbb R^n:|x|\le1\} and S^{n-1}=\partial D^n, for an integer n\ge1.

Suppose, for contradiction, that a continuous function f : Dn → Dn has no fixed point. This means that, for every point x in Dn, the points x and f(x) are distinct. Because they are distinct, for every point x in Dn, we can construct a unique ray from f(x) to x and follow the ray until it intersects the boundary Sn−1. By calling this intersection point F(x), we define a function F : Dn → Sn−1 sending each point in the disk to its corresponding intersection point on the boundary. As a special case, whenever x itself is on the boundary, then the intersection point F(x) must be x.

Consequently, F is a special type of continuous function known as a retraction: every point of the codomain (in this case Sn−1) is a fixed point of F.

Intuitively it seems unlikely that there could be a retraction of D^n onto S^{n-1}, and in the case n = 1, the impossibility is more basic, because S0 (i.e., the endpoints of the closed interval D1) is not even connected. The case n = 2 is less obvious, but can be proven by using basic arguments involving the fundamental groups of the respective spaces: the retraction would induce a surjective group homomorphism from the fundamental group of D2 to that of S1, but the latter group is isomorphic to Z while the first group is trivial, so this is impossible.

For n > 2, however, proving the impossibility of the retraction is more difficult. One way is to make use of homology groups: the homology Hn−1(Dn) is trivial, while Hn−1(Sn−1) is infinite cyclic. This shows that the retraction is impossible, because inclusion of the sphere, split by the retraction, would induce an injective group homomorphism from the latter group to the former.

Every nonempty compact convex subset K of a finite-dimensional Euclidean space is homeomorphic to a closed ball of its affine dimension. Under such a homeomorphism, continuity and fixed points are invariant. Consequently the closed-ball theorem gives the result for K; dimension zero is a singleton.

Proof 2: Construct a tangent field on an even-dimensional sphere

Use the hairy ball theorem: an even-dimensional sphere has no continuous nowhere-zero tangent vector field. First suppose that n is even. If there were a fixed-point-free continuous self-mapping f of the closed unit ball B of the n-dimensional Euclidean space V, set

{\displaystyle {\mathbf {w} }({\mathbf {x} })=(1-{\mathbf {x} }\cdot {\mathbf {f} }({\mathbf {x} }))\,{\mathbf {x} }-(1-{\mathbf {x} }\cdot {\mathbf {x} })\,{\mathbf {f} }({\mathbf {x} }).}

Write S=\partial B. Since f has no fixed points, it follows that, for x in the interior of B, the vector w(x) is non-zero; and for x in S, the scalar product x ⋅ w(x) = 1 – x ⋅ f(x) is strictly positive. From the original n-dimensional Euclidean space V, construct a new auxiliary (n + 1)-dimensional space W = V x R, with coordinates y = (x, t). Set

{\displaystyle {\mathbf {X} }({\mathbf {x} },t)=(-t\,{\mathbf {w} }({\mathbf {x} }),{\mathbf {x} }\cdot {\mathbf {w} }({\mathbf {x} })).}

By construction X is a continuous vector field on the unit sphere of W, satisfying the tangency condition y ⋅ X(y) = 0. Moreover, X(y) is nowhere vanishing (because, if x has norm 1, then x ⋅ w(x) is non-zero; while if x has norm strictly less than 1, then t and w(x) are both non-zero). This contradiction proves the fixed point theorem when n is even. For n odd, one can apply the fixed point theorem to the closed unit ball B in n + 1 dimensions and the mapping F(x,y) = (f(x),0).

Under such a homeomorphism, continuity and fixed points are invariant. Consequently the closed-ball theorem gives the result for K; dimension zero is a singleton.

Proof 4: Compute the Lefschetz number of a ball

Use the Lefschetz fixed-point theorem in the form: a continuous self-map of a finite simplicial complex whose Lefschetz number is nonzero has a fixed point. With rational homology, the Lefschetz number is L(f)=\sum_i(-1)^i\operatorname{Tr}(f_*:H_i(B;\mathbb Q)\to H_i(B;\mathbb Q)).

If B is a ball (or more generally a nonempty contractible finite simplicial complex) then the Lefschetz number is one because the only non-zero simplicial homology group is H_0(B;\mathbb Q)\cong\mathbb Q and f acts as the identity on this group, so f has a fixed point.

Every nonempty compact convex subset K of a finite-dimensional Euclidean space is homeomorphic to a closed ball of its affine dimension. Under such a homeomorphism, continuity and fixed points are invariant. Consequently the closed-ball theorem gives the result for K; dimension zero is a singleton.

Proof 5: Normalized displacement homotopy and degree — outline

Use continuous Brouwer degree with these properties: the identity has degree one at 0; degree at 0 is unchanged along a continuous homotopy whose boundary images avoid 0; nonzero degree implies a preimage of 0.

Let D^n=\{x\in\mathbb R^n:|x|\le1\} and S^{n-1}=\partial D^n, for an integer n\ge1. For a continuous f:D^n\to D^n, a boundary fixed point already proves the result. Otherwise put K=D^n.

If there is no fixed point of the boundary of {\displaystyle K}, then the function

{\displaystyle g(x)={\frac {x-f(x)}{\sup _{y\in K}\left|y-f(y)\right|}}}

is well-defined, and

{\displaystyle H(t,x)={\frac {x-tf(x)}{\sup _{y\in K}\left|y-tf(y)\right|}}}

defines a homotopy from the identity function to it. In particular, the identity function has degree one at the origin, so {\displaystyle g} also has degree one at the origin. As a consequence, the preimage {\displaystyle g^{-1}(0)} is not empty. The elements of {\displaystyle g^{-1}(0)} are precisely the fixed points of the original function f.

Under such a homeomorphism, continuity and fixed points are invariant. Consequently the closed-ball theorem gives the result for K; dimension zero is a singleton.
