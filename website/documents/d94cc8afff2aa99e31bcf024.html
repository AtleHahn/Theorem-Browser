Statement

Let a<b be real numbers, let f:[a,b]\to\mathbb R be continuous, and suppose \min(f(a),f(b))<u<\max(f(a),f(b)). Then there is c\in(a,b) with f(c)=u.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Understanding the statement involves real continuity; closed intervals; intermediate values. These prerequisites fit undergraduate coursework or a brief extension of it. A bright US college student should be able to understand this statement after approximately 30 minutes of focused background research; this assesses understanding the claim, not its proof.

Proof 1: Supremum of a strict sublevel set and an epsilon squeeze

The theorem may be proven as a consequence of the completeness property of the real numbers as follows:

We shall prove the first case, {\displaystyle f(a)<u<f(b)}. The second case is similar.

  1. Let {\displaystyle S} be the set of all {\displaystyle x\in [a,b]} such that {\displaystyle f(x)<u}.
  2. Then {\displaystyle S} is non-empty since {\displaystyle a} is an element of {\displaystyle S}.
  3. Since {\displaystyle S} is non-empty and bounded above by {\displaystyle b}, by completeness, the supremum {\displaystyle c=\sup S} exists. That is, {\displaystyle c} is the smallest number that is greater than or equal to every member of {\displaystyle S}.

Note that, due to the continuity of {\displaystyle f} at {\displaystyle a}, we can keep {\displaystyle f(x)} within any {\displaystyle \varepsilon >0} of {\displaystyle f(a)} by keeping {\displaystyle x} sufficiently close to {\displaystyle a}. Since {\displaystyle f(a)<u} is a strict inequality, consider the implication when {\displaystyle \varepsilon } is the distance between {\displaystyle u} and {\displaystyle f(a)}. No {\displaystyle x} sufficiently close to {\displaystyle a} can then make {\displaystyle f(x)} greater than or equal to {\displaystyle u}, which means there are values greater than {\displaystyle a} in {\displaystyle S}. A more detailed proof goes like this:

  1. Choose {\displaystyle \varepsilon =u-f(a)>0}. Then {\displaystyle \exists \delta >0} such that {\displaystyle \forall x\in [a,b]},

    {\displaystyle |x-a|<\delta \implies |f(x)-f(a)|<u-f(a)\implies f(x)<u.}

  2. Consider the interval {\displaystyle [a,\min(a+\delta ,b))=I_{1}}. Notice that {\displaystyle I_{1}\subseteq [a,b]} and every {\displaystyle x\in I_{1}} satisfies the condition {\displaystyle |x-a|<\delta }. Therefore for every {\displaystyle x\in I_{1}} we have {\displaystyle f(x)<u}. Hence {\displaystyle c} cannot be {\displaystyle a}.

Likewise, due to the continuity of {\displaystyle f} at {\displaystyle b}, we can keep {\displaystyle f(x)} within any {\displaystyle \varepsilon >0} of {\displaystyle f(b)} by keeping {\displaystyle x} sufficiently close to {\displaystyle b}. Since {\displaystyle u<f(b)} is a strict inequality, consider the similar implication when {\displaystyle \varepsilon } is the distance between {\displaystyle u} and {\displaystyle f(b)}. Every {\displaystyle x} sufficiently close to {\displaystyle b} must then make {\displaystyle f(x)} greater than {\displaystyle u}, which means there are values smaller than {\displaystyle b} that are upper bounds of {\displaystyle S}. A more detailed proof goes like this:

  1. Choose {\displaystyle \varepsilon =f(b)-u>0}. Then {\displaystyle \exists \delta >0} such that {\displaystyle \forall x\in [a,b]},

    {\displaystyle |x-b|<\delta \implies |f(x)-f(b)|<f(b)-u\implies f(x)>u.}

  2. Consider the interval {\displaystyle (\max(a,b-\delta ),b]=I_{2}}. Notice that {\displaystyle I_{2}\subseteq [a,b]} and every {\displaystyle x\in I_{2}} satisfies the condition {\displaystyle |x-b|<\delta }. Therefore for every {\displaystyle x\in I_{2}} we have {\displaystyle f(x)>u}. Hence {\displaystyle c} cannot be {\displaystyle b}.

With {\displaystyle c\neq a} and {\displaystyle c\neq b}, it must be the case {\displaystyle c\in (a,b)}. Now we claim that {\displaystyle f(c)=u}.

Fix some {\displaystyle \varepsilon >0}. Since {\displaystyle f} is continuous at {\displaystyle c}, {\displaystyle \exists \delta _{1}>0} such that {\displaystyle \forall x\in [a,b]}, {\displaystyle |x-c|<\delta _{1}\implies |f(x)-f(c)|<\varepsilon }.

Since {\displaystyle c\in (a,b)} and {\displaystyle (a,b)} is open, {\displaystyle \exists \delta _{2}>0} such that {\displaystyle (c-\delta _{2},c+\delta _{2})\subseteq (a,b)}. Set {\displaystyle \delta =\min(\delta _{1},\delta _{2})}. Then we have

{\displaystyle f(x)-\varepsilon <f(c)<f(x)+\varepsilon }

for all {\displaystyle x\in (c-\delta ,c+\delta )}. By the properties of the supremum, there exists some {\displaystyle a^{*}\in (c-\delta ,c]} that is contained in {\displaystyle S}, and so

{\displaystyle f(c)<f(a^{*})+\varepsilon <u+\varepsilon .}

Picking {\displaystyle a^{**}\in (c,c+\delta )}, we know that {\displaystyle a^{**}\not \in S} because {\displaystyle c} is the supremum of {\displaystyle S}. This means that

{\displaystyle f(c)>f(a^{**})-\varepsilon \geq u-\varepsilon .}

Both inequalities

{\displaystyle u-\varepsilon <f(c)<u+\varepsilon }

are valid for all {\displaystyle \varepsilon >0}, from which we deduce {\displaystyle f(c)=u} as the only possible value, as stated.

Proof 2: Connected image and the interval characterization

Use the following two properties of connectedness:

  • A continuous image of a connected space is connected.
  • A subset E\subset\mathbb R is connected if and only if it satisfies the following property: x,y\in E, x<r<y imply r\in E.

By the second property, I=[a,b] is a connected set. It follows from the first that the image, f(I), is also connected. For convenience, assume that f(a)<f(b). Then once more invoking the second property, f(a)<u<f(b) implies that u\in f(I), or f(c)=u for some c\in I. Since u\neq f(a),f(b), c\in(a,b) must actually hold, and the desired conclusion follows. The same argument applies if f(b)<f(a), so we are done.
