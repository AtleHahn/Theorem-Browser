Statement

Let f:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb R or \mathbb C be measurable with finite limit \alpha=\lim_{t\downarrow0}f(t). Suppose |f(t)|\le M e^{ct} for all t>0, for constants M<\infty,c\in\mathbb R. For real s>c let F(s)=\int_0^\infty f(t)e^{-st}\,dt. Then \lim_{s\to\infty}sF(s)=\alpha.

Proof 1: Exponentially damp the function and shift the transform

The theorem assuming just that {\displaystyle |f(t)|\le Me^{ct}\quad(t>0)} follows from the theorem for bounded {\displaystyle f}:

Define {\displaystyle g(t)=e^{-ct}f(t)}. Then {\displaystyle g} is bounded, so the bounded-function Laplace initial-value theorem gives {\displaystyle g(0^{+})=\lim _{s\to \infty }sG(s)}. But {\displaystyle f(0^{+})=g(0^{+})} and {\displaystyle G(s)=F(s+c)}, so

{\displaystyle \lim _{s\to \infty }sF(s)=\lim _{s\to \infty }(s-c)F(s)=\lim _{s\to \infty }sF(s+c)=\lim _{s\to \infty }sG(s),}

since {\displaystyle \lim _{s\to \infty }F(s)=0}. The limit F(s)\to0 follows because (s-c)F(s) has the finite limit \alpha.

Level justification

Damp the measurable function and shift its transform, then apply the bounded theorem. The algebra is short but the dependency is a theorem about arbitrary measurable Lebesgue integrals, so retain beyond undergraduate conservatively.
