Statement

Let f_1,\ldots,f_k be differentiable real functions, all nonzero on an interval, and put F=\prod_{i=1}^k f_i. Then

\frac{F'}F=\sum_{i=1}^k\frac{f_i'}{f_i},\qquad F'=F\sum_{i=1}^k\frac{f_i'}{f_i}.
Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Understanding the statement involves Differentiation; Limits; Elementary algebra. These prerequisites fit undergraduate coursework or a brief extension of it. A bright US college student should be able to understand this statement after approximately 30 minutes of focused background research; this assesses understanding the claim, not its proof.

Proof 1: Differentiate the logarithm of the product

Repeated use of the two-factor product rule makes F differentiable. The logarithmic derivative of a nonvanishing real function f, denoted here \operatorname{Logder}(f), is the derivative of \ln|f|. It follows that

\operatorname{Logder}(f)=\frac{f'}{f}.

Using that the logarithm of the absolute value of a product is the sum of the logarithms of the absolute values of the factors, the sum rule for derivatives gives immediately

\operatorname{Logder}(f_1\cdots f_k)=\sum_{i=1}^k\operatorname{Logder}(f_i).

The expression F'=F\sum_{i=1}^k f_i'/f_i is obtained by multiplying both members of this equation by the product of the f_i.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Apply ordinary real product and chain rules to logarithms of absolute values, then divide by the nonzero product. This is single-variable calculus.
