Statement

On real L^2([0,1]), let (Tf)(t)=\int_0^1\min(s,t)f(s)\,ds. Its positive eigenvalues and normalized real eigenfunctions, up to sign, are \lambda_k=((k-\tfrac12)\pi)^{-2} and e_k(t)=\sqrt2\sin((k-\tfrac12)\pi t), k\geq1.

Level
Beyond undergraduate level
Level justification

The operator acts on an infinite-dimensional L² space.

Proof 1: Differentiate the integral equation and impose boundary conditions

In order to find the eigenvalues and eigenvectors, we need to solve the integral equation:

{\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}(Te)(t)&=\lambda e(t)\qquad \forall t,0\leq t\leq 1\\\int _{0}^{1}\min(s,t)e(s)\,ds&=\lambda e(t)\qquad \forall t,0\leq t\leq 1\\\int _{0}^{t}se(s)\,ds+t\int _{t}^{1}e(s)\,ds&=\lambda e(t)\qquad \forall t,0\leq t\leq 1\end{aligned}}}

differentiating once with respect to t yields:

{\displaystyle \int _{t}^{1}e(s)\,ds=\lambda e'(t)}

a second differentiation produces the following differential equation:

{\displaystyle -e(t)=\lambda e''(t)}

For \lambda>0, the general solution of which has the form:

{\displaystyle e(t)=A\sin \left({\frac {t}{\sqrt {\lambda }}}\right)+B\cos \left({\frac {t}{\sqrt {\lambda }}}\right)}

where A and B are two constants to be determined with the boundary conditions. Setting t = 0 in the initial integral equation gives e(0) = 0 which implies that B = 0 and similarly, setting t = 1 in the first differentiation yields e' (1) = 0, whence:

{\displaystyle \cos \left({\frac {1}{\sqrt {\lambda }}}\right)=0}

which in turn implies that eigenvalues of the covariance operator T are:

{\displaystyle \lambda _{k}=\left({\frac {1}{(k-{\frac {1}{2}})\pi }}\right)^{2},\qquad k\geq 1}

The corresponding eigenfunctions are thus of the form:

{\displaystyle e_{k}(t)=A\sin \left((k-{\frac {1}{2}})\pi t\right),\qquad k\geq 1}

A is then chosen so as to normalize ek:

{\displaystyle \int _{0}^{1}e_{k}^{2}(t)\,dt=1\quad \implies \quad A=\pm{\sqrt {2}}}

Choose the positive sign. The source leaves the regularity and reverse substitution checks implicit.

Level justification

Differentiate the integral eigen-equation and solve the resulting boundary ODE. The ODE is undergraduate, but establishing this for arbitrary L2 eigenclasses needs Lebesgue regularity and representative arguments not supplied as a small explicit lemma; retain beyond undergraduate provisionally.
