Statement

Write i^2=-1 and e^z=\sum_{m=0}^\infty z^m/m! for the complex exponential. For z\in\mathbb C\setminus\{0\} and any real argument \varphi of z, the numbers \ln|z|+i(\varphi+2\pi k), k\in\mathbb Z, are exactly the logarithms of z, meaning all w with e^w=z.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

The complete statement explicitly defines the exponential by an infinite complex power series. Understanding that definition requires limits and series in addition to polar coordinates and real logarithms; undergraduate analysis suffices. No theory of analytic logarithm branches is required.

Proof 1: Exponentiate the real logarithm plus an argument — outline

We can use Euler's polar formula to define the logarithm of a complex number. To do this, we also use the definition of the real logarithm (as the inverse operator of exponentiation),

a=e^{\ln a}\qquad(a>0),

and

e^ae^b=e^{a+b},

valid for any complex numbers a and b. Therefore, one can write:

z=|z|e^{i\varphi}=e^{\ln|z|}e^{i\varphi}=e^{\ln|z|+i\varphi}

for any z\ne0. Thus \ln|z|+i\varphi is a logarithm of z. The logarithm of a complex number is a multi-valued function, because \varphi is multi-valued: all arguments are \varphi+2\pi k, k\in\mathbb Z. The source leaves the converse comparison of modulus and argument implicit.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Understanding the full statement includes its infinite-power-series definition of the complex exponential. The proof then applies Euler’s formula and the addition law, and compares modulus and arguments modulo 2pi; those identities are used, not reproved.
