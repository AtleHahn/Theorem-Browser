Statement

Let q:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb R be continuous and consider y\prime\prime+q(x)y=0. Call it oscillating if a nonzero real solution has infinitely many zeros tending to infinity. If \limsup_{x\to\infty}x^2q(x)<1/4, it is nonoscillating. If \liminf_{x\to\infty}x^2q(x)>1/4, it is oscillating.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Understanding the statement involves Linear ordinary differential equations; Oscillation; Upper and lower limits. These prerequisites fit undergraduate coursework or a brief extension of it. A bright US college student should be able to understand this statement after approximately 30 minutes of focused background research; this assesses understanding the claim, not its proof.

Proof 1: Compare with the inverse-square Euler equation — outline

Consider the model coefficient

{\displaystyle q(x)=\left({\frac {1}{4}}-a\right)x^{-2}\quad {\text{for}}\quad x>0}

where {\displaystyle a} is real and non-zero. To find the solutions for this choice of {\displaystyle q(x)}, substitute the 'Ansatz'

{\displaystyle y(x)=x^{n}}

which gives

{\displaystyle n(n-1)+{\frac {1}{4}}-a=\left(n-{\frac {1}{2}}\right)^{2}-a=0}

This means that (for non-zero {\displaystyle a}) the general complex solution is, by the standard basis theorem for second-order linear equations,

{\displaystyle y(x)=Ax^{{\frac {1}{2}}+{\sqrt {a}}}+Bx^{{\frac {1}{2}}-{\sqrt {a}}}}

where {\displaystyle A} and {\displaystyle B} are arbitrary constants.

It is not hard to see that for positive {\displaystyle a} nonzero real solutions do not oscillate while for negative {\displaystyle a=-\omega ^{2}} the identity

{\displaystyle x^{{\frac {1}{2}}\pm i\omega }={\sqrt {x}}\ e^{\pm (i\omega )\ln {x}}={\sqrt {x}}\ (\cos {(\omega \ln x)}\pm i\sin {(\omega \ln x)})}

shows that nonzero real solutions oscillate.

The general result follows from this example by the Sturm–Picone comparison theorem, comparing on a sufficiently large positive half-line.
