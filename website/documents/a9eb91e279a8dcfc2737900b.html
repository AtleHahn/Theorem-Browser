Statement

For every integer n\geq1, the Fourier transform

\widehat f(\xi)=\int_{\mathbb R^n}f(x)e^{-2\pi i x\cdot\xi}\,dx\qquad(f\in L^1(\mathbb R^n))

maps L^1(\mathbb R^n)\cap L^2(\mathbb R^n) into L^2(\mathbb R^n) and satisfies

\int_{\mathbb R^n}|\widehat f(\xi)|^2\,d\xi=\int_{\mathbb R^n}|f(x)|^2\,dx.

It has a unique extension to a unitary operator \mathcal F:L^2(\mathbb R^n)\to L^2(\mathbb R^n); that is, a surjective complex-linear isometry. In particular,

\int (\mathcal Ff)\overline{\mathcal Fg}=\int f\overline g\qquad(f,g\in L^2(\mathbb R^n)).

Here x\cdot\xi is the Euclidean inner product and L^p consists of measurable complex-valued functions with finite integral of |f|^p, identified when equal almost everywhere; all integrals use Lebesgue measure. The extended transform is the L^2 limit of transforms of any sequence from L^1\cap L^2 converging to f in L^2.

Proof availability

Source proof sketch and limitations

The source suggests checking the identity for the standard Gaussian, which is its own Fourier transform, extending to translated and scaled Gaussians, and then using density. It also explains that the norm identity gives an isometric extension from L^1\cap L^2 by completion, and that polarization gives the inner-product identity.

The Gaussian paragraph does not establish the required cross terms for linear combinations or the density argument in detail, and it does not prove surjectivity. These omissions are substantive for the full unitary conclusion. The sketch is retained as such; no proof is invented to fill those steps.
