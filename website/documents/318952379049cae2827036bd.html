Statement

Assuming the axiom of choice, every closed ball B\subset\mathbb R^3 of positive radius admits a partition into finitely many subsets which, by rotations and translations, can be reassembled into two disjoint balls congruent to B. The pieces are arbitrary point sets; no measurability is asserted.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

The statements use balls, finite set partitions and rigid motions. Undergraduate set theory suffices to distinguish arbitrary point subsets from physical solids, and the axiom of choice adds one explicit set-selection principle where invoked. Understanding the assertion does not require the advanced construction of the pieces.

Proof 1: Free rotation orbits, radial extension and absorption (outline)

Free rotation subgroup (external prerequisite)

There is a subgroup H=\langle a,b\rangle of SO(3) freely generated by two rotations. This existence result is used as a prerequisite here; its arithmetic proof is not supplied locally.

Orbit decomposition

The free group with two generators a and b consists of all finite strings that can be formed from the four symbols a, a−1, b and b−1 such that no a appears directly next to an a−1 and no b appears directly next to a b−1. Two such strings can be concatenated and converted into a string of this type by repeatedly replacing the "forbidden" substrings with the empty string. For instance: abab−1a−1 concatenated with abab−1a yields abab−1a−1abab−1a, which contains the substring a−1a, and so gets reduced to abab−1bab−1a, which contains the substring b−1b, which gets reduced to abaab−1a. One can check that the set of those strings with this operation forms a group with identity element the empty string e. This group may be called F2.

The group {\displaystyle F_{2}} can be "paradoxically decomposed" as follows: Let S(a) be the subset of {\displaystyle F_{2}} consisting of all strings that start with a, and define S(a−1), S(b) and S(b−1) similarly. Clearly,

{\displaystyle F_{2}=\{e\}\cup S(a)\cup S(a^{-1})\cup S(b)\cup S(b^{-1})}

but also

{\displaystyle F_{2}=aS(a^{-1})\cup S(a),}

and

{\displaystyle F_{2}=bS(b^{-1})\cup S(b),}

where the notation aS(a−1) means take all the strings in S(a−1) and concatenate them on the left with a.

This is at the core of the proof. For example, there may be a string {\displaystyle aa^{-1}b} in the set {\displaystyle aS(a^{-1})} which, because of the rule that {\displaystyle a} must not appear next to {\displaystyle a^{-1}}, reduces to the string {\displaystyle b}. Similarly, {\displaystyle aS(a^{-1})} contains all the strings that start with {\displaystyle a^{-1}} (for example, the string {\displaystyle aa^{-1}a^{-1}} which reduces to {\displaystyle a^{-1}}). In this way, {\displaystyle aS(a^{-1})} contains all the strings that start with {\displaystyle b}, {\displaystyle b^{-1}} and {\displaystyle a^{-1}}, as well as the empty string {\displaystyle e}.

Group F2 has been cut into four pieces (plus the singleton {e}), then two of them "shifted" by multiplying with a or b, then "reassembled" as two pieces to make one copy of {\displaystyle F_{2}} and the other two to make another copy of {\displaystyle F_{2}}. That is nearly what is intended to do to the ball.

Unfortunately, the identity element leaves behind a leftover piece of the ball when we decompose it like this, so we need a new paradoxical decomposition of {\displaystyle F_{2}} which includes the identity element in one of its sets. One solution is to place all the elements of {\displaystyle \langle a\rangle =\{a^{n}:n\in \mathbb {Z} \}} into the set {\displaystyle S(a)} (consequently, removing them from {\displaystyle S(a^{-1})}). Since {\displaystyle a\langle a\rangle =\langle a\rangle },

{\displaystyle F_{2}=(S(a)\cup \langle a\rangle )\cup a(S(a^{-1})\setminus \langle a\rangle )}

and therefore we can write {\displaystyle F_{2}} as the paradoxical decomposition

{\displaystyle F_{2}=(S(a)\cup \langle a\rangle )\cup (S(a^{-1})\setminus \langle a\rangle )\cup S(b)\cup S(b^{-1}),}

which has no leftover sets.

Let D be the points of S^2 fixed by some nonidentity element of H. Since any nonidentity rotation has exactly two fixed points on S^2, and H is countable, D is countable. Work first on Y=S^2\setminus D, where the action is free.

The punctured sphere Y=S^2\setminus D is partitioned into orbits by the action of our group H: two points belong to the same orbit if and only if there is a rotation in H which moves the first point into the second. The axiom of choice can be used to pick exactly one point from every orbit; collect these points into a set M. The action of H on a given orbit is free and transitive and so each orbit can be identified with H. In other words, every point in Y can be reached in exactly one way by applying the proper rotation from H to the proper element from M. Because of this, the paradoxical decomposition of H yields a paradoxical decomposition of Y into four pieces A1, A2, A3, A4 as follows:

{\displaystyle A_{1}=(S(a)\cup \langle a\rangle )M}{\displaystyle A_{2}=(S(a^{-1})\setminus \langle a\rangle )M}{\displaystyle \displaystyle A_{3}=S(b)M}{\displaystyle \displaystyle A_{4}=S(b^{-1})M}

where we define

{\displaystyle S(a)M=\{s(x)|s\in S(a),x\in M\}}

and likewise for the other sets.

The set Y has now been divided into four sets, and when two of these are rotated, the result double what was had before:

{\displaystyle aA_{2}=A_{2}\cup A_{3}\cup A_{4}}{\displaystyle bA_{4}=A_{1}\cup A_{2}\cup A_{4}}

Consequently A_1\sqcup aA_2=Y and A_3\sqcup bA_4=Y, giving two copies of Y.

Restore the countable exceptional set

What remains to be shown is the Claim: S^2-D is equidecomposable with S^2.

Let λ be some line through the origin that does not intersect any point in D. This is possible since D is countable. Let J be the set of angles, α, such that for some positive integer n, and some P in D, r(nα)P is also in D, where r(nα) is a rotation about λ of nα. Then J is countable. So there exists an angle θ not in J. Let ρ be the rotation about λ by θ. Then ρ acts on S2 with no fixed points in D, and, for every positive integer n, ρn(D) is disjoint from D, and for natural m<n, ρn(D) is disjoint from ρm(D). Let E be the disjoint union of ρn(D) over n = 0, 1, 2, ... . Then ρ(E) is the disjoint union of ρn(D) over n ≥ 1 and therefore ρ(E) = E − D. Now we can conclude

S2 = E ∪ (S2 − E) ~ ρ(E) ∪ (S2 − E) = (E − D) ∪ (S2 − E) = S2 − D, where ~ denotes "is equidecomposable to".

Use is made of the fact that if A ~ B and B ~ C, then A ~ C. The decomposition of A into C can be done using number of pieces equal to the product of the numbers needed for taking A into B and for taking B into C.

Thus S^2 is equidecomposable with Y, and each of the two reconstructed copies of Y can also be equidecomposed with S^2.

Extend radially and restore the center

Finally, connect every point on S^2 with a half-open segment to the origin, including the sphere point and excluding the origin; the paradoxical decomposition of S^2 then yields a paradoxical decomposition of the solid unit ball minus the point at the ball's center.

It remains to be shown that the ball minus its center point is equidecomposable with the ball. Consider a circle within the ball, containing the point at the center of the ball. Using the countable-orbit shift in the Claim, one can see that the full circle is equidecomposable with the circle minus the point at the ball's center. Explicitly, if \rho is a rotation of the circle with distinct iterates of the center o, and E=\{\rho^n(o):n=0,1,2,\ldots\}, then \rho(E)=E-\{o\}; leave the rest of the ball fixed. (Basically, a countable set of points on the circle can be rotated to give itself plus one more point.) Note that this involves the rotation about a point other than the ball's center, so the construction involves isometries of Euclidean 3-space rather than just SO(3).

Compose these finite equidecompositions and translate one of the resulting balls to make the two balls disjoint. The same construction applies after choosing the center and radius of any positive-radius ball.
