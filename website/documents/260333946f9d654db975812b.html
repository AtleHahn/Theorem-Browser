Statement

For four distinct points A,B,C,D in cyclic order on a Euclidean circle, AC\cdot BD=AB\cdot CD+BC\cdot DA.

Level
High school only
Level justification

The statements and local arguments use elementary geometry and algebra.

Review notes

Proof 1: Split a diagonal into two similar-triangle contributions

Let ABCD be a cyclic quadrilateral. On the chord BC, the inscribed angles \angle BAC=\angle BDC, and on AB, \angle ADB=\angle ACB. Construct K on AC such that \angle ABK=\angle CBD; since

\angle ABK+\angle CBK=\angle ABC=\angle CBD+\angle ABD,

\angle CBK=\angle ABD.

Now, by common angles, \triangle ABK is similar to \triangle DBC, and likewise \triangle ABD is similar to \triangle KBC. Thus

\frac{AK}{AB}=\frac{CD}{BD},\qquad \frac{CK}{BC}=\frac{DA}{BD};

equivalently, AK\cdot BD=AB\cdot CD, and CK\cdot BD=BC\cdot DA. By adding the two equalities, we have

AK\cdot BD+CK\cdot BD=AB\cdot CD+BC\cdot DA,

and factorizing this gives

(AK+CK)\cdot BD=AB\cdot CD+BC\cdot DA.

But AK+CK=AC, so AC\cdot BD=AB\cdot CD+BC\cdot DA.

Level
High school only
Level justification

Split a diagonal at the angle-defined point, use two triangle similarities and add their length-product identities. School Euclidean geometry suffices.

Proof 2: Sum the pedal distances on the Simson line — outline

We first use the distance formula stating that for distances {\displaystyle AD,BD,CD} from a fixed point {\displaystyle D} to the vertices of {\displaystyle \triangle ABC}, and perpendiculars {\displaystyle DA_{1},DB_{1},DC_{1}} dropped to the three sides {\displaystyle BC,AC,AB}, where the feet of these perpendiculars are the vertices of a triangle {\displaystyle A_{1}B_{1}C_{1}}, the sides of {\displaystyle \triangle A_{1}B_{1}C_{1}} are

{\displaystyle B_{1}C_{1}={\frac {BC\cdot AD}{2R}},\ C_{1}A_{1}={\frac {AC\cdot BD}{2R}},\ A_{1}B_{1}={\frac {AB\cdot CD}{2R}}}

where {\displaystyle R} is the radius of the triangle {\displaystyle ABC} circumcircle. These formulas follow by continuity in degenerate pedal configurations. In the nondegenerate case, to prove this, we apply the Law of Sines to the triangles {\displaystyle AC_{1}B_{1}}, {\displaystyle BA_{1}C_{1}}, {\displaystyle CB_{1}A_{1}}, and also to {\displaystyle \triangle ABC} itself.

In the sequel, we place the point {\displaystyle D} on the circumcircle to obtain the cyclic quadrilateral {\displaystyle ABCD}. Now, the feet {\displaystyle A_{1},B_{1},C_{1}} are colinear, and form the Simson line. For the stated cyclic order, B_1 lies between A_1 and C_1. Thus, {\displaystyle A_{1}C_{1}=A_{1}B_{1}+B_{1}C_{1}}, and substituting the displayed distance formulas and multiplying by 2R yields Ptolemy's theorem.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Derive the pedal-distance formulas by the sine law, extend them to degenerate configurations by continuity and add distances along the supplied Simson line. Undergraduate elementary continuity together with school geometry suffices; continuity is explicitly used to cover all configurations.

Proof 3: Expand the chord sine products

Let \alpha,\beta,\gamma>0 be half the successive arcs AB,BC,CD, so that \alpha+\beta+\gamma<\pi. The inscribed angles subtended by {\displaystyle AB}, {\displaystyle BC} and {\displaystyle CD} are, respectively, {\displaystyle \alpha }, {\displaystyle \beta } and {\displaystyle \gamma }, and let the radius of the circle be {\displaystyle R}. Then we have {\displaystyle AB=2R\sin \alpha }, {\displaystyle BC=2R\sin \beta }, {\displaystyle CD=2R\sin \gamma }, {\displaystyle AD=2R\sin(\pi-(\alpha +\beta +\gamma ))}, {\displaystyle AC=2R\sin(\alpha +\beta )} and {\displaystyle BD=2R\sin(\beta +\gamma )}, and the original equality to be proved is transformed to

{\displaystyle \sin(\alpha +\beta )\sin(\beta +\gamma )=\sin \alpha \sin \gamma +\sin \beta \sin(\alpha +\beta +\gamma )}

from which the factor {\displaystyle 4R^{2}} has disappeared by dividing both sides of the equation by it.

Now by using the angle sum formulae, {\displaystyle \sin(x+y)=\sin {x}\cos y+\cos x\sin y} and {\displaystyle \cos(x+y)=\cos x\cos y-\sin x\sin y}, it is trivial to show that both sides of the above equation are equal to

{\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}&\sin \alpha \sin \beta \cos \beta \cos \gamma +\sin \alpha \cos ^{2}\beta \sin \gamma \\+{}&\cos \alpha \sin ^{2}\beta \cos \gamma +\cos \alpha \sin \beta \cos \beta \sin \gamma .\end{aligned}}}

Q.E.D.

Level
High school only
Level justification

Express all six chords by their half-arc sines and expand the angle-addition formulas. School trigonometry and algebra suffice.

Proof 4: Reflect a triangle and compare areas — outline

Let \alpha,\beta,\gamma>0 be half the successive arcs AB,BC,CD of the given cyclic quadrilateral, so \alpha+\beta+\gamma<\pi. Define a new quadrilateral {\displaystyle ABCD'} inscribed in the same circle, where {\displaystyle A,B,C} are the same as in {\displaystyle ABCD}, and {\displaystyle D'} located at a new point on the same circle, defined by {\displaystyle |{\overline {AD'}}|=|{\overline {CD}}|}, {\displaystyle |{\overline {CD'}}|=|{\overline {AD}}|}. (Picture triangle {\displaystyle ACD} flipped, so that vertex {\displaystyle C} moves to vertex {\displaystyle A} and vertex {\displaystyle A} moves to vertex {\displaystyle C}. Vertex {\displaystyle D} will now be located at a new point D’ on the circle.) Then, {\displaystyle ABCD'} has the same lengths of edges, and consequently the same inscribed angles subtended by the corresponding edges, as {\displaystyle ABCD}, only in a different order. That is, {\displaystyle \alpha }, {\displaystyle \beta } and {\displaystyle \gamma }, for, respectively, {\displaystyle AB,BC} and {\displaystyle AD'}. Reflect across the perpendicular bisector of AC, so that D and D' lie on the same side of AC. Also, {\displaystyle ABCD} and {\displaystyle ABCD'} have the same area. Then,

{\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}\mathrm {Area} (ABCD)&={\frac {1}{2}}AC\cdot BD\cdot \sin(\alpha +\gamma );\\\mathrm {Area} (ABCD')&={\frac {1}{2}}AB\cdot AD'\cdot \sin(\pi-\alpha -\gamma )+{\frac {1}{2}}BC\cdot CD'\cdot \sin(\alpha +\gamma )\\&={\frac {1}{2}}(AB\cdot CD+BC\cdot AD)\cdot \sin(\alpha +\gamma ).\end{aligned}}}

Equating the areas and cancelling the positive factor \sin(\alpha+\gamma)/2 proves the equality.

Level
High school only
Level justification

Reflect the indicated triangle to preserve area and compute the two quadrilateral areas using sine formulas, then cancel the positive factor. School Euclidean geometry and trigonometry suffice.

Proof 5: Invert the circle to a line

Choose an auxiliary circle {\displaystyle \Gamma } of radius {\displaystyle r} centered at D with respect to which the circumcircle of ABCD is inverted into a line under the map X\mapsto X' determined by DX\cdot DX'=r^2 on the ray DX. The images A',B',C' occur in this order on the line. Then {\displaystyle A'B'+B'C'=A'C'.} Then {\displaystyle A'B',B'C'} and {\displaystyle A'C'} can be expressed as {\textstyle {\frac {AB\cdot DB'}{DA}}}, {\textstyle {\frac {BC\cdot DB'}{DC}}} and {\textstyle {\frac {AC\cdot DC'}{DA}}} respectively. Multiplying each term by {\textstyle {\frac {DA\cdot DC}{DB'}}} and using {\textstyle {\frac {DC'}{DB'}}={\frac {DB}{DC}}} yields Ptolemy's equality.

Q.E.D.

Level
High school only
Level justification

Apply the explicitly defined inversion and its distance formula, add the ordered collinear image distances and cancel scale factors. School plane geometry and algebra suffice for these supplied inversion facts.

Proof 6: Use positivity of the complex cross-ratio

Choose clockwise coordinates and embed the convex quadrilateral ABCD in the complex plane {\displaystyle \mathbb {C} } by identifying {\displaystyle A\mapsto z_{A},\ldots ,D\mapsto z_{D}} as four distinct complex numbers {\displaystyle z_{A},\ldots ,z_{D}\in \mathbb {C} }. Define the cross-ratio

{\displaystyle \zeta :={\frac {(z_{A}-z_{B})(z_{C}-z_{D})}{(z_{A}-z_{D})(z_{B}-z_{C})}}\in \mathbb {C} _{\neq 0}}.

Then

{\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}{\overline {AB}}\cdot {\overline {CD}}+{\overline {AD}}\cdot {\overline {BC}}&=\left|z_{A}-z_{B}\right|\left|z_{C}-z_{D}\right|+\left|z_{A}-z_{D}\right|\left|z_{B}-z_{C}\right|\\&=\left|(z_{A}-z_{B})(z_{C}-z_{D})\right|+\left|(z_{A}-z_{D})(z_{B}-z_{C})\right|\\&=\left(\left|{\frac {(z_{A}-z_{B})(z_{C}-z_{D})}{(z_{A}-z_{D})(z_{B}-z_{C})}}\right|+1\right)\left|(z_{A}-z_{D})(z_{B}-z_{C})\right|\\&=\left(\left|\zeta \right|+1\right)\left|(z_{A}-z_{D})(z_{B}-z_{C})\right|\\&\geq \left|(\zeta +1)(z_{A}-z_{D})(z_{B}-z_{C})\right|\\&=\left|(z_{A}-z_{B})(z_{C}-z_{D})+(z_{A}-z_{D})(z_{B}-z_{C})\right|\\&=\left|(z_{A}-z_{C})(z_{B}-z_{D})\right|\\&=\left|z_{A}-z_{C}\right|\left|z_{B}-z_{D}\right|\\&={\overline {AC}}\cdot {\overline {BD}}\end{aligned}}}

with equality if and only if the cross-ratio {\displaystyle \zeta } is a positive real number. For the strictly convex quadrilateral in the statement, it remains to relate this positivity to cyclicity.

All argument equalities are understood modulo 2\pi. From the polar form of a complex number {\displaystyle z=\vert z\vert e^{i\arg(z)}}, it follows

{\displaystyle {\begin{aligned}\arg(\zeta )&=\arg {\frac {(z_{A}-z_{B})(z_{C}-z_{D})}{(z_{A}-z_{D})(z_{B}-z_{C})}}\\&=\arg(z_{A}-z_{B})+\arg(z_{C}-z_{D})-\arg(z_{A}-z_{D})-\arg(z_{B}-z_{C}){\pmod {2\pi }}\\&=\arg(z_{A}-z_{B})+\arg(z_{C}-z_{D})-\arg(z_{A}-z_{D})-\arg(z_{C}-z_{B})-\arg(-1){\pmod {2\pi }}\\&=-\left[\arg(z_{C}-z_{B})-\arg(z_{A}-z_{B})\right]-\left[\arg(z_{A}-z_{D})-\arg(z_{C}-z_{D})\right]-\arg(-1){\pmod {2\pi }}\\&=-\angle ABC-\angle CDA-\pi {\pmod {2\pi }}\\&=0\end{aligned}}}

with the last equality holding if and only if ABCD is cyclic, since a quadrilateral is cyclic if and only if opposite angles sum to {\displaystyle \pi }.

Q.E.D.

Note that this proof is equivalently made by observing that the cyclicity of ABCD, i.e. the supplementarity {\displaystyle \angle ABC} and {\displaystyle \angle CDA}, is equivalent to the condition

{\displaystyle \arg \left[(z_{A}-z_{B})(z_{C}-z_{D})\right]=\arg \left[(z_{A}-z_{D})(z_{B}-z_{C})\right]=\arg \left[(z_{A}-z_{C})(z_{B}-z_{D})\right]{\pmod {2\pi }}};

in particular there is a rotation of {\displaystyle \mathbb {C} } in which this {\displaystyle \arg } is 0 (i.e. all three products are positive real numbers), and by which Ptolemy's theorem

{\displaystyle {\overline {AB}}\cdot {\overline {CD}}+{\overline {AD}}\cdot {\overline {BC}}={\overline {AC}}\cdot {\overline {BD}}}

is then directly established from the simple algebraic identity

{\displaystyle (z_{A}-z_{B})(z_{C}-z_{D})+(z_{A}-z_{D})(z_{B}-z_{C})=(z_{A}-z_{C})(z_{B}-z_{D}).}
Level

Level justification
