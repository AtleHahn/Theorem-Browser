Statement

Every logically valid first-order formula in classical logic with nonempty domains is provable in a standard first-order proof calculus (with the equality axioms when equality is present).

Proof 1: Quantifier-degree reduction and coherent truth assignments — outline

Work in classical first-order logic over nonempty domains, with a standard sound proof calculus, such as Hilbert–Ackermann. A formula is refutable if its negation is provable. Satisfiability of an open formula allows an assignment to its free variables. Use the standard syntactic quantifier rules, soundness, prenex normal form and uniform capture-avoiding predicate substitution. For the degree argument the language is relational, with no functions, constants or distinguished equality. Let R be the class of prenex sentences beginning with a nonempty universal block and ending with a nonempty existential block. The degree is the number of universal blocks after consecutive like quantifiers are grouped.

Preliminary reductions

It is enough to prove that every formula is refutable or satisfiable: if a formula is valid, its negation is unsatisfiable, hence refutable; double-negation elimination proves the formula.

We approach the proof of the refutability-or-satisfiability assertion by successively restricting the class of all formulas φ for which we need to prove "φ is either refutable or satisfiable". At the beginning we need to prove this for all possible formulas φ in our language. However, suppose that for every formula φ there is some formula ψ taken from a more restricted class of formulas C, such that "ψ is either refutable or satisfiable" → "φ is either refutable or satisfiable". Then, once this claim (expressed in the previous sentence) is proved, it will suffice to prove "φ is either refutable or satisfiable" only for φ's belonging to the class C. If φ is provably equivalent to ψ (i.e., (φ ≡ ψ) is provable), then it is indeed the case that "ψ is either refutable or satisfiable" → "φ is either refutable or satisfiable" (the soundness theorem is needed to show this).

There are standard techniques for rewriting an arbitrary formula into one that does not use function or constant symbols, at the cost of introducing additional quantifiers; we will therefore assume that all formulas are free of such symbols. Gödel's paper uses a version of first-order predicate calculus that has no function or constant symbols to begin with.

Next we consider a generic formula φ (which no longer uses function or constant symbols) and apply the prenex form theorem to find a formula ψ in normal form such that φ ≡ ψ (ψ being in normal form means that all the quantifiers in ψ, if there are any, are found at the very beginning of ψ). It follows now that we need only prove the refutability-or-satisfiability assertion for formulas φ in normal form.

Next, we eliminate all free variables from φ by quantifying them existentially: if, say, x1...xn are free in φ, we form {\displaystyle \psi =\exists x_{1}\cdots \exists x_{n}\varphi }. If ψ is satisfiable in a structure M, then certainly so is φ and if ψ is refutable, then {\displaystyle \neg \psi =\forall x_{1}\cdots \forall x_{n}\neg \varphi } is provable, and then so is ¬φ, thus φ is refutable. We see that we can restrict φ to be a sentence, that is, a formula with no free variables.

Finally, we would like, for reasons of technical convenience, that the prefix of φ (that is, the string of quantifiers at the beginning of φ, which is in normal form) begin with a universal quantifier and end with an existential quantifier. To achieve this for a generic φ (subject to restrictions we have already proved), we take some one-place relation symbol F unused in φ, and two new variables y and z. If φ = (P)Φ, where (P) stands for the prefix of φ and Φ for the matrix (the remaining, quantifier-free part of φ) we form {\displaystyle \psi =\forall y(P)\exists z(\Phi \wedge [F(y)\vee \neg F(z)])}. Since {\displaystyle \forall y\exists z(F(y)\vee \neg F(z))} is clearly provable, it is easy to see that {\displaystyle \varphi \leftrightarrow\psi } is provable.

Reduction in quantifier degree

Assume every degree-k sentence is refutable or satisfiable.

Let φ be a formula of degree k + 1; then we can write it as

{\displaystyle \varphi =(\forall x)(\exists y)(\forall u)(\exists v)(P)\psi }

where all bound variables are chosen distinct, (P) is the remainder of the prefix of {\displaystyle \varphi } (it contains the remaining k − 1 universal blocks) and {\displaystyle \psi } is the quantifier-free matrix of {\displaystyle \varphi }. x, y, u and v denote here tuples of variables rather than single variables; e.g. {\displaystyle (\forall x)} really stands for {\displaystyle \forall x_{1}\forall x_{2}\cdots \forall x_{n}} where {\displaystyle x_{1}\ldots x_{n}} are some distinct variables.

Let now x' and y' be tuples of previously unused variables of the same length as x and y respectively, and let Q be a previously unused relation symbol that takes as many arguments as the sum of lengths of x and y; we consider the formula

{\displaystyle \Phi =(\forall x')(\exists y')Q(x',y')\wedge (\forall x)(\forall y)(Q(x,y)\rightarrow (\forall u)(\exists v)(P)\psi )}

Clearly, {\displaystyle \Phi \rightarrow \varphi } is provable.

Now since the string of quantifiers {\displaystyle (\forall u)(\exists v)(P)} does not contain variables from x or y, the following equivalence is easily provable with the help of whatever formalism we're using:

{\displaystyle (Q(x,y)\rightarrow (\forall u)(\exists v)(P)\psi )\equiv (\forall u)(\exists v)(P)(Q(x,y)\rightarrow \psi )}

And since these two formulas are equivalent, if we replace the first with the second inside Φ, we obtain the formula Φ' such that Φ≡Φ':

{\displaystyle \Phi '=(\forall x')(\exists y')Q(x',y')\wedge (\forall x)(\forall y)(\forall u)(\exists v)(P)(Q(x,y)\rightarrow \psi )}

Now Φ' has the form {\displaystyle (S)\rho \wedge (S')\rho '}, where (S) and (S') are some quantifier strings, ρ and ρ' are quantifier-free, and, furthermore, no variable of (S) occurs in ρ' and no variable of (S') occurs in ρ. Under such conditions every formula of the form {\displaystyle (T)(\rho \wedge \rho ')}, where (T) is a string of quantifiers containing all quantifiers in (S) and (S') interleaved among themselves in any fashion, but maintaining the relative order inside (S) and (S'), will be equivalent to the original formula Φ'(this is yet another basic result in first-order predicate calculus that we rely on). To wit, we form Ψ as follows:

{\displaystyle \Psi =(\forall x')(\forall x)(\forall y)(\forall u)(\exists y')(\exists v)(P)[Q(x',y')\wedge (Q(x,y)\rightarrow \psi )]}

and we have {\displaystyle \Phi '\equiv \Psi }.

Now {\displaystyle \Psi } is a formula of degree k and therefore by assumption either refutable or satisfiable. If {\displaystyle \Psi } is satisfiable in a structure M, then, considering {\displaystyle \Psi \equiv \Phi '\equiv \Phi \text{ and }\Phi \rightarrow \varphi }, we see that {\displaystyle \varphi } is satisfiable as well, after restricting the model to the original language. If {\displaystyle \Psi } is refutable, then so is {\displaystyle \Phi }, which is equivalent to it; thus {\displaystyle \neg \Phi } is provable. Rename bound variables as necessary to avoid capture. Now we can replace all occurrences of Q inside the provable formula {\displaystyle \neg \Phi } by some other formula dependent on the same variables, and we will still get a provable formula. (This is yet another basic result of first-order predicate calculus. Depending on the particular formalism adopted for the calculus, it may be seen as a simple application of a "functional substitution" rule of inference, as in Gödel's paper, or it may be proved by considering the formal proof of {\displaystyle \neg \Phi }, replacing in it all occurrences of Q by some other formula with the same free variables, and noting that all logical axioms in the formal proof remain logical axioms after the substitution, and all rules of inference still apply in the same way.)

In this particular case, we replace Q(x',y') in {\displaystyle \neg \Phi } with the formula {\displaystyle (\forall u)(\exists v)(P)\psi (x,y\mid x',y')}. Here (x,y | x',y') means that instead of ψ we are writing a different formula, in which x and y are replaced with x' and y'. Q(x,y) is simply replaced by {\displaystyle (\forall u)(\exists v)(P)\psi }.

{\displaystyle \neg \Phi } then becomes

{\displaystyle \neg ((\forall x')(\exists y')(\forall u)(\exists v)(P)\psi (x,y\mid x',y')\wedge (\forall x)(\forall y)((\forall u)(\exists v)(P)\psi \rightarrow (\forall u)(\exists v)(P)\psi ))}

and this formula is provable; since the part under negation and after the {\displaystyle \wedge } sign is obviously provable, and the part under negation and before the {\displaystyle \wedge } sign is obviously φ, just with x and y replaced by x' and y', we see that {\displaystyle \neg \varphi } is provable, and φ is refutable. We have proved that φ is either satisfiable or refutable, and this concludes the degree reduction.

Degree-one construction

Let A=(\forall x_1\cdots x_k)(\exists y_1\cdots y_m)\theta(x_1,\ldots,x_k,y_1,\ldots,y_m), with k,m\ge1 and quantifier-free \theta in a relational language without equality, functions or constants. Enumerate tuples of positive integers by increasing sum, then lexicographically within each sum, writing the nth tuple as (a_1^n,\ldots,a_k^n). For fresh variables z_1,z_2,\ldots, put

B_n=\theta(z_{a_1^n},\ldots,z_{a_k^n},z_{(n-1)m+2},\ldots,z_{nm+1}),\quad D_n=(\exists z_1\cdots z_{nm+1})\bigwedge_{j=1}^n B_j.

By induction on n. For n\ge2, the ordering of the tuples is such that

a_i^n<(n-1)m+2\qquad(1\le i\le k).

Consequently, the universal arguments of B_n use only the previously available variables, and variable substitution in A gives

\begin{aligned} D_{n-1}\wedge A &\ \longrightarrow\ D_{n-1}\wedge (\forall z_1\cdots z_{(n-1)m+1}) (\exists z_{(n-1)m+2}\cdots z_{nm+1})B_n\\ &\ \longrightarrow\ D_n. \end{aligned}

The last implication chooses the new existential witnesses after the witnesses in D_{n-1} have been chosen.

For the base case,

\begin{aligned} D_1 &=(\exists z_1\cdots z_{m+1}) \theta(z_{a_1^1},\ldots,z_{a_k^1},z_2,\ldots,z_{m+1})\\ &=(\exists z_1\cdots z_{m+1}) \theta(z_1,\ldots,z_1,z_2,\ldots,z_{m+1}) \end{aligned}

is a corollary of A as well. So A\to D_n is provable for every n.

The elementary tuple-index bound is left implicit in the source.

Now if D_n is refutable for some n, it follows that A is refutable. On the other hand, suppose that D_n is not refutable for any n. Then for each n there is some way of assigning truth values to the distinct atomic formulas E_h in B_1\wedge\cdots\wedge B_n, ordered by their first appearance, such that this conjunction will be true when each atom is evaluated in this fashion. “Distinct” means distinct predicates or distinct ordered tuples of variables; repeated occurrences of the same atom receive the same value. This follows from the completeness of the underlying propositional logic.

We will now show that there is such an assignment of truth values to E_h so that all the conjunctions B_1\wedge\cdots\wedge B_n will be true. The E_h appear in the same order in every initial conjunction in which they occur. We will inductively define a general assignment to them by a sort of “majority vote”: since there are infinitely many assignments (one for each sufficiently large n) affecting E_1, either infinitely many make E_1 true, or infinitely many make it false and only finitely many make it true. In the former case, we choose E_1 to be true in general; in the latter we take it to be false in general. Then from the infinitely many n for which E_1 through E_{h-1} are assigned the same truth value as in the general assignment, we pick a general assignment to E_h in the same fashion.

This general assignment must lead to every one of the B_k being true, since if one of the B_k were false under the general assignment, B_1\wedge\cdots\wedge B_n would also be false under that assignment for every n>k. But this contradicts the fact that for the finite collection of general E_h assignments appearing in B_1\wedge\cdots\wedge B_k, there are infinitely many n where the assignment making B_1\wedge\cdots\wedge B_n true matches the general assignment.

From this general assignment we construct an interpretation of the language's predicates that makes A true. The universe of the model will be the positive integers. Each i-ary predicate \Psi should be true of (u_1,\ldots,u_i) precisely when the atom \Psi(z_{u_1},\ldots,z_{u_i}) is either true in the general assignment, or not assigned by it because it never appears in any of the B_k.

In this model, each of the formulas

(\exists y_1\cdots y_m)\theta(a_1^n,\ldots,a_k^n,y_1,\ldots,y_m)

is true by construction: the witness indices are (n-1)m+2,\ldots,nm+1. But this implies that A itself is true in the model, since the tuples a^n range over all possible k-tuples of positive integers. So A is satisfiable, and the degree-one case is proved.

To see how the construction supplies the required witnesses, we may write each B_i as \theta(x_1,\ldots,x_k,y_1,\ldots,y_m) for some z variables, calling the x variables “first arguments” and the y variables “last arguments”. Take B_1 for example. Its last arguments are z_2,z_3,\ldots,z_{m+1}, and for every possible ordered k-tuple of these variables there is some j such that they appear as first arguments in B_j. Thus for large enough n_1, all these tuples appear as first arguments in some B_j within D_{n_1}. For every B_i there is a D_{n_i} with the corresponding property.

In the constructed interpretation, the objects corresponding to z_1,z_2,\ldots therefore have the following property: for every ordered k-tuple z_{p_1},\ldots,z_{p_k} there are z_{q_1},\ldots,z_{q_m} making

\theta(z_{p_1},\ldots,z_{p_k},z_{q_1},\ldots,z_{q_m})

satisfied. This is exactly the requirement in A.

Induction on degree and the preliminary reductions prove the equality-free case.

Adding equality

For a sentence \varphi in a relational language with equality, replace equality by a fresh binary relation Eq, obtaining \varphi'. Conjoin \varphi' with the universal closures of

Eq(x,x),\quad Eq(x,y)\to(Eq(x,z)\to Eq(y,z)),\quad Eq(x,y)\to(Eq(z,x)\to Eq(z,y)),

and, for every r-ary predicate A occurring in \varphi, the congruence condition

\bigwedge_{i=1}^r Eq(x_i,y_i)\ \to\ [A(x_1,\ldots,x_r)\leftrightarrow A(y_1,\ldots,y_r)].

Call the resulting equality-free sentence T_\varphi. If T_\varphi is refutable, substitute actual equality for Eq. Its added axioms are equality theorems, so \varphi is refutable. If T_\varphi has a model, quotient its domain by the equivalence relation interpreted by Eq. Predicate congruence makes all relations well defined on equivalence classes, and the quotient satisfies \varphi.

Applying the equality-free case to T_\varphi and the two transfers proves the case with equality as well.

Reduction in quantifier degree

Assume every degree-k sentence is refutable or satisfiable.

Let φ be a formula of degree k + 1; then we can write it as

{\displaystyle \varphi =(\forall x)(\exists y)(\forall u)(\exists v)(P)\psi }

where all bound variables are chosen distinct, (P) is the remainder of the prefix of {\displaystyle \varphi } (it contains the remaining k − 1 universal blocks) and {\displaystyle \psi } is the quantifier-free matrix of {\displaystyle \varphi }. x, y, u and v denote here tuples of variables rather than single variables; e.g. {\displaystyle (\forall x)} really stands for {\displaystyle \forall x_{1}\forall x_{2}\cdots \forall x_{n}} where {\displaystyle x_{1}\ldots x_{n}} are some distinct variables.

Clearly, {\displaystyle \Phi \rightarrow \varphi } is provable.

{\displaystyle (Q(x,y)\rightarrow (\forall u)(\exists v)(P)\psi )\equiv (\forall u)(\exists v)(P)(Q(x,y)\rightarrow \psi )}

And since these two formulas are equivalent, if we replace the first with the second inside Φ, we obtain the formula Φ' such that Φ≡Φ':

{\displaystyle \Phi '=(\forall x')(\exists y')Q(x',y')\wedge (\forall x)(\forall y)(\forall u)(\exists v)(P)(Q(x,y)\rightarrow \psi )}

To wit, we form Ψ as follows:

{\displaystyle \Psi =(\forall x')(\forall x)(\forall y)(\forall u)(\exists y')(\exists v)(P)[Q(x',y')\wedge (Q(x,y)\rightarrow \psi )]}

and we have {\displaystyle \Phi '\equiv \Psi }.

Now {\displaystyle \Psi } is a formula of degree k and therefore by assumption either refutable or satisfiable. If {\displaystyle \Psi } is satisfiable in a structure M, then, considering {\displaystyle \Psi \equiv \Phi '\equiv \Phi \text{ and }\Phi \rightarrow \varphi }, we see that {\displaystyle \varphi } is satisfiable as well, after restricting the model to the original language. (This is yet another basic result of first-order predicate calculus. Depending on the particular formalism adopted for the calculus, it may be seen as a simple application of a "functional substitution" rule of inference, as in Gödel's paper, or it may be proved by considering the formal proof of {\displaystyle \neg \Phi }, replacing in it all occurrences of Q by some other formula with the same free variables, and noting that all logical axioms in the formal proof remain logical axioms after the substitution, and all rules of inference still apply in the same way.)

In this particular case, we replace Q(x',y') in {\displaystyle \neg \Phi } with the formula {\displaystyle (\forall u)(\exists v)(P)\psi (x,y\mid x',y')}. Here (x,y | x',y') means that instead of ψ we are writing a different formula, in which x and y are replaced with x' and y'. Q(x,y) is simply replaced by {\displaystyle (\forall u)(\exists v)(P)\psi }.

{\displaystyle \neg ((\forall x')(\exists y')(\forall u)(\exists v)(P)\psi (x,y\mid x',y')\wedge (\forall x)(\forall y)((\forall u)(\exists v)(P)\psi \rightarrow (\forall u)(\exists v)(P)\psi ))}

and this formula is provable; since the part under negation and after the {\displaystyle \wedge } sign is obviously provable, and the part under negation and before the {\displaystyle \wedge } sign is obviously φ, just with x and y replaced by x' and y', we see that {\displaystyle \neg \varphi } is provable, and φ is refutable. Degree-one construction

Let A=(\forall x_1\cdots x_k)(\exists y_1\cdots y_m)\theta(x_1,\ldots,x_k,y_1,\ldots,y_m), with k,m\ge1 and quantifier-free \theta in a relational language without equality, functions or constants. Enumerate tuples of positive integers by increasing sum, then lexicographically within each sum, writing the nth tuple as (a_1^n,\ldots,a_k^n). For fresh variables z_1,z_2,\ldots, put

B_n=\theta(z_{a_1^n},\ldots,z_{a_k^n},z_{(n-1)m+2},\ldots,z_{nm+1}),\quad D_n=(\exists z_1\cdots z_{nm+1})\bigwedge_{j=1}^n B_j.

By induction on n. For n\ge2, the ordering of the tuples is such that

a_i^n<(n-1)m+2\qquad(1\le i\le k).

Consequently, the universal arguments of B_n use only the previously available variables, and variable substitution in A gives

\begin{aligned} D_{n-1}\wedge A &\ \longrightarrow\ D_{n-1}\wedge (\forall z_1\cdots z_{(n-1)m+1}) (\exists z_{(n-1)m+2}\cdots z_{nm+1})B_n\\ &\ \longrightarrow\ D_n. \end{aligned}

The last implication chooses the new existential witnesses after the witnesses in D_{n-1} have been chosen.

For the base case,

\begin{aligned} D_1 &=(\exists z_1\cdots z_{m+1}) \theta(z_{a_1^1},\ldots,z_{a_k^1},z_2,\ldots,z_{m+1})\\ &=(\exists z_1\cdots z_{m+1}) \theta(z_1,\ldots,z_1,z_2,\ldots,z_{m+1}) \end{aligned}

is a corollary of A as well. So A\to D_n is provable for every n.

The elementary tuple-index bound is left implicit in the source.

Now if D_n is refutable for some n, it follows that A is refutable. On the other hand, suppose that D_n is not refutable for any n. Then for each n there is some way of assigning truth values to the distinct atomic formulas E_h in B_1\wedge\cdots\wedge B_n, ordered by their first appearance, such that this conjunction will be true when each atom is evaluated in this fashion. “Distinct” means distinct predicates or distinct ordered tuples of variables; repeated occurrences of the same atom receive the same value. This follows from the completeness of the underlying propositional logic.

We will now show that there is such an assignment of truth values to E_h so that all the conjunctions B_1\wedge\cdots\wedge B_n will be true. The E_h appear in the same order in every initial conjunction in which they occur. We will inductively define a general assignment to them by a sort of “majority vote”: since there are infinitely many assignments (one for each sufficiently large n) affecting E_1, either infinitely many make E_1 true, or infinitely many make it false and only finitely many make it true. In the former case, we choose E_1 to be true in general; in the latter we take it to be false in general. Then from the infinitely many n for which E_1 through E_{h-1} are assigned the same truth value as in the general assignment, we pick a general assignment to E_h in the same fashion.

This general assignment must lead to every one of the B_k being true, since if one of the B_k were false under the general assignment, B_1\wedge\cdots\wedge B_n would also be false under that assignment for every n>k. But this contradicts the fact that for the finite collection of general E_h assignments appearing in B_1\wedge\cdots\wedge B_k, there are infinitely many n where the assignment making B_1\wedge\cdots\wedge B_n true matches the general assignment.

From this general assignment we construct an interpretation of the language's predicates that makes A true. The universe of the model will be the positive integers. Each i-ary predicate \Psi should be true of (u_1,\ldots,u_i) precisely when the atom \Psi(z_{u_1},\ldots,z_{u_i}) is either true in the general assignment, or not assigned by it because it never appears in any of the B_k.

In this model, each of the formulas

(\exists y_1\cdots y_m)\theta(a_1^n,\ldots,a_k^n,y_1,\ldots,y_m)

is true by construction: the witness indices are (n-1)m+2,\ldots,nm+1. But this implies that A itself is true in the model, since the tuples a^n range over all possible k-tuples of positive integers. So A is satisfiable, and the degree-one case is proved.

To see how the construction supplies the required witnesses, we may write each B_i as \theta(x_1,\ldots,x_k,y_1,\ldots,y_m) for some z variables, calling the x variables “first arguments” and the y variables “last arguments”. Take B_1 for example. Its last arguments are z_2,z_3,\ldots,z_{m+1}, and for every possible ordered k-tuple of these variables there is some j such that they appear as first arguments in B_j. Thus for large enough n_1, all these tuples appear as first arguments in some B_j within D_{n_1}. For every B_i there is a D_{n_i} with the corresponding property.

In the constructed interpretation, the objects corresponding to z_1,z_2,\ldots therefore have the following property: for every ordered k-tuple z_{p_1},\ldots,z_{p_k} there are z_{q_1},\ldots,z_{q_m} making

\theta(z_{p_1},\ldots,z_{p_k},z_{q_1},\ldots,z_{q_m})

satisfied. This is exactly the requirement in A.

Induction on degree and the preliminary reductions prove the equality-free case.

Adding equality

For a sentence \varphi in a relational language with equality, replace equality by a fresh binary relation Eq, obtaining \varphi'. Conjoin \varphi' with the universal closures of

Eq(x,x),\quad Eq(x,y)\to(Eq(x,z)\to Eq(y,z)),\quad Eq(x,y)\to(Eq(z,x)\to Eq(z,y)),

and, for every r-ary predicate A occurring in \varphi, the congruence condition

\bigwedge_{i=1}^r Eq(x_i,y_i)\ \to\ [A(x_1,\ldots,x_r)\leftrightarrow A(y_1,\ldots,y_r)].

Call the resulting equality-free sentence T_\varphi. If T_\varphi is refutable, substitute actual equality for Eq. Its added axioms are equality theorems, so \varphi is refutable. If T_\varphi has a model, quotient its domain by the equivalence relation interpreted by Eq. Predicate congruence makes all relations well defined on equivalence classes, and the quotient satisfies \varphi.

Applying the equality-free case to T_\varphi and the two transfers proves the case with equality as well.
