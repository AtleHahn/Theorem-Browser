Statement

The language of properly nested strings of left and right parentheses is not regular. A string is properly nested when each prefix has at least as many left as right parentheses and the total counts agree.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

A finite automaton reads a word using finitely many states; a grammar generates words by the explicitly listed substitutions. These are concrete finite rules that can be learned through short examples. The claims then use finite strings and formal power-series arithmetic familiar from algebra and calculus.

Proof 1: Pump the initial left parentheses

Use the pumping lemma: if a language L is regular, some pumping length p\ge1 allows every w\in L with |w|\ge p to be written w=xyz, where |y|\ge1, |xy|\le p and xy^kz\in L for every integer k\ge0.

Given {\displaystyle p}, there is a string of balanced parentheses that begins with more than {\displaystyle p} left parentheses, so that {\displaystyle y} will consist entirely of left parentheses. By repeating {\displaystyle y}, a string can be produced that does not contain the same number of left and right parentheses, and so they cannot be balanced.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

The supplied pumping lemma reduces the proof to a finite-string counting contradiction: choose p+1 opening parentheses followed by p+1 closing ones, then every allowed nonempty y contains only openings. Repeating y changes the total counts. Finite automata and regular languages are additional concepts, but a finite-state recognizer and its accepted strings are a small concrete extension of undergraduate discrete mathematics; no automata hierarchy or proof of the pumping lemma is needed.
