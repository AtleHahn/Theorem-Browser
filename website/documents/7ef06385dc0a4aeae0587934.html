Statement

An integer n>1 can be written as n=a^2+b^2 with integers a,b if and only if every prime congruent to 3 modulo 4 occurs with an even exponent in the prime factorization of n. Zero squares and equal squares are allowed.

Level
High school only
Level justification

Only elementary divisibility, primes and integer powers are needed.

Proof 1: Gaussian integer division and prime factors (external)

A link to the proof can be found here (Theorem9.10, pp.33–34; prerequisites in Sections3–6 and9).

The norm identity multiplies representations. A prime p\equiv3\pmod4 dividing a^2+b^2 divides both a and b, so division by p^2 and repetition force an even exponent. Conversely, use 2=1^2+1^2, Theorem9.6 for primes congruent to 1 modulo 4, and p^{2k}=(p^k)^2+0^2 for the remaining factors. These constructions allow both zero and equal squares.

Level justification

The statement and Gaussian-integer argument use integer factorization, complex-number arithmetic, rounding, Euclidean division and finite polynomial root counting. Gaussian divisibility is ordinary divisibility for pairs of integers with the explicitly given multiplication law; the decreasing norm makes the Euclidean algorithm terminate. The needed prime and factorization facts follow by back substitution and integer induction, without a general ring-theory prerequisite.
