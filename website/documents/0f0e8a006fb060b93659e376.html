Statement

Let A=(A_1\ A_1B) be an m\times n matrix over a field F, with the r columns of A_1 independent and B\in F^{r\times(n-r)}. Then the columns of X=\binom{-B}{I_{n-r}} form a basis of \ker A.

Level justification

Introductory ring theory and linear algebra supply principal ideals or matrix blocks and inverses. The statements are explicit ring identities or kernel-basis assertions; extending scalar entries to a field or unital ring introduces no additional theory needed for comprehension.

Proof 1: Check zero product, independence and spanning

Use the given factorization A=(A_1\ A_1B).

Let

{\displaystyle {X} ={\begin{pmatrix}-{B} \\{I} _{n-r}\end{pmatrix}},}

where {\displaystyle {I} _{n-r}} is the {\displaystyle (n-r)\times (n-r)} identity matrix. So, {\displaystyle {X} } is an {\displaystyle n\times (n-r)} matrix such that

{\displaystyle {A} {X} ={\begin{pmatrix}{A} _{1}&{A} _{1}{B} \end{pmatrix}}{\begin{pmatrix}-{B} \\{I} _{n-r}\end{pmatrix}}=-{A} _{1}{B} +{A} _{1}{B} ={0} _{m\times (n-r)}.}

Therefore, each of the {\displaystyle n-r} columns of {\displaystyle {X} } are particular solutions of {\displaystyle {Ax} ={0}_{{F}^{m}}}.

Furthermore, the {\displaystyle n-r} columns of {\displaystyle {X} } are linearly independent because {\displaystyle {Xu} ={0} _{{F}^{n}}} will imply {\displaystyle {u} ={0} _{{F}^{n-r}}} for {\displaystyle {u} \in {F}^{n-r}}:

{\displaystyle {X} {u} ={0} _{{F}^{n}}\implies {\begin{pmatrix}-{B} \\{I} _{n-r}\end{pmatrix}}{u} ={0} _{{F}^{n}}\implies {\begin{pmatrix}-{B} {u} \\{u} \end{pmatrix}}={\begin{pmatrix}{0} _{{F}^{r}}\\{0} _{{F}^{n-r}}\end{pmatrix}}\implies {u} ={0} _{{F}^{n-r}}.}

Therefore, the column vectors of {\displaystyle {X} } constitute a set of {\displaystyle n-r} linearly independent solutions for {\displaystyle {Ax} ={0} _{F ^{m}}}.

We next prove that any solution of {\displaystyle {Ax} ={0} _{{F}^{m}}} must be a linear combination of the columns of {\displaystyle {X} }.

For this, let

{\displaystyle {u} ={\begin{pmatrix}{u} _{1}\\{u} _{2}\end{pmatrix}}\in {F}^{n}}

be any vector such that {\displaystyle {Au} ={0} _{{F}^{m}}}. Since the columns of {\displaystyle {A} _{1}} are linearly independent, {\displaystyle {A} _{1}{x} ={0} _{{F}^{m}}} implies {\displaystyle {x} ={0} _{{F}^{r}}}.

Therefore,

\begin{aligned} Au&=0_{F^m}\\ \implies \begin{pmatrix}A_1&A_1B\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}u_1\\u_2\end{pmatrix} &=A_1u_1+A_1Bu_2=A_1(u_1+Bu_2)=0_{F^m}\\ \implies u_1+Bu_2&=0_{F^r}\\ \implies u_1&=-Bu_2. \end{aligned}{\displaystyle \implies {u} ={\begin{pmatrix}{u} _{1}\\{u} _{2}\end{pmatrix}}={\begin{pmatrix}-{B} \\{I} _{n-r}\end{pmatrix}}{u} _{2}={X} {u} _{2}.}

This proves that any vector {\displaystyle {u} } that is a solution of {\displaystyle {Ax} ={0} } must be a linear combination of the {\displaystyle n-r} special solutions given by the columns of {\displaystyle {X} }. And we have already seen that the columns of {\displaystyle {X} } are linearly independent. Hence, the columns of {\displaystyle {X} } constitute a basis for the null space of {\displaystyle {A} }.
