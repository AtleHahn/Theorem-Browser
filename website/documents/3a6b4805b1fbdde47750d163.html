Major

Establishes limits to formal axiomatization: no consistent, effectively axiomatized theory strong enough for arithmetic decides every sentence, and under the stated derivability conditions it cannot prove its own standard consistency statement. This challenges the prospect of a single complete effective foundation for mathematics, without establishing that human reasoning transcends computation.

Statement

Let T be a consistent, effectively axiomatized classical first-order theory that contains, or interprets, Robinson arithmetic Q. Effectively axiomatized means that an algorithm can enumerate all the theorems of T; consistency means that no sentence and its negation are both provable. Write T\vdash A for formal provability in T.

  1. First incompleteness theorem (with Rosser’s consistency improvement). There is a sentence G in the language of T such that T\nvdash G and T\nvdash\neg G. Thus T is syntactically incomplete.

  2. Second incompleteness theorem. Let \operatorname{Prov}_T be the standard arithmetized provability predicate for T, and suppose it satisfies the following Hilbert–Bernays derivability conditions. Writing \Box A for \operatorname{Prov}_T(\ulcorner A\urcorner), where \ulcorner A\urcorner is the natural-number code of the sentence A, require, for all sentences A,B:

T\vdash A\ \Longrightarrow\ T\vdash\Box A,\qquad T\vdash\Box A\to\Box\Box A,\qquad T\vdash\Box(A\to B)\to(\Box A\to\Box B).

Then T does not prove its standard consistency sentence

\operatorname{Con}(T)=\neg\operatorname{Prov}_T(\ulcorner 0=1\urcorner).

Here the provability predicate expresses that a natural number codes a formal derivation of the sentence in question; the consistency sentence asserts that no such derivation of the indicated arithmetic contradiction exists. In a theory interpreting arithmetic, arithmetic expressions are understood through that interpretation. The additional derivability conditions in the second assertion are not implied merely by containing Q.
