Statement

Two players choose C or D. At (C,C) each receives R; at (D,D) each receives P; a player choosing D against C receives T, and the cooperator receives S. Assume T>R>P>S. A Nash equilibrium allows no player to improve its expected payoff by changing its own choice or probability distribution alone. Repeat the game a commonly known finite number N\ge1 of rounds with observable past actions and total payoff equal to the sum of the round payoffs. Backward induction prescribes D for both players at each history, and the resulting all-defect strategy pair is a Nash equilibrium.

Level
High school only
Level justification

The four payoff values, their ordering, finite observed histories, summed payoff and unilateral-improvement definition of Nash equilibrium are supplied. A strategy selects a choice after each finite history; this is elementary finite functions and payoff comparison, including weighted averages for random choices.

Proof 1: Backward induction from the last round (outline)

The proof is inductive: one might as well defect on the last turn, since the opponent will not have a chance to later retaliate. Therefore, both will defect on the last turn. Thus, the player might as well defect on the second-to-last turn, since the opponent will defect on the last no matter what is done, and so on.

In the resulting strategy pair each opponent always defects, so changing any of one's choices to C gives S instead of P on that round and cannot improve any later payoff. Thus no unilateral strategy change improves the total payoff.
