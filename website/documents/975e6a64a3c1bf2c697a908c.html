Statement

A ring R satisfies (a+b)(a-b)=a^2-b^2 for all a,b\in R if and only if it is commutative.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Linear algebra and introductory abstract algebra supply determinants, matrices, field roots, ring operations and divisibility. The displayed assertion uses these directly; no graduate concepts are needed to understand it.

Proof 1: Expand and cancel in both directions

The proof of the factorization identity is straightforward. Starting from the product (a+b)(a-b), apply the distributive law to get

{\displaystyle (a+b)(a-b)=a^{2}+ba-ab-b^{2}.}

By the commutative law, the middle two terms cancel:

{\displaystyle ba-ab=0}

leaving

{\displaystyle (a+b)(a-b)=a^{2}-b^{2}.}

The proof holds not only for numbers, but for elements of any commutative ring. Conversely, if this identity holds in a ring R for all pairs of elements a and b, then R is commutative. To see this, apply the distributive law to the product (a+b)(a-b) and get

{\displaystyle a^{2}+ba-ab-b^{2}.}

For this to be equal to ⁠{\displaystyle \textstyle a^{2}-b^{2}}⁠, we must have

{\displaystyle ba-ab=0}

for all pairs a, b, so R is commutative.
