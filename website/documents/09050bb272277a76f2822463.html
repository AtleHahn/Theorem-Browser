Statement

For a,b\in\mathbb R^n, n\ge2, with the Euclidean norm on \Lambda^2\mathbb R^n, \|a\wedge b\|^2=\|a\|^2\|b\|^2-(a\cdot b)^2.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

From finite-dimensional linear algebra, add the concrete exterior-square space with orthonormal coordinates indexed by pairs i<j. The wedge has coefficients a_i b_j-a_j b_i and its norm is the ordinary Euclidean coordinate norm. This small extension makes the entire all-dimensions statement understandable within about 30 minutes, without manifold or differential-form theory.

Proof 1: Set the two bivectors equal

The inner product on the exterior algebra of a vector space is defined on wedge-decomposable elements as the Gram determinant of their components. For a,b,c,d\in\mathbb R^n, this is

(a\wedge b)\cdot(c\wedge d)= \det\begin{pmatrix}a\cdot c&a\cdot d\\b\cdot c&b\cdot d\end{pmatrix}.

It may also be written as a formula giving the dot product of two wedge products, as

(a\wedge b)\cdot(c\wedge d)=(a\cdot c)(b\cdot d)-(a\cdot d)(b\cdot c).

In the special case a=c and b=d, the formula yields

\|a\wedge b\|^2=\|a\|^2\|b\|^2-|a\cdot b|^2.
Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

The second exterior power can be represented by coordinates on the basis e_i wedge e_j, with a wedge b having coefficients a_i b_j-a_j b_i. Learning this degree-two construction is a small linear-algebra extension. Set c=a,d=b in the explicitly supplied inner-product determinant and expand. No manifold or differential-form theory is involved.
