Statement

Let X_1,\ldots,X_n be independent normal random variables with common mean \mu and variance q>0, where n\ge1 is an integer. With known \mu, set T=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^n(X_i-\mu)^2. Then E(T)=q and \operatorname{Var}(T)=2q^2/n.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

The probability model is the explicit normal distribution, whose density is a Gaussian calculus function. Independence means a product density, and mean/variance are its weighted integrals. The displayed sample statistics and probability limits require only these closely related elementary probability definitions, without general stochastic-process theory.

Proof 1: Independence and the fourth normal moment

The statistic

T=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^n(X_i-\mu)^2}{n}

is unbiased for q, as E(T)=q. Its variance is

\begin{aligned} \operatorname{Var}(T) &=\operatorname{Var}\left(\frac{\sum_{i=1}^n(X_i-\mu)^2}{n}\right)\\ &=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^n\operatorname{Var}((X_i-\mu)^2)}{n^2}\\ &=\frac{n\operatorname{Var}((X_1-\mu)^2)}{n^2}\\ &=\frac1n\left[E\{(X_1-\mu)^4\} -\left(E\{(X_1-\mu)^2\}\right)^2\right]. \end{aligned}

The second equality follows directly from the definition of variance and the fact that the X_i are independent. The first term in brackets is the fourth moment about the mean and has value 3q^2; the second is the square of the variance, or q^2. Thus

\operatorname{Var}(T)=\frac{2q^2}{n}.
