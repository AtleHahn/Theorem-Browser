Statement

Start with four mutually tangent circles, with distinct points of tangency, having integer signed curvatures. Repeatedly replace one circle by the alternative circle tangent to the other three. Every circle obtained after any finite number of such replacements has integer signed curvature; hence every circle in the resulting Apollonian gasket does. A straight line, if one occurs in the configuration or replacement process, is assigned curvature zero.

Level
High school only
Level justification

The gasket is defined here by a finite-stage replacement rule; the conclusion is integer preservation at every stage.

Proof 1: Integer-root preservation at every replacement

For four mutually tangent generalized circles with distinct tangencies, signed curvatures satisfy (k_1+k_2+k_3+k_4)^2=2(k_1^2+k_2^2+k_3^2+k_4^2); a line has curvature zero, an enclosing circle has curvature -1/r, and the other circles have curvature 1/r. For three fixed curvatures, the two possible fourth curvatures are

k_4=k_1+k_2+k_3\pm2\sqrt{k_1k_2+k_2k_3+k_3k_1}.

When four tangent circles described by the displayed quadratic solutions all have integer curvatures, the alternative fourth circle described by the second solution to the equation must also have an integer curvature. This is because both solutions differ from an integer by the square root of an integer, and so either solution can only be an integer if this square root, and hence the other solution, is also an integer.

Starting with any four mutually tangent circles, and repeatedly replacing one of the four with its alternative solution (Vieta jumping), in all possible ways, leads to a system of infinitely many tangent circles called an Apollonian gasket. When the initial four circles have integer curvatures, so does each replacement, and therefore all of the circles in the gasket have integer curvatures.

Level
High school only
Level justification

Signed curvature is explicitly just a reciprocal radius, with a sign convention and zero for a line. For fixed integer k_1,k_2,k_3 the two displayed quadratic roots sum to 2(k_1+k_2+k_3), so if one is an integer the other is too. Induction over finitely many replacements proves the result. These are elementary circle geometry, quadratic-root arithmetic and finite induction; no differential geometry or theory of fractals is required.
