Statement

For every a>3 and every bounded-radius family \mathcal F of positive-radius balls in a metric space, there is a disjoint subfamily \mathcal G such that every B\in\mathcal F meets a C\in\mathcal G with B\subseteq aC. If the space is separable, \mathcal G is countable. Here cB denotes the ball with the same center as B and radius multiplied by c.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Metric balls, diameter, disjoint families and separability are basic topology. A Vitali cover is explicitly arbitrarily small balls at every point; nullity means box covers of arbitrarily small total volume. These limited geometric definitions suffice without general integration.

Proof 1: General-scale selection outline

The empty family is immediate. Otherwise let R be the finite supremum of the radii. First suppose the metric space is separable.

Consider the partition of F into subcollections Fn, n ≥ 0, defined by

{\displaystyle \mathbf {F} _{n}=\{B\in \mathbf {F} :2^{-n-1}R<{\text{rad}}(B)\leq 2^{-n}R\}.}

That is, {\textstyle \mathbf {F} _{n}} consists of the balls B whose radius is in (2−n−1R, 2−nR]. A sequence Gn, with Gn ⊂ Fn, is defined inductively as follows. First, set H0 = F0 and let G0 be a maximal disjoint subcollection of H0 (such a subcollection exists by Zorn's lemma). Assuming that G0,...,Gn have been selected, let

{\displaystyle \mathbf {H} _{n+1}=\{B\in \mathbf {F} _{n+1}:\ B\cap C=\emptyset ,\ \ \forall C\in \mathbf {G} _{0}\cup \mathbf {G} _{1}\cup \dots \cup \mathbf {G} _{n}\},}

and let Gn+1 be a maximal disjoint subcollection of Hn+1. The subcollection

{\displaystyle \mathbf {G} :=\bigcup _{n=0}^{\infty }\mathbf {G} _{n}}

of F satisfies the requirements of the theorem: G is a disjoint collection, and is thus countable since the given metric space is separable. Moreover, every ball B ∈ F intersects a ball C ∈ G such that B ⊂ 5 C. Indeed, if we are given some {\displaystyle B\in \mathbf {F} }, there must be some n be such that B belongs to Fn. Either B does not belong to Hn, which implies n > 0 and means that B intersects a ball from the union of G0, ..., Gn−1, or B ∈ Hn and by maximality of Gn, B intersects a ball in Gn. In any case, B intersects a ball C that belongs to the union of G0, ..., Gn. Such a ball C must have a radius larger than 2−n−1R. Since the radius of B is less than or equal to 2−nR, we can conclude by the triangle inequality that B ⊂ 5 C, as claimed. From this {\displaystyle \bigcup _{B\in \mathbf {F} }B\subseteq \bigcup _{C\in \mathbf {G} }5\,C} immediately follows.

For any constant a>3, put c=(a-1)/2>1. If the scale c^{-n} is used instead of 2^{-n} for defining \mathbf F_n, the final value is 1+2c=a instead of 5. Thus any constant larger than 3 gives a correct statement of the lemma. In a non-separable space, the same argument shows a pairwise disjoint subfamily exists, but that family need not be countable.
