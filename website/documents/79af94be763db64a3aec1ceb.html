Statement

Let F be a real C^1 function near (x_0,y_0,z_0) with F(x_0,y_0,z_0)=0 and all three partial derivatives F_x,F_y,F_z nonzero there. Locally on F=0, each coordinate is a differentiable function of the other two. At the given point, \left(\frac{\partial x}{\partial y}\right)_z\left(\frac{\partial y}{\partial z}\right)_x\left(\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}\right)_y=-1, where the subscript specifies the held-fixed coordinate. Equivalently, \left(\frac{\partial x}{\partial y}\right)_z=-\left(\frac{\partial z}{\partial y}\right)_x/\left(\frac{\partial z}{\partial x}\right)_y.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Understanding the statement involves real C1 functions; partial derivatives with a held-fixed variable; implicit coordinate functions; nonzero derivatives. These prerequisites fit undergraduate coursework or a brief extension of it. A bright US college student should be able to understand this statement after approximately 30 minutes of focused background research; this assesses understanding the claim, not its proof.

Proof 1: Differentiate along a constant-coordinate curve

Under the hypotheses in the statement, the implicit function theorem supplies local coordinate functions on F(x,y,z)=0. Write z as a function of x and y. Thus the total differential dz is

{\displaystyle dz=\left({\frac {\partial z}{\partial x}}\right)_{y}dx+\left({\frac {\partial z}{\partial y}}\right)_{x}dy}

Suppose that we move along a curve with dz = 0, where the curve is parameterized by x. Thus y can be written in terms of x, so on this curve

{\displaystyle dy=\left({\frac {\partial y}{\partial x}}\right)_{z}dx}

Therefore, the equation for dz = 0 becomes

{\displaystyle 0=\left({\frac {\partial z}{\partial x}}\right)_{y}\,dx+\left({\frac {\partial z}{\partial y}}\right)_{x}\left({\frac {\partial y}{\partial x}}\right)_{z}\,dx}

Since this must be true for all dx, rearranging terms gives

{\displaystyle \left({\frac {\partial z}{\partial x}}\right)_{y}=-\left({\frac {\partial z}{\partial y}}\right)_{x}\left({\frac {\partial y}{\partial x}}\right)_{z}}

Dividing by the derivatives on the right hand side gives the triple product rule

{\displaystyle \left({\frac {\partial x}{\partial y}}\right)_{z}\left({\frac {\partial y}{\partial z}}\right)_{x}\left({\frac {\partial z}{\partial x}}\right)_{y}=-1}

The reciprocal derivatives exist and are nonzero by the hypotheses. Rearranging the same relation gives (\partial x/\partial y)_z=-(\partial z/\partial y)_x/(\partial z/\partial x)_y.

Proof 2: Substitute the total differentials

Under the hypotheses in the statement, use (x,z) as local independent coordinates on F(x,y,z)=0. Write the total differentials of the variables

{\displaystyle dx=\left({\frac {\partial x}{\partial y}}\right)_{z}dy+\left({\frac {\partial x}{\partial z}}\right)_{y}dz}{\displaystyle dy=\left({\frac {\partial y}{\partial x}}\right)_{z}dx+\left({\frac {\partial y}{\partial z}}\right)_{x}dz}

Substitute dy into dx

{\displaystyle dx=\left({\frac {\partial x}{\partial y}}\right)_{z}\left[\left({\frac {\partial y}{\partial x}}\right)_{z}dx+\left({\frac {\partial y}{\partial z}}\right)_{x}dz\right]+\left({\frac {\partial x}{\partial z}}\right)_{y}dz}

By using the chain rule one can show the coefficient of dx on the right hand side is equal to one, thus, because dx,dz are independent coordinate differentials, the coefficient of dz must be zero

{\displaystyle \left({\frac {\partial x}{\partial y}}\right)_{z}\left({\frac {\partial y}{\partial z}}\right)_{x}+\left({\frac {\partial x}{\partial z}}\right)_{y}=0}

Subtracting the second term and multiplying by its inverse gives the triple product rule

{\displaystyle \left({\frac {\partial x}{\partial y}}\right)_{z}\left({\frac {\partial y}{\partial z}}\right)_{x}\left({\frac {\partial z}{\partial x}}\right)_{y}=-1.}
