Statement

Work over Zermelo–Fraenkel set theory (ZF). Let AC denote the axiom of choice, BPI the Boolean prime ideal theorem (every proper ideal of a Boolean algebra is contained in a prime ideal), and KM the assertion that every nonempty compact convex subset of a real or complex Hausdorff locally convex topological vector space has an extreme point. Let (*) be the assertion that for every real normed space E, the closed unit ball \{f\in E^*: \|f\|\le1\} of its continuous dual has an extreme point. Then \mathrm{AC}\iff(*).

Level
Beyond undergraduate level
Level justification

Understanding the full hypotheses involves ZF set theory; axiom of choice; continuous duals of real normed spaces; extreme points of dual unit balls; Boolean prime ideal theorem; Krein–Milman existence. These require substantial specialist preparation beyond a brief introduction from undergraduate mathematics. Under the statement-understanding criterion, a bright US college student would need more than approximately 30 minutes of focused background research to understand the full claim.

Proof 1: Compose the quoted dual-ball implications

Under the Zermelo–Fraenkel set theory (ZF) axiomatic framework, the axiom of choice (AC) suffices to prove the Krein–Milman statement KM. The axiom of choice also implies the Boolean prime ideal theorem (BPI).

Use the two implications quoted from Bell and Fremlin's A geometric form of the axiom of choice:

\mathrm{BPI}\ \&\ \mathrm{KM}\implies(*)\qquad\text{(Theorem 1.2)},\qquad (*)\implies\mathrm{AC}\qquad\text{(Theorem 2.1)}.

It follows that under ZF, the axiom of choice is equivalent to the following statement:

The closed unit ball of the continuous dual space of any real normed space has an extreme point.

Level
Beyond undergraduate level
Level justification

The final inference is propositional logic, but understanding its assertions requires choice principles over ZF, Boolean prime ideals, continuous duals and compact convex sets in locally convex topological vector spaces. This combination is substantially beyond a short extension of ordinary undergraduate algebra and analysis. Treating the assertions as uninterpreted letters would not assess understanding of this mathematical proof.
