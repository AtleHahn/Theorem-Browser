Statement

A family \mathcal F of subsets of a finite set U shatters T\subseteq U when \{S\cap T:S\in\mathcal F\} is the family of all subsets of T. If |U|=n, an integer 0\leq d\leq n, and no set of size d+1 is shattered, then |\mathcal F|\leq\sum_{i=0}^{d}\binom ni.

Level
High school only
Level justification

Shattering is explicitly equality of finite trace families with all subsets. The bound is a finite binomial sum.

Proof 1: Inductively count shattered sets, then bound their size

Shattered-set bound

Every finite set family \mathcal F shatters at least |\mathcal F| sets. This is immediate for the empty family; for nonempty families we use mathematical induction.

Base

Every family of only one set shatters the empty set.

Step

Assume the lemma is true for all families of size less than {\displaystyle |{\mathcal {F}}|} and let {\displaystyle {\mathcal {F}}} be a family of two or more sets. Because {\displaystyle {\mathcal {F}}} has at least two distinct sets, there exists an element {\displaystyle x} that belongs to some but not all of the sets. Split {\displaystyle {\mathcal {F}}} into two subfamilies, of the sets that contain {\displaystyle x} and the sets that do not contain {\displaystyle x}. By the induction assumption, these two subfamilies shatter two collections of sets whose sizes add to at least {\displaystyle |{\mathcal {F}}|}. None of these shattered sets contain {\displaystyle x}, since a set that contains {\displaystyle x} cannot be shattered by a family in which all sets contain {\displaystyle x} or all sets do not contain {\displaystyle x}. Some of the shattered sets may be shattered by both subfamilies. When a set {\displaystyle S} is shattered by only one of the two subfamilies, it contributes one unit both to the number of shattered sets of the subfamily and to the number of shattered sets of {\displaystyle {\mathcal {F}}}. When a set {\displaystyle S} is shattered by both subfamilies, both {\displaystyle S} and {\displaystyle S\cup \{x\}} are shattered by {\displaystyle {\mathcal {F}}}, so {\displaystyle S} contributes two units to the number of shattered sets of the subfamilies and of {\displaystyle {\mathcal {F}}}. Therefore, the number of shattered sets of {\displaystyle {\mathcal {F}}} is at least equal to the number shattered by the two subfamilies of {\displaystyle {\mathcal {F}}}, which is at least {\displaystyle |{\mathcal {F}}|}.

Count the small subsets

This immediately implies the Sauer–Shelah lemma, because only \sum_{i=0}^{d}\binom ni of the subsets of an n-item universe have cardinality at most d. Thus, if |\mathcal F|>\sum_{i=0}^{d}\binom ni, there are not enough small sets to be shattered, so one of the shattered sets must have cardinality at least d+1. Every subset of a shattered set is shattered, contradicting the hypothesis.
