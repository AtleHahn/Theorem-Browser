Likely alternative prerequisite level: Undergraduate level; lowest recorded proof level: Beyond undergraduate level. The pointwise limits satisfy |b_k|<=M_k. Given epsilon, choose K with sum_(k>K) M_k small. For n>=K compare the first K terms by their finite pointwise limits and bound both remaining tails by the majorant tail. This proves convergence to the absolutely convergent sum, with the triangular indexing handled explicitly. Ordinary undergraduate epsilon-tail analysis replaces Lebesgue integration.

Statement

Let a_k(n) be real numbers for integers 0\le k\le n, with a_k(n)\to b_k for each fixed k as n\to\infty. Suppose |a_k(n)|\le M_k and \sum_{k=0}^\infty M_k<\infty. Then \lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{k=0}^n a_k(n)=\sum_{k=0}^\infty b_k.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Understanding the statement involves real sequences; infinite series; limits. These prerequisites fit undergraduate coursework or a brief extension of it. A bright US college student should be able to understand this statement after approximately 30 minutes of focused background research; this assesses understanding the claim, not its proof.

Proof 1: Apply dominated convergence to the summable sequences — outline

View the finite sums as sums on \mathbb N_0, extending a_k(n) by zero for k>n. Counting measure turns these into integrals, dominated by the summable sequence (M_k). The coordinatewise limits are b_k. Lebesgue’s dominated convergence theorem therefore interchanges the limit and sum and gives the formula.

Level
Beyond undergraduate level
Level justification

The written proof recasts sequences as counting-measure integrals and invokes Lebesgue dominated convergence. Although a direct series proof is elementary analysis, this proof uses measure-theoretic integration.
