Statement

Let F be a field and a,b\in F[x] not both zero. Repeated polynomial division, stopping whenever the divisor is zero, terminates. Its last nonzero polynomial is a greatest common divisor of a,b, unique up to multiplication by a nonzero scalar. Here a gcd divides both polynomials and is divisible by every common polynomial divisor.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Introductory polynomial and ring theory supplies degrees, divisibility, ideals and finite-field arithmetic. The radical consists of elements with a power in the ideal, and Krull dimension counts lengths of prime-ideal chains. These limited ideal-based definitions suffice without schemes, localization or field-extension arithmetic.

Proof 1: Descend in degree and preserve common divisors — outline

The basic procedure is similar to that for integers. At each step k, a quotient polynomial qk(x) and a remainder polynomial rk(x) are identified to satisfy the recursive equation

{\displaystyle r_{k-2}(x)=q_{k}(x)r_{k-1}(x)+r_{k}(x),}

where r−2(x) = a(x) and r−1(x) = b(x). Each quotient polynomial is chosen such that each remainder is either zero or has a degree that is smaller than the degree of its predecessor: deg[rk(x)] < deg[rk−1(x)]. Since the degree is a nonnegative integer, and since it decreases with every step, the Euclidean algorithm concludes in a finite number of steps. The last nonzero remainder is the greatest common divisor of the original two polynomials, a(x) and b(x).

Here the common-divisor invariant is used as for integers: the common divisors of two successive polynomials are unchanged by a division-with-remainder step. If the divisor is zero initially, the process stops immediately.
