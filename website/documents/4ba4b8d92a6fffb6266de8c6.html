Statement

A C^1 function f:\mathbb R^3\to\mathbb R satisfies f_x+f_y=0 and f_y+f_z=0 if and only if there is a C^1 function C:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R such that f(x,y,z)=C(x-y+z).

Level justification

Understanding the statement involves real multivariable differentiation; linear algebra. These prerequisites fit undergraduate coursework or a brief extension of it. Proof 1: Integral planes and their scalar labels (outline)

Even though the system is overdetermined there are infinitely many solutions. Nevertheless, these solutions still have enough structure that they may be completely described. The integral surfaces for this system are the planes in \mathbb R^3 of the form x-y+z=t, for t a constant. A solution is constant on each such plane.

Once these integral surfaces are known, all solutions can then be given in terms of an arbitrary function. Define a function C(t) by:

f(x,y,z)=C(t)\quad\text{whenever }x-y+z=t.

Here C(t)=f(t,0,0) is C^1. Conversely, if a C^1 function C(t) is given, then each function f given by this expression is a solution of the original equations. Thus, because of the existence of this family of integral surfaces, solutions of the original equations are in a one-to-one correspondence with C^1 functions of one variable.

The source leaves the constancy-on-planes and converse differentiation checks implicit. Individual level sets of a solution may contain more than one of these planes.

Level justification

The two vanishing directional derivatives give constancy along the planes spanned by their directions; restriction to (t,0,0) defines C and the chain rule verifies the converse. Undergraduate multivariable calculus and linear algebra suffice.
