Statement

For every positive integer n, x\mapsto x^n is differentiable on \mathbb R with derivative nx^{n-1}. The constant polynomial x\mapsto1 has derivative zero.

Proof 1: Binomial expansion of the difference quotient

The constant polynomial has derivative

\frac{d}{dx}(1)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{1-1}{h}=\lim_{h\to0}\frac0h=0.

Let y=x^n, where n\ge1 is an integer. For n=1, the difference quotient is 1. For n\ge2,

\begin{aligned} \frac{dy}{dx} &=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{(x+h)^n-x^n}{h}\\[4pt] &=\lim_{h\to0}\frac1h\left[x^n+\binom n1x^{n-1}h+\binom n2x^{n-2}h^2+\dots+\binom nnh^n-x^n\right]\\[4pt] &=\lim_{h\to0}\left[\binom n1x^{n-1}+\binom n2x^{n-2}h+\dots+\binom nnh^{n-1}\right]\\[4pt] &=nx^{n-1}. \end{aligned}

Since n choose 1 is equal to n, and the rest of the terms all contain a positive power of h, those terms tend to zero as h\to0.

Proof 2: Induction using the product rule

Let n be a nonnegative integer. For n\ge1, it is required to prove that \frac{d}{dx}x^n=nx^{n-1}; the constant case is treated separately.

When n=0,

\frac{d}{dx}(1)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{1-1}{h}=\lim_{h\to0}\frac0h=0.

When n=1,

\frac{d}{dx}x=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{(x+h)-x}{h}=\lim_{h\to0}\frac hh=1.

Therefore, the base case holds either way.

Suppose the statement holds for some positive integer k, i.e. \frac{d}{dx}x^k=kx^{k-1}.

When n=k+1, the product rule gives

\begin{aligned} \frac{d}{dx}x^{k+1} &=\frac{d}{dx}(x^k\cdot x)\\ &=x^k\cdot\frac{d}{dx}x+x\cdot\frac{d}{dx}x^k\\ &=x^k+x\cdot kx^{k-1}\\ &=x^k+kx^k=(k+1)x^k=(k+1)x^{(k+1)-1}. \end{aligned}

By the principle of mathematical induction, the statement is true for all positive integers n, together with the separate constant case.

Proof 3: Factor a difference of powers

Let y=x^n, where n\ge1 is an integer, and let a,b\in\mathbb R. By the geometric sum formula, for b\ne a,

\frac{b^n-a^n}{b-a}=\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}a^{n-1-j}b^j.

Then,

\begin{aligned} \left.\frac{dy}{dx}\right|_{x=a} &=\lim_{b\to a}\frac{b^n-a^n}{b-a}\\[4pt] &=\lim_{b\to a}\left[a^{n-1}+a^{n-2}b+\dots+b^{n-1}\right]\\[4pt] &=na^{n-1}, \end{aligned}

where for n=1 the bracket is just 1. The constant case has zero difference quotient.

Level justification

Factor the difference of powers into a finite sum and evaluate its limit by continuity. First undergraduate calculus suffices.
