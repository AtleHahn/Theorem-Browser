Statement

Every group of order 30 has a nontrivial proper normal subgroup.

Level
Within undergraduate level
Level justification

Finite groups, normal subgroups and simplicity are introductory algebra. Perfect means equal to the subgroup generated by commutators, one short group-theoretic definition. No classification or character theory is needed to understand these claims.

Proof 1: Counting elements of orders three and five

Let n_p denote the number of Sylow p-subgroups of G. If |G|=30 and G were simple, then n_3>1 to avoid a normal 3-subgroup, and by Sylow's counting theorem n_3 must both divide 10=2\cdot5 and equal 1 modulo 3, hence n_3=10. The group then has 10 distinct cyclic subgroups of order 3, each with 2 elements of order 3. This means G has at least 20 distinct elements of order 3.

Similarly, n_5=6, since n_5>1 must divide 6=2\cdot3, and n_5 must equal 1 modulo 5. Thus G also has 24 distinct elements of order 5. Here distinct subgroups of the same prime order intersect only at the identity, and elements of order 3 and 5 are different. But the order of G is only 30, so a simple group of order 30 cannot exist.

Level justification

Use Sylow counting to force ten order-three and six order-five subgroups, then count their disjoint nonidentity elements. Standard undergraduate group theory suffices.
