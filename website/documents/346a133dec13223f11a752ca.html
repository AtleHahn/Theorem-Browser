Statement

Let S\subset\mathbb R^2 be bounded and Lebesgue measurable, with area A. There is a vector t\in\mathbb R^2 such that S+t contains at least \lceil A\rceil distinct points of \mathbb Z^2. Equivalently, S contains that many distinct points with all pairwise differences in \mathbb Z^2.

Level
Beyond undergraduate level
Level justification

The statement quantifies over arbitrary bounded Lebesgue-measurable plane sets and their area.

Proof 1: Cut into unit squares and count overlaps

Put n=\lceil A\rceil; if A=0, the assertion is immediate.

The basic idea of the proof is to cut {\displaystyle S} into pieces along the boundaries of the unit squares of the integer lattice. Assign boundaries disjointly, using the translates of [0,1)^2. Then, translate each of those pieces by an integer vector so that they all move into the same unit square. This translation may cause some points of the unit square to be covered more than once. If the combined area of the translated pieces is counted with multiplicity (counting each point by the number of times it is covered) it remains equal to {\displaystyle A}. On the other hand, if the whole unit square were covered with multiplicity at most {\displaystyle n-1} its area would be at most {\displaystyle n-1}, less than {\displaystyle A}. Therefore, some point {\displaystyle p} of the unit square must be covered with multiplicity at least {\displaystyle n}. A translation that takes {\displaystyle p} to the origin will take the {\displaystyle n} points of {\displaystyle S} that covered {\displaystyle p} to integer points, which is what was required.

Level justification

The geometric counting idea is elementary, but the proof concerns arbitrary bounded Lebesgue-measurable plane sets and compares area with multiplicity after disjoint partition and translation. Whether the required measurable-set background is a small enough extension is doubtful under the agreed baseline, so the original advanced level is retained provisionally.
